## High-Level Summary
The sitting opened with a motion to sit in private, which was negatived. It then held Second Reading debates on the Homes and Planning Bill, the Cancer (Reporting and Strategy) Bill and the Coastal Communities (Health) Bill; the first two were not supported by the Government, while the Minister responding on coastal health recognised the inequalities but did not state a position on the Bill before the debate was adjourned. Nineteen further Private Members’ Bills were objected to and deferred to later Fridays. The sitting concluded with an adjournment debate on defence research and development, during which the Minister confirmed increased investment and said that a defence finance strategy would arrive in December.

## Detailed Summary
### Opening business: motion to sit in private
Adam Thompson moved that the House sit in private. The question was put forthwith under Standing Order No. 163 and negatived: “Question put forthwith (Standing Order No. 163) and negatived.” No further outcome was stated in the transcript.

### Homes and Planning Bill — community-led housing and controls on second homes
Andrew George (St Ives) introduced the Bill and declared non-pecuniary interests as a member of the Cornwall Rural Housing Association and a volunteer board member of the Cornwall Community Land Trust. He said the Bill would provide optional tools for local authorities and communities to identify land for affordable housing and to manage the use of homes as second homes or short-term lets. He emphasised that it would offer local choices rather than impose a uniform national requirement: “The Bill does not impose from the centre but offers a menu of policy options for local authorities and communities.” Members including Steve Darling, Brian Mathew, Ben Maguire, Caroline Voaden, Sarah Gibson and Iqbal Mohamed discussed housing shortages, community-led schemes, infrastructure and the effect of second homes and short-term lets on permanent housing. Maguire argued that the issue was not simply the number of properties being built, saying: “We are not short of residential properties, which are being built every year.”

Members who opposed the Bill, including Samantha Dixon, Steve Yemm and Matthew Pennycook, argued that its provisions duplicated existing planning powers, housing programmes and legislation, and could create legal and administrative difficulties. Pennycook said that the proposed land provisions could interfere with property rights and increase compensation claims, while the proposed C5 and C6 use classes would not automatically make every change of use subject to planning permission and could conflict with established planning principles. Summarising the Government’s position, he said: “Taken together, its measures would add considerable prescription and complexity to the planning and land system.” The Government did not support the Bill. No division was recorded; the debate was adjourned and ordered to resume on Friday 15 January 2027.

### Cancer (Reporting and Strategy) Bill — annual accountability for the national cancer plan
Clive Jones (Wokingham) proposed statutory annual reporting to Parliament on cancer prevention, diagnosis and treatment, together with requirements to publish and maintain the Government’s cancer strategy. He argued that regular reporting would support parliamentary scrutiny and help ensure that commitments survived changes of Government. He quoted the 64 supporting cancer charities, which described the Bill as: “practical, proportionate and uncontroversial.” Members including Emily Darlington, Scott Arthur, Vikki Slade, Lee Pitcher and Damian Hinds discussed early diagnosis, treatment waiting times, age-specific data, rare cancers, inequalities and patients’ experiences. Hinds referred to deteriorating performance against the faster diagnosis, 31-day treatment and 62-day treatment standards, while Jones cited Cancer Research UK’s assessment that current trends might not achieve the 62-day target by March 2029.

Government Members accepted the objectives but argued that further legislation was unnecessary because the 10-year national cancer plan already included reporting arrangements. Alison McGovern, the Minister responding, said that an annual progress report would be published from the plan’s first anniversary and that the National Cancer Board had considered the Bill. She also announced that the report would be “laid before Parliament and accompanied by an oral statement from the Secretary of State.” McGovern said she expected the responsible Minister to arrange a meeting with Jones and the charities supporting the Bill. The Government did not support the Bill. No division was recorded; the debate was adjourned and ordered to resume on Friday 5 February 2027.

### Coastal Communities (Health) Bill — health inequalities and cross-government strategy
Steffan Aquarone (North Norfolk) introduced a Bill proposing a duty to act on coastal health inequalities and a cross-government coastal health strategy. Referring to the Chief Medical Officer’s 2021 report, he discussed lower life expectancy, poorer health, workforce recruitment problems and access difficulties in coastal communities. He described the Bill’s two principal measures as: “a duty to act and a strategy that brings Government together to deliver.” Other Members discussed maternity provision, transport, pharmacy closures, mental health, deprivation and recruitment. Sadik Al-Hassan supported a cross-government approach and suggested a Minister should have specific responsibility for coastal communities, while Ian Roome referred to births being redirected more than two hours inland because of a non-operational maternity unit.

Adam Thompson supported action to address deprivation and health inequalities but opposed a coastal-specific statutory framework, arguing that similar problems affected inland and rural communities and that support should be based on need rather than geography: “Support should be offered—that is absolutely correct—but on the basis of need, not just geographical location.” Alison McGovern, the Minister responding, recognised significant and persistent health inequalities in some coastal communities and said that services should reflect local circumstances. She also linked health outcomes to housing, education, employment, income, transport and the wider environment, stating: “Place and geography matter for health, but tackling inequalities between places requires action within healthcare and far beyond it.” McGovern said she would not refuse a meeting and referred Aquarone to the Minister for Public Health and Patient Safety for discussion of general health inequalities. The transcript does not record a Government position on whether the Bill was supported. The debate stood adjourned and was ordered to resume on Friday 5 February 2027.

### Business without debate — objections to Private Members’ Bills
Second Reading motions for 19 Private Members’ Bills were objected to, and each Bill was deferred to a later Friday. The Gambling Act 2005 (Mandatory Conditions of Lottery Operating Licences) Bill and the Debt Relief (Developing Countries) Bill were scheduled for 12 March 2027; the Social Security (Marriage) Bill for 15 January 2027; the Non-Domestic Rating (Retail, Hospitality and Leisure) Bill, the Multi-Storey Car Parks (Safety) Bill and the Sale and Supply of Glue Traps (Prohibition) Bill for 27 November; and the Tax Reliefs, Statutory Instruments Act 1946 (Amendment), Arm’s-Length Bodies (Accountability to Parliament), Bailiffs (Warrants of Possession), Bank of England (Inflation Targets), Public Sector Exit Payments (Limitation), BBC Licence Fee Non-Payment (Decriminalisation for Over-75s), British Broadcasting Corporation (Privatisation), Dangerous Dogs Act 1991 (Amendment), Hospitality Services (Value Added Tax Exemption), Sporting and Physical Education Services (Value Added Tax Exemption), Meat (Information About Method of Killing) and Covid-19 Vaccine Damage Payments Bills for 4 December. The record states: “Object.” No substantive debate or division was recorded.

### Defence research and development spending — adjournment debate
Chris Coghlan (Dorking and Horley) argued that defence research and development could strengthen national security while supporting civilian innovation and economic growth. He welcomed the Government’s £5 billion investment in dual-use military technology and asked whether it would be scaled through further borrowing and co-operation with allies. He described the civilian benefits of military research as follows: “The spillover effect from publicly developed military technology to private sector civilian use is one of the most powerful forces for economic growth.”

Calvin Bailey, the Minister responding, said that defence research and development was central to technological capability, industrial resilience and economic growth. He confirmed that the Ministry of Defence’s research and development budget would exceed £2 billion in 2026–27 and rise in subsequent years, while expenditure had reached approximately £3.1 billion in 2024–25. He also outlined UK Defence Innovation, defence skills funding, regional defence growth deals, university collaboration and co-operation with allies. In response to Coghlan, Bailey said that the defence finance strategy would arrive in December and support delivery of the defence investment plan and strategic defence review: “That strategy will arrive in December.” He cited the DragonFire laser programme as an example of long-term research producing military capability, skilled employment and lower-cost air defence. The motion to adjourn was agreed and the House adjourned.
