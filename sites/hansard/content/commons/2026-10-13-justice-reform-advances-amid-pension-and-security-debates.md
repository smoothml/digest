## High-Level Summary
The day covered energy security and costs, the prisoner early release scheme, threats from Iran, pensions and social care, the Falkland Islands, criminal justice reform, Northern Ireland legacy legislation and bone and joint health. Ministers outlined measures on clean energy, household and business support, prison capacity, sanctions and security, and a proposed adjustment to the state pension uprating mechanism from April 2030. The Courts and Tribunals Bill passed Third Reading by 301 votes to 193 after the Government removed its general provision for judge-only Crown Court trials, while retaining other reforms. The House also agreed to grant leave to introduce the Hares Bill and to extend proceedings on the Northern Ireland Troubles Bill to 29 April 2027 by 304 votes to 179.

## Detailed Summary
### Energy Security and Net Zero oral answers
Ministers argued that reducing bills in the long term required lower exposure to international fossil-fuel markets through renewable generation, electrification, home upgrades and grid investment. Miatta Fahnbulleh said gas set the electricity price 60% of the time, compared with 90% in 2020, and said the Government would act to “break the link between gas and electricity”. The Government also cited the removal of VAT from domestic electricity bills, a £150 reduction in energy costs, the warm homes plan, support for off-grid households and the British industrial competitiveness scheme. Opposition Members sought further short-term relief, questioned the scale of the VAT saving, criticised some renewable infrastructure and promoted a “cheap power plan”; Fahnbulleh described that plan as based on “dodgy figures”.

Further questions covered cooling measures, climate resilience, peatland protection, the cross-border electricity connection, business energy costs, Rosebank and Jackdaw, and grid connections. Ministers said the warm homes plan was intended to include passive cooling measures such as solar shading and reflective coatings, and that a Minister would meet accreditation bodies about delays to air-to-air heat-pump applications: “I will be meeting the relevant bodies soon”. Ministers would not comment on individual oil and gas consenting decisions, but said decisions must be compatible with climate obligations and that scope 3 emissions would be included in the environmental assessment. Other commitments included writing to Ofgem about regional network charges and meeting Members about grid and constituency projects. No division was recorded.

### Prisoner early release scheme
Jake Richards defended the progression model in the context of a prison system that had reached 99% capacity. He cited plans for 14,000 additional prison places by 2031, the removal of foreign national offenders, exclusions for rape and serious child-sex offences, electronic monitoring and probation supervision. The Government had “excluded rape and serious child sex offences” from eligibility for the relevant release-date changes, and victim support included a helpline backed by nearly £19 million. Opposition Members questioned how many released prisoners had been tagged, alleged breaches and reoffending, and argued that prison capacity and staffing had not been expanded sufficiently before releases began.

Richards did not provide the requested current tagging figures, saying that release data would be published in the usual way and that “the next batch of data will be released in January of next year”. Members also raised concerns about domestic-abuse victims, probation capacity, prison staffing, individual cases and unused cells. The Government said prisoners who needed tagging should be tagged “at source as soon as possible”, cited £750 million of investment in probation, described further victim-support funding, and said recruitment initiatives were being introduced for prison officers. It also referred to recall where licence conditions were breached. No division or separate decision was recorded.

### Iran: threats to British interests
Stephen Doughty said that Iran’s direct and proxy activity posed a significant threat to the United Kingdom, its allies and the rules-based international system. The Government had sanctioned two Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps officers, summoned Iran’s ambassador, signed a statement with 35 other Foreign Ministers and continued to work with the police, intelligence agencies and international partners. Doughty said the Government would “not hesitate to take further action where appropriate”. The statement referred to the RAF Fairford incident, an alleged plot targeting Jewish communities in Manchester, threats to shipping and Houthi activity; investigations into Fairford remained ongoing.

Questions covered the Foreign Secretary’s meeting with Iran’s Foreign Minister, maritime security in the straits of Hormuz and Bab al-Mandab, possible further designations, cyber and disinformation threats, the protection of British-Iranian communities, and diplomatic and military responses. Doughty said the Foreign Secretary had raised de-escalation, freedom of navigation, hostile activity in the United Kingdom and the detention of Craig and Lindsay Foreman. He declined to comment on future designations or intelligence assessments, while describing sanctions, defensive measures, diplomatic engagement, support for Gulf partners and information-defence work. No division was held.

### Pensions triple lock and national care service
Pat McFadden announced that the triple lock would remain in place for the current Parliament, followed by an adjusted mechanism from April 2030. The state pension would continue to rise by the highest of inflation, 2.5% or the amount required to maintain its record level against average earnings. The Government said this would end the ratchet effect and help fund a national care service. McFadden described the changes as “a new settlement for pensioners” and estimated that they would reduce spending by £15 billion a year by the end of the next decade, rising to about £50 billion by 2050.

The Opposition argued that the proposal amounted to abandoning the triple lock, would not provide enough money for social care and should be replaced by savings from working-age welfare. Liberal Democrat and Labour Members sought impact assessments, stronger protections for poorer pensioners and greater clarity on care funding. The Government said pensioner poverty was forecast to fall, that the Pensions Commission would report the following year, and that additional funding would flow to Scotland through Barnett consequentials. McFadden confirmed that “the state pension will continue to rise every year”. No division was recorded.

### Falkland Islands and Argentina
Uma Kumaran said that Argentina had threatened sanctions and criminal measures against individuals and companies connected with the Sea Lion project, which the Government regarded as lawful activity regulated by the Falkland Islands Government. The United Kingdom reaffirmed the islands’ British status and the islanders’ right to self-determination, engaged affected businesses, prepared to use protection of trading interests legislation, mobilised international partners and announced support for 45th-anniversary commemorations. The Government stated that Argentina’s notification under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea did not itself invalidate licences or require hydrocarbon activity to stop: “It does not constitute a judgment by an international court or tribunal”.

The Opposition welcomed support for the islanders but sought details about the Foreign Secretary’s role, business and consular protections, possible sanctions, CPTPP accession and military readiness. Kumaran said that the Ministry of Defence considered the military presence appropriate and that the Government would engage with the UNCLOS process without providing “a running commentary”. In response to questions about drilling consent, she said that drilling in the Falkland Islands was a matter for the Falkland Islands Government under applicable local legislation. No division was held.

### Hares Bill
Ruth Jones sought leave to introduce a Bill establishing a close season, from the start of February to the end of September, during which killing or taking brown hares would be prohibited. The Bill would also provide protection for mountain hares and repeal the seasonal prohibition on selling hares. Jones cited population decline, the timing of organised shoots and the absence of a statutory close season in England and Wales. She quoted the Game & Wildlife Conservation Trust’s estimate that “a February hare shoot can remove 60% of the breeding stock”.

The House agreed to the motion for leave without a recorded division: “Question put and agreed to”. Ruth Jones then presented the Bill, which was read the First time and ordered to be read a Second time on Friday 19 March 2027; it was ordered to be printed as Bill 154.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill
The Report-stage debate covered Crown Court backlogs, jury-trial rights, magistrates’ court capacity, appeal rights, legal aid, victims’ experiences and the repeal of the presumption of parental involvement in family proceedings. The Government defended investment, modernisation and structural reform, including the removal of the financial limit on Crown Court sitting days, specialist rape and serious sexual offence courts, digital systems and increased magistrates’ court powers. Sarah Sackman confirmed that the Government would remove the general judge-only trial provision in clause 3, saying that “all defendants facing a trial in the Crown court will be heard with a jury”. The Government retained judge-only trials for specified complex or lengthy cases, continued the proposed removal of the defendant’s right to elect Crown Court trial in either-way cases, increased magistrates’ sentencing powers and changed the magistrates’ appeal framework.

Opposition Members and some Government backbenchers argued that the reforms would transfer pressure to magistrates’ courts, weaken safeguards and increase the risk of unrepresented defendants where legal-aid thresholds were lower. Supporters emphasised delays experienced by victims and the proposed transfer of case triage from defendants to courts. New clause 23, requiring an independent review of whether the reforms created or exacerbated “differences in treatment or in outcomes”, was added to the Bill. A proposed expiry clause was defeated, 189 to 306, as was an amendment removing clause 1, 196 to 304. Government amendment 3 removed clause 3, and an amendment linking legal-aid funding to the likely sentence was defeated, 89 to 312. The Bill passed Third Reading by 301 votes to 193.

### Northern Ireland Troubles Bill carry-over extension
The House debated whether to extend the period for completing proceedings on the Northern Ireland Troubles Bill by 198 days. Chris Bryant said the extension was needed to allow further work on investigations, information recovery and protections for victims, families and veterans. He outlined proposed amendments concerning the distinction between lawful security-service activity and paramilitary conduct, repeat investigations, on-the-run letters, the composition of the advisory group, Attorney General consent for future prosecutions and co-operation by the Irish Government. He said that “it is so important that we get answers for people” after decades of uncertainty.

Supporters argued that families needed a functioning process for truth and accountability while also supporting safeguards for veterans. Opponents, including some Northern Ireland Members and the Liberal Democrats, said that the Bill remained flawed and that the Government’s 156 amendments had been published too late for adequate scrutiny. Some argued that the process could expose veterans to repeated investigations without delivering justice. The Government said that the Committee stage would take place the following week and that Irish legislation was intended to proceed in parallel. The carry-over motion was agreed by 304 votes to 179, extending proceedings “until 29 April 2027”.

### Bone and joint health
David Reed led an Adjournment debate on musculoskeletal conditions, drawing on his experience of a missed ankle fracture and subsequent osteoarthritis. He argued for earlier diagnosis, timely physiotherapy and rehabilitation, national leadership, assessment of the economic case for early intervention and improved support for veterans. He said that “More than 20 million people in the UK live with an MSK condition” and linked delayed care to pain, disability, absence from work and pressure on public services.

James Frith said that the Government were pursuing earlier diagnosis and community-based care through the 10-year health plan, additional DEXA scanners, the Getting It Right First Time musculoskeletal programme, physical-activity hubs and improved digital access. He reported that waits of more than 18 weeks had fallen by about 20% in participating systems and said that the Government aimed for 80% of community health-services activity to take place within 18 weeks by 2028-29. He said that “care should come to people”. The debate concluded with the question agreed to, and the House adjourned.
