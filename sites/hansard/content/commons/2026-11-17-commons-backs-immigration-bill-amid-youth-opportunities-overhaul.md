## High-Level Summary
The day began with Energy Security and Net Zero questions on household bills, the Warm Homes Plan, heating oil, renewable energy, grid infrastructure, business costs and community ownership. The House then considered the Milburn review on young people not in education, employment or training; the Government accepted an opportunity and support guarantee, more personalised local delivery and the creation of a youth opportunities unit, with a White Paper and legislation to follow. The Immigration and Asylum Bill was debated, amended and read a Third time after several proposed new clauses were rejected in divisions, including proposals on EU migration arrangements, citizenship, settlement and Skilled Worker visas. Other business included Bills presented, a ten-minute rule Bill on nursery CCTV, delegated legislation, points of order and an adjournment debate on the NHS Federated Data Platform; the House then adjourned.

## Detailed Summary
### Energy Security and Net Zero: homes, bills and heating oil
Questions covered the £1 billion reduction in capital grant funding for the Warm Homes Plan, environmental and social levies on electricity bills, and support for off-grid homes using heating oil. Martin McCluskey maintained that the Government remained committed to upgrading up to 5 million homes and said, “we still have many billions of pounds of spending for home upgrades.” Polly Billington said that 75% of the cost of the renewables obligation had been moved to the Exchequer and that “No new policy costs will be put on bills until a comprehensive cost control framework is put in place at the spending review in 2027.”

Ministers referred to the £100 household energy payment, zero-rate VAT on electricity bills extended to July 2027 and £53 million of targeted support for low-income heating-oil users. On minimum heating-oil orders, Billington said, “We will explore how minimum payments will fit into regulation.” She also said that the Government would consider a new discretionary fund to improve targeting. No specific extension of the off-grid grant uplift was announced in the transcript.

### Renewable energy, the grid and regional growth
Ministers discussed emerging renewable technologies, including floating offshore wind, Great British Energy, the National Wealth Fund, Contracts for Difference allocation round 8, offshore grid co-ordination and proposed solar and wind developments. Michael Shanks said affordability would be central to allocation round 8 but stated, “I will not be drawn on what will or will not come in AR8.” He also said that national infrastructure need did not remove the requirement to assess local impacts, including cumulative effects.

Questions also covered Berwick Bank, Grangemouth and growth in the Forth Valley. On Grangemouth, Blair McDougall identified ground remediation as a significant barrier and said the Government were working with the Scottish Government so that the £200 million allocation could be deployed, subject to investors coming forward. Michael Shanks said he would not comment on the particular Chatteris application but that the planning system would assess scale and cumulative impact; he also agreed to discuss community engagement concerning a proposed North Shropshire substation with Helen Morgan.

### Business energy costs, community ownership and vulnerable households
The Government said that the supercharger and the British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme were reducing energy costs for businesses by up to £40 per megawatt-hour. Blair McDougall also said that further electrification and energy efficiency would be needed, particularly for smaller businesses, while questions raised the costs of possible European energy arrangements and the availability of support for firms outside existing schemes. The Government’s stated longer-term approach was to expand domestic clean generation and reduce exposure to fossil-fuel prices.

Community energy, grassroots sport, fuel poverty and a possible social tariff for people with terminal illness were also discussed. Martin McCluskey said that Great British Energy had launched £30 million of funding as a down payment on a planned £1 billion investment in community energy. Polly Billington said that reforms to the energy retail market should consider how to support people with unavoidably high energy use because of medical need and welcomed better data matching, but no social tariff was announced.

### Climate participation and topical energy questions
Katie White described the youth climate and nature panel and said that, after meeting the final 25 applicants from 1,000 applications, “we agreed that we would co-create the process that we will work on over the next two years.” She said that the Government pursued elements of the “polluter pays” principle through the energy profits levy, while negotiations at the climate conference remained ongoing.

Topical questions covered offshore wind, biomethane, the Smart Export Guarantee, energy debt, solar on agricultural land, oil and gas workers, grid capacity and community benefits. Martin McCluskey said, “We are reviewing the smart energy guarantee, and we will come forward with proposals in due course.” Katie White said the Government would provide greater certainty on the future biomethane framework as soon as possible. On Rosebank, Michael Shanks declined to comment on the live process and said that the Government would set out its decision in due course.

### Milburn Review: young people and work
Pat McFadden presented Alan Milburn’s review of young people not in education, employment or training. The Government accepted the recommendations for an opportunity and support guarantee, a more personalised system delivered locally, and a youth opportunities unit reporting directly to the Prime Minister. McFadden said the Government were “today committing to the creation of a new youth opportunities unit, which will report directly to the Prime Minister.” The proposed system would provide a route into education, training, an apprenticeship, high-quality work experience or a job, alongside an expectation that eligible young people take up suitable support.

The Government also agreed to make the six-month jobs guarantee available after 12 months on Universal Credit rather than 18, and referred to commitments by retail, hospitality and tourism employers to hire 250,000 young people over the next few years. Members debated funding, employer costs, disability benefits, mental health, apprenticeships, transport, school attendance, regional delivery, care leavers and special educational needs and disabilities. McFadden said, “The Timms review will report soon, and in the new year the Government will publish a White Paper and, following that, legislation.” He also said that people with severe and lasting disabilities for whom work was not realistic should receive more support.

### Points of order and Bills presented
Before the Bills and private Members’ business, Rupert Lowe raised a point of order disputing a ministerial description of people at a migrant camp as being “contained”. The Deputy Speaker said that correcting the record was a matter for the Minister and that the Clerks in the Table Office could advise on further options. Wendy Morton asked about parliamentary scrutiny of the Chagos Islands agreement; the Deputy Speaker said, “I have received no notice from Ministers that they intend to make a statement on this matter.”

Two Bills were presented and read the First time: the Images (Non-consensual Recording and Distribution) Bill, with Second Reading scheduled for 15 January 2027, and the Abattoir Services etc. Bill, with Second Reading scheduled for 12 March 2027.

### Safety of Nurseries (CCTV) Bill
Tulip Siddiq sought leave to introduce a Bill requiring CCTV in nurseries, alongside provisions on retention, storage, disclosure, review, auditing and device security. She argued that CCTV could act as a deterrent, support staff who raised safeguarding concerns and provide evidence after abuse; she said, “CCTV is not just about providing evidence after a crime has been committed.”

Christopher Chope supported the safeguarding objective but questioned universal legislation, its cost and the role of Ofsted, arguing that existing regulation should first be assessed. He said, “we should surely rely upon Ofsted to make a judgment” on whether universal CCTV was required. Leave was granted, the Bill was presented and the Question was agreed to; it was read the First time and ordered to be read a Second time on Friday 15 January 2027.

### Immigration and Asylum Bill: Report-stage debate and agreed measures
The Report-stage debate addressed the proposed Independent Immigration Appeals Authority (IIAA), removal notices, late claims, Article 31 of the Refugee Convention, modern slavery, asylum-support repayment, family reunion, settlement, legal aid, safe routes, large asylum accommodation and artificial intelligence. Anna Turley said that the IIAA was intended to address delays, noting that “The average time taken to clear appeals is at 65 weeks in the first-tier tribunal immigration and asylum chamber.” Government amendments provided for changes to the appeals process, including procedure rules on out-of-time appeals, remote hearings where appropriate, evidence statements and case management.

Members supporting the Bill emphasised faster decisions, removals and public confidence, while other Members raised concerns about safeguards, trafficking victims, children, families, settlement expectations and the qualifications of adjudicators. The Government said that the modern slavery amendments were compatible with the European Convention on Human Rights and that officials had worked with the Scottish Government. New clause 9 was added to the Bill, and new clauses 10, 11 and 12 and various Government amendments were also brought up and added; the transcript records no separate division on those measures. The Bill was later read a Third time and passed by 290 votes to 94.

### Immigration and Asylum Bill: divisions on proposed new clauses
The House rejected new clause 18, which would have required negotiations on United Kingdom participation in the European Union’s Pact on Migration and Asylum, by 60 votes to 410. It also rejected new clause 50, which would have restricted specified citizenship registrations and naturalisation applications for people who had met the conditions set out in the proposed duty-to-remove clause, by 102 votes to 382. New clause 54, which proposed a ten-year qualifying period for indefinite leave to remain for specified categories, was rejected by 99 votes to 384.

New clause 62, which would have protected existing Skilled Worker visa holders from adverse changes to settlement and other conditions, was rejected by 44 votes to 368. Supporters, including Neil Duncan-Jordan, argued that affected workers had made career, financial and family decisions under the existing rules. Anna Turley opposed the clause, saying that settlement was determined at the point of application and arguing that the clause would exempt 1.1 million people from the proposed criminality conditions as well as from changes to language and other requirements.

### Delegated legislation
The House approved, without debate, four international development instruments: orders concerning replenishment of the African Development Fund, the International Fund for Agricultural Development and the International Development Association. It also approved draft Persistent Organic Pollutants (Amendment) Regulations 2026 and the draft Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 (Amendment) Order 2026. The motions were put together under the leave of the House, and the transcript records that the Question was agreed to.

### NHS Federated Data Platform: Palantir
In the adjournment debate, Martin Wrigley questioned the NHS’s continuing use of Palantir’s Federated Data Platform (FDP). He called for an independent audit of usage and dependency, stronger data sovereignty, open standards, multiple suppliers and a competitive process that would allow United Kingdom small and medium-sized enterprises to participate. His view was that the system could be switched off without affecting the NHS, but this was presented as his assessment rather than as an established conclusion. He also asked how the Government would prevent Palantir’s incumbency from distorting future competition.

James Frith said that a new procurement process had begun and that the current contract would end in March 2029, while the existing supplier remained in place to provide continuity during the transition. He said, “The data remains NHS, as does the intellectual property, and there is no lock-in.” He also said that the Procurement Act 2023’s equal-treatment requirement would be taken into account, that access to data was restricted to authorised users and approved purposes, and that data was held in UK data centres and did not leave the UK. The Question was agreed to and the House adjourned.
