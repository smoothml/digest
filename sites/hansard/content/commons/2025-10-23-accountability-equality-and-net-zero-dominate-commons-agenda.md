## High-Level Summary
The Commons sat for Cabinet Office Questions on public sector reform, resilience, security and procurement, followed by two Urgent Questions on the collapsed China spying prosecution and the Fishing and Coastal Growth Fund. The weekly Business Statement set out forthcoming business and took questions on constituency and procedural issues. MPs then held a Government‑time debate to mark Black History Month, covering Windrush, equalities policy and racial health disparities. The day concluded with an adjournment debate on decarbonising UK cement, focusing on carbon capture, investment and market frameworks.

## Detailed Summary
### Speaker’s Statement: Parameters for debate on members of the Royal Family
The Speaker clarified how Members may raise matters relating to Prince Andrew and the Royal Family. He said that “Any reflections on the conduct of members of the royal family can properly be discussed on substantive motions” and that “criticism of members of the royal family cannot be made as part of questions.” [ref: b1081.1/1] He noted he cannot allocate time for such a motion but others may do so. Next steps: not stated in the transcript.

### Cabinet Office Questions: Reform, resilience, security and procurement
Opening on public sector reform, the Chief Secretary to the Prime Minister said the Government is “focused on changing how Government works…using modern technology with more accountability” [ref: b1081.8/1], and confirmed that “the Cabinet Office has responsibility for the policy, legislation and strategic oversight of the digital ID programme” with DSIT leading technical delivery [ref: b1082.0/1]. On probation accountability, Ministers said public protection is a priority and that the MoJ/HMPPS “take learnings from serious further offence reviews…to identify opportunities for improvement” [ref: b1082.2/1]. 

On future risks, Ministers cited Exercise Pegasus and new doctrine and data tools: “our preparedness…has been stepped up through Exercise Pegasus” [ref: b1084.7/1]; Government has “published an updated central crisis management doctrine—the Amber Book—as well as launching the biothreats radar” [ref: b1085.4/1]. A recent emergency alerts drill “reached 96% of cell masts” [ref: b1085.7/1]. On cyber resilience, Ministers said the Home Office is progressing “a new package of legislative measures to protect UK businesses from ransomware attacks” [ref: b1087.9/1]. 

On civil society partnerships, Government announced that “from April next year, all Government Departments must set a two-year target for direct spend with [VCSEs]…and…publish their results annually” [ref: b1084.1/1]. On procurement reform, Ministers said they are consulting on “a public interest test” to evaluate insourcing before contracting out [ref: b1090.0/1]. On UK‑EU relations and trade friction, the Chief Secretary said the Government had “entered into a new trade deal…with the European Union,” highlighting an SPS strand being worked on by the Europe Minister [ref: b1091.4/1]; the Europe Minister later described this as “the new common understanding” [ref: b1099.5/1]. 

On the civil service, Ministers set out a vision to recruit the “brightest and best” and a “Fast, fair, inclusive” approach [ref: b1088.3/2], affirmed confidence in the Cabinet Secretary [ref: b1089.2/1], and restated location reform: “By 2030, half of the senior civil service will be located outside London” [ref: b1092.0/1]. On national security coordination, Ministers referenced the resilience action plan and implementing the national security strategy [ref: b1092.5/1]. On defence, a Member referred to the Government’s “commitment to increasing defence spending to 2.5% by 2027” [ref: b1092.6/1]; the Minister highlighted sustained increases and industrial benefits [ref: b1092.7/1]. 

On infected blood compensation, the Paymaster General reported: “2,476 people have now received offers totalling over £1.8 billion…[and] applications are opening today for further interim payments of £210,000 to the estates” [ref: b1093.4/1]. On Opposition questions regarding the alleged China spy case, the Chief Secretary later told the House, “I was informed of this decision after the Prime Minister.” [ref: b1095.0/2] Outcomes: no divisions; several consultations and programmes to continue or expand.

### Urgent Question: Alleged spying case—role of the Attorney General’s Office
The Solicitor General emphasised prosecutorial independence: “prosecutions…are free from political influence” [ref: b1103.2/2]. She confirmed that for Official Secrets Act cases “Consent was given by my predecessor on 3 April 2024. Following that date, no Law Officer intervened in the case at any stage” [ref: b1103.2/4], and that the CPS decision to offer no evidence was made “without any political influence” [ref: b1103.2/5]. 

Pressed on when the Attorney General knew the case was at risk and what action was taken, she said, “The Attorney General will be setting out his evidence to the Joint Committee on the National Security Strategy next Tuesday.” [ref: b1109.3/1] She added that more recent legislation has removed the “enemy test”, making such prosecutions easier than under the 1911 Act [ref: b1107.2/1]. Outcome: no immediate action; next step is evidence to the JCNSS.

### Urgent Question: Fishing and Coastal Growth Fund
The Minister outlined the £360 million, 12‑year fund to modernise fleets, invest in skills and promote the seafood sector, and confirmed devolution: “the…fund would be devolved, and…devolved Governments would have full discretion over how to allocate funding.” [ref: b1111.2/3] She said this followed calls to learn from previous schemes and enable regional tailoring [ref: b1111.2/2, b1111.2/3]. Questions centred on allocations (including the Barnett formula), front‑loading, support for small fleets, and local priorities. On EU trade facilitation, she said an SPS agreement awaits the EU mandate “by the end of November,” after which the UK aims to proceed quickly [ref: b1117.0/1]. Outcome: approach confirmed; no changes announced.

### Business of the House: Forthcoming business and issues raised
The Leader announced business for the week commencing 27 October, including Remaining Stages of the Victims and Courts Bill and the Sentencing Bill, and Backbench Business debates [ref: b1119.2/2, b1119.2/5]. He said Government would “publish our response to the fair funding review 2.0 later this autumn,” followed by a provisional multi‑year settlement [ref: b1120.0/8]. Members raised topics such as fireworks misuse; he noted a “firework safety campaign” and existing enforcement powers [ref: b1124.0/2]. Outcome: business scheduled; no divisions.

### General Debate: Black History Month
Opening for the Government, the Minister for Equalities framed Black History Month as recognising that black history is British history (citing last year’s debate) and set out the Windrush reset, including: “re-established the Windrush unit in the Home Office and recruited a Windrush commissioner…the…single named caseworkers process…[and] a £1.5 million grant-funding programme” [ref: b1142.4/13]. She committed to “introducing mandatory ethnicity and disability pay gap reporting for large employers” with a draft Bill this Session [ref: b1145.1/2]. The Mother of the House welcomed progress on representation but urged delivery, saying many Windrush victims still await compensation: “the majority of Windrush migrants have not had their compensation” [ref: b1148.1/2]. 

Contributions ranged across racism, policing, education and health inequalities. The Health and Social Care Committee’s work on maternal outcomes was cited: “a black woman is 2.3 times more likely than a white woman to die during pregnancy or childbirth” [ref: b1166.0/8]. Closing, the Equalities Minister reported compensation progress—“paid over £116 million to over 3,000 applicants; 93%…have received final decisions” [ref: b1180.1/4]—and announced an independent investigation into maternity and neonatal services, with an interim recommendation “by December” [ref: b1181.1/2]. Outcome: motion agreed to without division [ref: b1183.0/4].

### Adjournment Debate: Decarbonisation of cement
The Member for Derbyshire Dales highlighted cement’s importance and emissions challenge, advocating carbon capture and storage (CCS) via the Peak Cluster, “the world’s largest cement decarbonisation project,” to decarbonise up to “40% of the UK’s cement and lime production” and remove “more than 3 million tonnes of CO2 each year” [ref: b1184.2/9, b1184.2/10]. He welcomed a £28 million National Wealth Fund investment but sought “a clear route to market” and Contracts‑for‑Difference‑style revenue certainty for CCS beyond Track‑1/2 [ref: b1184.2/12, b1184.2/13]. He stressed, “we cannot achieve zero-carbon cement without carbon capture and storage.” [ref: b1184.2/7]

The Industry Minister explained the calcination challenge—“two thirds of the emissions are from the calcination process” [ref: b1187.1/8]—and said he is open to exploring CfDs for CCS: “there could be a role for contracts for difference…[I] would be prepared to look at [it] more closely” [ref: b1187.1/12]. He also pointed to industrial electricity support and a UK carbon border adjustment mechanism, with draft legislation to “deliver it by January 2027” [ref: b1187.1/23]. Outcome: openness to CfD consideration; further engagement with Peak Cluster indicated.
