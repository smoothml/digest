## High-Level Summary
The day included Attorney General’s questions, Prime Minister’s Questions, two Opposition day debates, legislative business and an adjournment debate. Members discussed prosecution and victim support for violence against women and girls, welfare and youth employment, the proposed UK–EU agreements, and the future of the Rosebank oilfield. The House rejected the original Opposition motions on UK–EU relations and Rosebank, and agreed the Government amendments; Rosebank’s regulatory decision remained pending. The House also approved the specified delegated legislation and the deferred employment-rights regulations by 299 votes to 159.

## Detailed Summary
### Attorney General’s Questions
Questions to the Attorney General and Solicitor General covered prosecution rates for violence against women and girls, prevention of misogyny, technology-facilitated abuse, workplace harassment, economic abuse, rape-trial advocacy, jury-trial reform, Troubles-related prosecutions, reparations for the transatlantic slave trade, the Serious Fraud Office and victim support. Ellie Reeves said: “This Government are committed to halving violence against women and girls.” She cited victims’ rights to review, dedicated victim liaison officers, pre-trial meetings and measures concerning non-disclosure agreements. She also said that the Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) would publish a technology-facilitated violence against women and girls strategy: “The CPS will shortly publish its tech-facilitated VAWG strategy”. On economic abuse, she said she would discuss the issue with Ministry of Justice colleagues. On the Serious Fraud Office, Andrew Slaughter cited more than half a billion pounds secured for the taxpayer over five years and said the Government would seek a suitable legislative vehicle to strengthen its powers.

On jury trials, Slaughter declined to advise the Lord Chancellor to abandon the proposed end to elections for jury trial in certain burglary and theft cases, while affirming that both magistrates’ and Crown courts should provide the highest standards of justice. Reeves said that any Attorney General consent decisions for serious Troubles-related offences would be quasi-judicial, independent of Government and based on established evidential and public-interest tests. On reparations, she said financial reparations were not the right answer but supported structured, time-bound and inclusive dialogue, including discussion of a formal apology. On victim support, she said the national victim transformation programme would extend dedicated liaison officers to domestic-abuse victims and strengthen support for bereaved families: “It will no longer be a postcode lottery”.

### Prime Minister’s Questions and related points of order
Andy Burnham answered questions on cancer services, farming, welfare, youth employment, UK–EU relations, men’s health, brain-tumour research, transport, fishing, housing, homelessness, online abuse, taxis, radio astronomy, non-disclosure agreements and myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME). In response to Tim Farron, he said: “I will arrange for a Minister to meet him to discuss this issue.” Other commitments included working with Members on farming, fishing and transport in north Wales; supporting the youth hub in Hyndburn; focusing on women’s homelessness; meeting Andy’s Man Club; and having the Health Secretary meet Beth Dobbin about ME. Burnham also said that a national men’s health strategy was being launched and that the Government would support a new national brain-cancer clinical lead.

Kemi Badenoch pressed Burnham on whether welfare spending would be cut during the Parliament and argued that the Government’s approach would not produce immediate savings. Burnham said the Government would consider Alan Milburn’s report and had asked the Cabinet to reprioritise spending for 2027–28 to begin a fund supporting 25,000 work placements for 16- and 17-year-olds. He defended closer UK–EU engagement as a means of supporting growth and reducing costs, while reiterating the Government’s manifesto red lines and declining to commit to rejoining the EU. He also reaffirmed support for Ukraine. In a subsequent point of order, Julian Lewis raised the absence of a Government statement on discussions concerning the British Indian Ocean Territory; Lindsay Hoyle said that no ministerial statement had been notified.

### Opposition Day: UK–EU Relations
The Opposition motion argued that taxation, product and food standards, migration policies and related spending should remain under UK control, and opposed possible payments to the European Union, Erasmus+ arrangements and youth mobility proposals. Mike Wood argued that the proposed agreements could involve dynamic regulatory alignment without UK voting rights, impose costs on businesses and taxpayers, and reduce parliamentary control. He said: “We do not have a meaningful seat at that table”. Other Opposition Members questioned the transparency of the negotiations and the possible extent of dynamic alignment.

The Government amendment said that the previous EU agreement had imposed costs, checks and red tape, and supported a closer relationship with the EU to promote growth while respecting the manifesto red lines against freedom of movement, the Single Market and the Customs Union in the current Parliament. Hamish Falconer argued that the proposed agreements could reduce export costs, including through an agrifood arrangement, and cited a possible long-term GDP increase of 0.4%. Uma Kumaran said that the Government would pursue agreements on food and drink, emissions trading, energy, youth opportunities, security and migration. She also said: “The UK Government will bring forward primary legislation next week to implement the agreements.” The original motion was rejected by 103 votes to 274; the amendment was agreed, and the amended motion was resolved in favour, supporting progress at the UK–EU Summit on Friday 20 November 2026. The precise terms and costs of the agreements remained subject to the summit and subsequent legislation.

### Opposition Day: Rosebank Oilfield
The Opposition motion called for the Rosebank oilfield to be developed for energy security, economic growth and jobs. Andrew Bowie argued that domestic production would support employment, investment, tax receipts and energy resilience, and cited projected peak production of about 69,000 barrels of oil a day and possible annual tax receipts of around £500 million. Other Members disputed whether the field would materially improve energy security or reduce prices, noting that most of the oil was expected to enter the international market and raising concerns about its emissions and fiscal impact.

The Government argued for a managed North Sea transition in which existing oil and gas would remain part of the energy mix while investment, training and infrastructure supported renewables and other lower-carbon industries. Martin McCluskey said that Rosebank was subject to a live regulatory process and that: “Any decision must be taken in full accordance with the relevant legal requirements”. Liberal Democrat and Green speakers opposed approval on energy-security, fiscal and climate grounds, while SNP speakers argued that any resulting tax revenue should be ring-fenced for Scotland. The original motion was rejected by 90 votes to 270; the Government amendment was agreed by 253 votes to zero. The resulting resolution noted the quasi-judicial consenting process, required legal robustness and welcomed the commitment to update Parliament first on its outcome. No decision on Rosebank itself was announced; the timing of the decision was not stated in the transcript.

### Other business, points of order and adjournment debate
The Powers of Attorney Bill and the Future Generations (Policy Impact) Bill were presented and received First Reading. Their scheduled Second Reading dates were 27 November and 19 March 2027 respectively. Leave was granted for Tom Hayes’s Park Homes Bill, which proposes stronger enforcement powers, civil penalties, management orders, flood-risk protections, changes to the 10% sales charge and action concerning complex ownership structures. The motion was agreed—“Question put and agreed to.”—and the Bill was ordered for Second Reading on 15 January 2027. The two tobacco and vaping delegated-legislation motions were approved without debate. A National Policy Statement for Data Centres Committee was established, with Toby Perkins as Chair.

Neil Duncan-Jordan raised a point of order about a statement made by the Minister for Border Security and Asylum during the previous day’s debate; the Deputy Speaker said that the concern had been placed on the record and that established correction mechanisms existed. In the adjournment debate, Patrick Hurley argued for accurate and impartial free-to-air television news, stronger regional provision and scrutiny of Ofcom’s enforcement of broadcasting rules. Ian Murray confirmed that the Government remained committed to due accuracy and impartiality, said that the media Green Paper was examining Ofcom’s powers, and said that options for strengthening rules on politicians presenting current-affairs programmes were under consideration: “I can confirm that the Government remain committed to ensuring that free-to-air TV news is duly accurate and impartial.” The adjournment motion was agreed and the House adjourned. A deferred Division then approved the Employment Rights Act 2025 regulations on removing the limit on compensation for unfair dismissal by 299 votes to 159.
