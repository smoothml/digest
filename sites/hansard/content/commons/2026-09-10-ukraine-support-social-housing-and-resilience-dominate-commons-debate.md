## High-Level Summary
The Speaker announced arrangements to mark the 25th anniversary of the 9/11 attacks. Cabinet Office questions covered lobbying, national resilience, the EU–UK relationship, public bodies, civil service pensions, artificial intelligence, procurement and emergency preparedness, with Ministers giving several undertakings to report back or arrange meetings. An urgent question examined the 8 September air traffic control disruption; the Government sought a report from NATS by 16 September and an independent Civil Aviation Authority review within six months. The Defence Secretary reaffirmed support for Ukraine, including £100 million for air-defence interceptors, while Members debated defence spending, resilience and frozen Russian assets. The Social Housing Bill [Lords] received a Second Reading after the Opposition’s reasoned amendment was defeated by 77 votes to 292, and the day ended with an adjournment debate on rainwater management, sustainable drainage and water resilience.

## Detailed Summary
### Speaker’s statement: anniversary of the 9/11 attacks
Speaker Lindsay Hoyle announced that the Speaker’s Chaplain would deliver a special prayer before the following day’s business and that Members would be able to mark the anniversary in the next day’s Adjournment debate. He said that the House would remember the victims, their families and those involved in the rescue effort: “I know that colleagues across the House will join me in remembering the victims”.

### Cabinet Office questions: lobbying, ethics and integrity
Mark Ferguson, the Minister for the Cabinet Office, said the Government were considering the Ethics and Integrity Commission’s report on lobbying and would respond to Parliament in due course. Joe Powell supported the commission’s proposed unified digital open lobbying register; Jim Shannon asked how the rules would apply in Northern Ireland; and Lisa Smart questioned whether financial rewards linked to political activity on X should fall within lobbying and ethics rules.

Ferguson acknowledged shortcomings in the existing system, saying that it “is not good enough”. He did not announce immediate legislative action or give a timetable, and said that the Government would respond to the report as soon as they could.

### Cabinet Office questions: national resilience and severe weather
Dan Jarvis outlined the resilience action plan, a new pandemic preparedness strategy, updates to the Government’s “war books”, a planned home-defence exercise and a public awareness campaign on household preparedness. Members raised fire-service capacity, wildfire awareness, parish-council involvement, drought, flooding and local resilience planning. Jarvis said that decisions on fire services in Scotland were for the Scottish Government, while referring to the UK Government’s record block grant: “Decisions on fire services in Scotland are a matter for the Scottish Government”.

Jarvis said that the resilience action plan would involve the voluntary, community and faith sectors, including parish councils, and that the Government were learning from international partners such as Estonia. In later questions, he undertook to consider a Drinking Water Inspectorate report with the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs and to consult ministerial colleagues about flood boards. A later business-questions answer also referred to £97 million for national resilience assets and a wildfire summit in the autumn.

### EU–UK relationship, No. 10 North and economic capabilities
Hamish Falconer said that the Government wanted to deepen ties with the European Union and were holding intensive discussions on a sanitary and phytosanitary agreement. He could not give a date for the next summit. When Alex Burghart asked whether rejoining the EU would be ruled out, Falconer said: “We are not rerunning the arguments of 2016 once again”. He said that the Government’s manifesto red lines remained in place and that his focus was on improving the existing agreement.

Louise Haigh described the Office for the Prime Minister and Cabinet and No. 10 North as mechanisms for co-ordinating regional leaders, the Treasury and Departments, including through proposed fiscal devolution to mayors. She undertook to ask No. 10 North to consider local retention powers for the Golden Valley development and to write to Max Wilkinson. On the cost and staffing of No. 10 North, Dan Tomlinson said that expenditure and employment details would be published in the usual way with the Cabinet Office accounts.

### Arm’s-length bodies review
Mark Ferguson said that the Government had reviewed all 306 active arm’s-length bodies and had reform commitments affecting more than a third of them. He said that functions could be transferred to local leaders or returned to Departments under the principle of “devolution by default”: “all critical decisions should be taken by Ministers who are directly accountable to Parliament and the public”.

Peter Bedford asked how many bodies had been abolished and how much had been saved, but Ferguson reiterated that the review concerned the 306 active arm’s-length bodies. No abolition figure or saving was given in the transcript.

### Civil service pension scheme
Sally Jameson said that service levels under Capita’s administration of the civil service pension scheme were unacceptable. The Government were withholding contractual payments, had deployed about 147 surge staff, appointed an independent Capita-funded remedial adviser and were examining the long-term option of bringing the scheme in-house. She said: “No team members will be withdrawn from that surge team until service levels are permanently fixed”.

Jameson said that she expected to provide a more substantive update in October, following further technical and remedial work. She also offered to investigate individual cases where Members wrote to her. Further casework and the proposed October update were raised during business questions.

### Artificial intelligence, honours and infected blood compensation
Kanishka Narayan outlined Government programmes intended to support AI jobs through skills, computing capacity, capital and research. He said that the AI skills boost programme would focus on upskilling 2 million people in small and medium-sized enterprises and that the Government were considering boot-camp pilots for young people not in employment, education or training.

On honours, Hamish Falconer said that potential cases of disrepute could be considered by the independent Forfeiture Committee, but declined to comment on an individual case. On infected blood compensation, Louise Haigh said that the Infected Blood Compensation Authority had asked 6,321 people to start claims and had received 20,487 registrations by 25 August: “As of 25 August, IBCA had received 20,487 registrations”. She said that the Government would respond to the Public Accounts Committee in due course and offered a ministerial meeting about an individual case.

### Public procurement, technology and emergency alerts
Louise Haigh said that public-procurement reforms would reduce red tape, focus social value on jobs and skills, and be supported by new guidance in the autumn. Mark Ferguson said that Departments had set targets for spending with small and medium-sized enterprises and that changes to social-value requirements were intended to make it easier for smaller businesses to bid. Ministers offered meetings on local procurement, manufacturing and farming-related proposals.

Kanishka Narayan said that technology and artificial intelligence should support public services and British businesses, including through faster procurement. Dan Jarvis said that evidence after the recent emergency alert showed a measurable reduction in new wildfire incidents, while acknowledging concerns about the order and language of alerts in Wales and undertaking to learn lessons. No division or immediate legislative decision was recorded on these matters.

### Topical Cabinet Office questions
Topical questions covered political donations, the civil service pension backlog, contaminated-blood compensation, procurement, fire-service volunteering, emergency alerts, civil-service structures and several constituency cases. On alleged foreign funding and political donations, Dan Jarvis said that the Government would establish a cross-Government task-and-finish team on megadonors, while noting that a live police investigation limited what he could say: “We will assemble a new cross-Government task-and-finish team”.

Ministers also undertook to arrange meetings or provide updates on pension cases, contaminated-blood claims, fire-service pressures, procurement concerns and other constituency matters. Louise Haigh said that mayors would not receive powers to set income-tax thresholds or issue income-tax rebates, although grants would remain available. The transcript records no division on the topical questions.

### Urgent question: air traffic control disruption
Keir Mather apologised for the disruption caused by a technical problem in NATS’s flight-processing system at Swanwick on 8 September. He said that the problem affected data supplied to controllers and caused widespread delays, cancellations and diversions; NATS did not believe that it was cyber-related. The Government asked NATS to provide a report by Wednesday 16 September and asked the Civil Aviation Authority to conduct an independent review, with an update within six months: “NATS has confirmed that it was a technical issue and it does not believe it to be cyber-related”.

Members questioned the review timetable, contingency planning, investment, the implementation of recommendations from the 2023 outage, passenger compensation and support for workers and disabled passengers. Mather said that the 34 recommendations from the 2023 incident had been implemented and defended six months as proportionate for a highly technical review. He confirmed that passengers whose flights were cancelled were entitled to a refund within seven days or rerouting, and said that the incident would inform the Civil Aviation (Consumer Protection and Regulatory Reform) Bill.

### Business of the House
Leader of the House Alan Campbell announced business for the week beginning 14 September, including the Sovereign Grant Bill, debates on Israel and Palestine, fracking and World Sepsis Awareness Month, and the planned stages of the Northern Ireland Troubles Bill. He said that the House would rise for the conference recess at the conclusion of business on Tuesday 15 September and return on Monday 12 October.

John Lamont questioned the absence of a separate Report stage for the Northern Ireland Troubles Bill and asked about reports of an early general election. Campbell said that he would consider the timing of amendments and stated that the Prime Minister “has no intention of calling an early general election”. Members also raised suicide prevention, baby-loss debate scheduling, hospital radio, social care, violence against women and girls, local government reform, access to cash, housing, health and constituency casework. Campbell gave several undertakings to seek ministerial updates or arrange meetings, including an October update on the civil service pension scheme.

### Defence statement: Ukraine and Russia
Wes Streeting, making his first statement to the House as Defence Secretary, reaffirmed UK support for Ukraine and described Russia’s invasion as a threat to European collective security. He announced £100 million for the rapid delivery of Ukrainian air-defence interceptors, reaffirmed a commitment to provide more than 120,000 drones during the year and cited total UK commitments of £25 billion. He said that the Government would support Ukraine “for as long as it takes”.

Mark Francois welcomed support for Ukraine but questioned reported restrictions on military training and pressed for a timetable for defence spending to reach 3% of gross domestic product. Streeting said that training continued but had been reprioritised, that the Defence Investment Plan had a shortfall of just under £5 billion which the Chancellor had committed to address at the Budget, and that he would not pre-empt the spending review. Members also raised air defence, frozen Russian assets, Russian energy revenues, disinformation, national resilience and UK defence manufacturing. Streeting said that he would report back on plans for a wider national conversation about resilience.

### Bills presented and committee arrangements
The Highways (Financing) Bill and Housing Bill were presented and given First Reading. The Highways Bill was scheduled for Second Reading the following day, and the Housing Bill for Second Reading on Friday 27 November.

The House agreed to establish a Joint Committee on the draft Conversion Practices Bill, comprising six Commons Members and reporting by 22 January 2027. It also discharged Michael Wheeler from the Committee on Standards and the Committee of Privileges and added James Asser to each.

### Social Housing Bill [Lords]: Second Reading and programme
Matthew Pennycook said that the Bill would reform right to buy, strengthen protections for social tenants experiencing domestic abuse, repeal unused provisions of the Housing and Planning Act 2016 and streamline housing consents. The proposals included a 10-year eligibility requirement, revised discounts, exemption of newly built social homes from right to buy for 30 years, rural exemptions and notification requirements before private registered providers disposed of social homes. Pennycook said that the package was expected to produce a net gain of about 18,000 social homes over 10 years, compared with a projected loss of 26,000 under the existing system.

The Opposition’s reasoned amendment argued that the Bill would undermine home ownership, fail to increase housebuilding and should ensure that social housing was reserved for British citizens. Gareth Bacon supported the domestic-abuse provisions but opposed the right-to-buy reforms and said that the Bill would not build new homes. Other Members sought stronger action on council building, rural housing, housing-association disposals, shared ownership, furniture poverty and domestic-abuse-related rent arrears. The amendment was defeated, Ayes 77 and Noes 292. The Bill then received a Second Reading and was committed to a Public Bill Committee, whose proceedings were scheduled to conclude by 3 November 2026.

### Point of order: cross-party social-care talks
Ellie Chowns challenged the Leader of the House’s account of invitations to cross-party social-care talks, saying that the Green Party had not received an invitation. Deputy Speaker Caroline Nokes said that the content of ministerial answers was not a matter for the Chair, but that the Government Front Bench would have noted the point: “If any correction to the record is necessary because of an inadvertent error, I am sure it will be forthcoming”.

No ruling was made on the accuracy of the earlier ministerial answer, and no correction was recorded in the transcript.

### Adjournment debate: water management
Gideon Amos argued for managing rainwater where it falls through household rainwater harvesting, sustainable drainage systems, separated drainage and retrofitting, alongside—not instead of—reservoirs and other infrastructure. He linked the approach to water scarcity, flooding, storm overflows, carbon emissions and household bills, and said that it could reduce bills by roughly a quarter to a third. Members also discussed developers’ contributions, reservoirs and the implementation of Schedule 3 to the Flood and Water Management Act 2010.

Emma Hardy agreed that prevention-focused and nature-based measures should form part of a wider system that included reservoirs. She said that the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government was reviewing water-efficiency standards and considering safe, practical and cost-effective water-reuse systems. She also referred to national standards for sustainable drainage and joined-up planning for housing, drainage, water supply and wastewater. The motion to adjourn was agreed and the House adjourned.
