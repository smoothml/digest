## High-Level Summary
The House considered Justice questions on rehabilitation, family courts, prison recalls and capacity, court backlogs, probation, violence against women and girls, and victim support. An urgent question examined alleged Chinese access to parliamentarians’ data, with the Security Minister citing an FBI investigation, a live Interpol arrest warrant and the need not to prejudice ongoing proceedings; he also referred Members to private discussions and existing security guidance. Statements covered the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting, including investment, climate resilience and a structured dialogue on the legacy of slavery, and revised mobile-phone guidance for schools alongside a consultation on education-technology safety. The House gave the Financial Services and Markets Bill a Second Reading and agreed its programme, money and ways-and-means motions, before considering delegated legislation and an adjournment debate on Stepping Hill Hospital.

## Detailed Summary
### Deferred Divisions and opening business
**Context and participants —** The Speaker announced the results of deferred Divisions held the previous Wednesday. The Housing (Tenancy Deposit Schemes) Order 2026 was approved by 310 votes to 114, and the Private Rented Sector Database Regulations 2026 by 316 votes to 149. The announcement stated: “the Ayes were 310 and the Noes were 114”.

**Outcome and next steps —** Both questions were agreed to, and the Division lists were published at the end of the day’s debates. The later records give the same totals: 310 to 114 and 316 to 149.

### Justice questions: rehabilitation, reoffending and prison education
**Context and participants —** Helen Maguire asked about rehabilitative programmes. Alex Norris said that education, skills, work, offender-behaviour programmes and substance-misuse treatment were central, stating: “Education, skills and work are central to rehabilitation”. He supported multi-agency, place-based planning before release and said the Government were working with local authorities.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** Catherine Atkinson, Chair of the Justice Committee, raised concerns about prison education and purposeful activity. Norris linked capacity pressures to difficulties moving prisoners to treatment, education and work, and said he intended to work with the Committee. Liz Saville-Roberts argued that devolving justice was important to improving outcomes in Wales; Norris instead referred to discussions with the Welsh Government and cooperation among prisons, local authorities, health services and charities. Adam Thompson asked about pregnant women leaving prison; Norris described housing as “the anchor” and referred to up to 84 days of emergency accommodation.

### Justice questions: sexual offending, family courts and cross-border protection
**Context and participants —** Lauren Sullivan asked about sexual offences committed by healthcare professionals. Alex Davies-Jones said such offences should be dealt with regardless of the perpetrator’s profession, agreed to meet Sullivan and survivors, and said she would continue discussions with the Department of Health and Social Care. She also said that unauthorised access to patient records should attract accountability and that she would raise the issue with counterparts across the United Kingdom.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** Jake Richards outlined the child-focused model, new child-safety measures and a future family-justice strategy, with national rollout of the model by 2029. He acknowledged that the 26-week statutory target for care proceedings was being missed and said he would report to Parliament on progress. In response to a case involving an alleged abduction to Saudi Arabia, he offered to meet the Member and said information-sharing between family courts, the Home Office, border security and the Foreign Office needed to improve so that protective orders carried weight across borders.

### Justice questions: early release, recall and prison-estate capacity
**Context and participants —** Members asked how many people released under the early-release scheme had later been recalled. Norris said it was too early to draw conclusions about recall patterns under the progression model and referred Members to the quarterly offender-management statistics. In response to questions from Bradley Thomas and Julian Lewis, he defended recalling people who breached licence conditions and said: “one in seven prisoners has been recalled”. He did not provide a forecast of future recalls, referring Members to the usual publication cycle.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** Nick Timothy questioned tagging and early release. Norris said that full data would be published in due course and repeated the Government’s commitments to a presumption of tagging and the largest expansion of tagging in history. In response to Jessica Brown-Fuller, he said the Sentencing Act 2026 was intended to move the system away from early-release schemes and stressed the importance of keeping existing cells operational. Catherine McKinnell said the Government were targeting 14,000 additional prison places by 2031, had delivered 3,300, and were bringing forward £110 million to accelerate delivery.

### Justice questions: court backlog and artificial intelligence in legal services
**Context and participants —** Sarah Sackman said the Crown Court backlog stood at nearly 81,000 cases. She identified investment, additional sitting-day capacity, technology and the Courts and Tribunals Bill as the Government’s principal responses. Members raised delays in rape, sexual-abuse and fraud cases, employment tribunals, and mental-health legal-aid provision.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** Sackman said the judiciary sought to prioritise cases where delay might prevent a trial, acknowledged that further work was needed on civil and mental-health legal aid, and referred to additional judicial recruitment for employment tribunals. On legal technology, she cited an advisory artificial-intelligence growth lab and continued funding for LawtechUK. She also described AI transcription, case-readiness tools and scheduling tools being used in the courts.

### Justice questions: sex-offender supervision, recidivism and probation
**Context and participants —** Catherine McKinnell said probation staff had access to restriction zones, GPS monitoring and a strengthened supervision package for the most dangerous offenders convicted of sexual offences, supported by more than £700 million of investment. She said probation services could already prohibit offenders on licence from possessing or using smart glasses and agreed to look further into concerns about vacancies and tag failures; she cited 1,389 trainee probation officers as having been onboarded.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** On reoffending, McKinnell referred to better access to treatment, education, employment and accommodation, and to the expansion of intensive supervision courts. She acknowledged the challenges posed by drugs and drones in prisons and linked rehabilitation difficulties to capacity pressures. She also said that people homeless on release had reoffending rates nearly double those of people released into accommodation, and referred to cross-Government work on homelessness.

### Justice questions: violence against women and girls and victim support
**Context and participants —** Alex Davies-Jones described the Government’s approach to violence against women and girls as preventing offences, pursuing perpetrators and supporting victims. She said the Courts and Tribunals Bill would address the use of rape myths and previous allegations, and referred to independent legal advisers and specialist rape and sexual-abuse courts.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** Members raised post-separation abuse, injunctions, strategic lawsuits against public participation, jurisdiction in cases involving United States service personnel, and proposed “Eleanor’s law”. Davies-Jones said privacy injunctions were considered independently by the courts, while the Government were examining what more could be done, including on strategic lawsuits against public participation. She agreed to look into Eleanor’s law. On victim services, she said £550 million would be invested over three years and that the Government’s ambition was to extend free sentencing-remarks transcripts to all victims in Crown Court cases from spring 2027.

### Justice topical questions: prisons, victims, coroners and further commitments
**Context and participants —** Topical questions revisited early release, tagging, prison capacity, coercive-control offences, coroners’ backlogs, care-experienced people in custody, legal aid and access to data. Ministers referred to continuing or planned work, including publication of early-release and tagging data, a protocol on care-experienced people in prison, and consideration of prevention-of-future-deaths reports.

**Outcome and next steps —** Ministers also agreed to or offered meetings on domestic-abuse legal aid, animal abuse and domestic abuse, horse-related road incidents, coronial delays affecting religious burial, and a constituent’s grievous-bodily-harm case. Jake Richards said a protocol on care-experienced people in prison would be published “in the next few weeks”. He also estimated that up to 120,000 families would benefit from child-trust-fund changes, with an average amount of £2,600.

### Urgent question: alleged Chinese access to parliamentarians’ data
**Context and participants —** Iain Duncan Smith asked what the Government knew about alleged access to the phone records and financial information of parliamentarians sanctioned by China, and why affected Members had not been briefed. Security Minister Dan Jarvis said there was an ongoing FBI investigation and a live Interpol arrest warrant, and that commenting on specific allegations could prejudice the investigation. He said officials were available to meet affected Members and referred to guidance from the National Cyber Security Centre and the National Protective Security Authority.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** Members pressed for information about notification, China’s status under the Foreign Influence Registration Scheme (FIRS), and possible consequences for transnational repression. Jarvis said FIRS had been introduced on 1 July 2025 and was still being developed as an operational capability. He said the first annual report would be published as soon as possible. He described China as posing threats including cyber-attacks, foreign interference, espionage and transnational repression, but did not commit to placing it in the enhanced tier of FIRS. Mr Speaker said he had not been briefed, and ended the exchange by saying that the matter would be pursued through other avenues.

### Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting statement
**Context and participants —** Chris Elmore reported on the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in Antigua and Barbuda from 1 to 4 November. He highlighted trade and investment, climate resilience, democratic resilience and support for small states, including a British International Investment partnership, infrastructure and clean-power funding, and a Jamaica reconstruction partnership. He said the summit had supported a more focused Commonwealth and helped build momentum towards COP31 and the United Kingdom’s G20 presidency.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** A principal point of debate was the proposed structured dialogue on the legacy of the transatlantic slave trade. Elmore said the Government had expressed sorrow and regret, was open to considering a formal apology, and would consider remembrance, education, culture and practical cooperation, while stating: “our position on financial reparations has not changed”. Opposition Members questioned the emphasis on the past, aid reductions, Chagos and engagement with India, Africa and the Caribbean. Elmore said the dialogue was time-limited and forward-looking, confirmed that the position on reparations had not changed, and declined to set a timetable for returning official development assistance to 0.7%.

### Mobile phones in schools and education-technology safety
**Context and participants —** Josh MacAlister announced revised statutory guidance for England so that schools should ensure pupils could not keep unsecured mobile phones in blazers or bags throughout the school day. The guidance is due to take effect from September 2027; schools may use collection, lockers, pouches or another secure method, with reasonable adjustments and exemptions where appropriate, including for medical needs and special educational needs and disabilities. The announcement cited reported compliance of 20% in schools with “not seen, not heard” policies and 74% in schools with mandatory collection policies.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** The Government also launched a consultation on independent safety certification for education technology in schools, colleges and early-years settings. It will consider harmful content, personal-data use, cognitive development and social and emotional wellbeing, with findings informing any future scheme. Members raised implementation costs, bus-payment arrangements, screen time, young-carer exemptions, data protection, and the safety and efficacy of artificial-intelligence tutors. MacAlister said trials and evaluation would assess educational outcomes before widespread deployment and announced parental guidance on healthy screen time for older children early the following year.

### English Ethnic Identity (Census Particulars) Bill
**Context and participants —** Josh Babarinde sought leave to introduce a Bill providing for English ethnic-identity categories, including “Black English” and “Asian English”, in census particulars in England. He argued that the current ethnicity question places “English” only under the White category and said the proposal would add choices without removing existing categories.

**Outcome and next steps —** The House agreed to the motion. Babarinde presented the Bill, with David Lammy, Sir James Cleverly, Adam Jogee, Ben Obese-Jecty, Victoria Collins, Munira Wilson, Layla Moran and Mark Pritchard listed as presenters. It was read the First time and ordered to be read a Second time on Friday 23 April 2027 and printed as Bill 178.

### Financial Services and Markets Bill [Lords]: Second Reading
**Context and participants —** John Healey moved Second Reading of the Financial Services and Markets Bill. He described its four principal purposes as streamlining regulation, expanding credit unions, modernising consumer protection and redress, and supporting business lending and investment. He said the Government would amend the Bill in Committee to improve access to child trust funds for young people lacking mental capacity, potentially benefiting up to 126,000 people. He also said the measures in the Bill were estimated to be worth about £1.6 billion over 10 years.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** The Opposition said it would not vote against Second Reading but argued for more extensive deregulation, including abolition rather than reform of ringfencing, lower capital requirements and a statutory digital-assets strategy. Other Members warned that changes to the Financial Ombudsman Service, including a 10-year complaint limit, referrals to the Financial Conduct Authority and changes to the fair-and-reasonable test, could reduce consumer redress. The Minister said amendments would be brought forward on the referral mechanism, that the FCA would specify which products were unsuitable for the 10-year rule, and that the Government would consider the Lords’ digital-assets reforms. The Bill was read a Second time without a recorded division.

### Financial Services and Markets Bill: Committee issues and programme
**Context and participants —** Members discussed credit-union membership, affordable credit, mortgage prisoners, defence small and medium-sized enterprises, mutuals, face-to-face banking, anti-money-laundering supervision and legally privileged material. The Government confirmed that it intended to restore the power to act on the access-to-banking review and to extend common-bond reforms in Committee. It also said that a forthcoming defence finance and investment strategy would review barriers to legitimate defence companies obtaining finance.

**Outcome and next steps —** Members supported consolidation of anti-money-laundering supervision of legal, accountancy, and trust and company service providers within the Financial Conduct Authority, while raising concerns about transition, resources and access to legally privileged material. Detailed amendments were left for Committee and later stages. The House ordered the Bill to a Public Bill Committee, with proceedings due to conclude by Tuesday 15 December 2026; the Committee may sit twice on its first day. The related money and ways-and-means motions were agreed.

### Delegated legislation: terrorism and court recording
**Context and participants —** The House considered three delegated-legislation motions without debate: continuation of Terrorism Prevention and Investigation Measures, amendments concerning recording and broadcasting in the Administrative Court and Court of Appeal, and the Magistrates’ Courts (Recording and Broadcasting) Order.

**Outcome and next steps —** Each question was agreed to.

### Adjournment debate: Stepping Hill Hospital
**Context and participants —** Tom Morrison raised the condition of Stepping Hill Hospital, citing leaks, sinking floors, infection-control constraints, accessibility problems, outdated information technology and insufficient privacy for end-of-life care. He called for a fully funded strategy and cited an estimated £134 million requirement for comprehensive repairs. Lisa Smart supported examining a new Stockport site as a longer-term use of capital. Navendu Mishra highlighted existing allocations, including £2.5 million for targeted safety works, £26 million for a pathology building and £75 million for the trust over four years.

**Arguments, outcome and next steps —** Alison McGovern said the debate would be reported to the responsible Minister and confirmed that Stepping Hill would benefit from regional estates-safety funding. She cited £26 million for the pathology building, £17 million for 11 critical repair projects and £75 million in operational capital over four years. She agreed to provide written responses on concerns about staff toilets and end-of-life care and to pass on requests for ministerial visits. The motion was agreed to and the House adjourned.
