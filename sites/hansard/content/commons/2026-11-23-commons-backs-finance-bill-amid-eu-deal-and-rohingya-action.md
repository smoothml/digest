## High-Level Summary
The House began with Work and Pensions questions on youth employment, personal independence payment, welfare spending, migration-related benefit access, fraud and devolved employment support. The Prime Minister then reported on the UK-EU summit, defending proposed arrangements on trade, security, youth mobility, energy, food exports and other matters while Members questioned their cost, detail and implications. The Foreign Secretary formally recognised the genocide of the Rohingya following the International Court of Justice judgment and announced sanctions, humanitarian assistance and diplomatic measures. The House rejected the Finance Bill reasoned amendment by 98 votes to 317, gave the Bill a Second Reading by 311 votes to 164, agreed its programme, dealt with formal committee changes and later debated the closure of Leweston school before adjourning.

## Detailed Summary
### Work and Pensions questions: youth employment and the youth guarantee
Pat McFadden said the Government was delivering a youth guarantee through earlier and more intensive jobcentre support, more than 175 youth hubs, a £3,000 youth jobs grant and a national jobs guarantee. He said eligibility for the jobs guarantee would be brought forward from 18 months to 12 months on universal credit from the following April, and referred to agreements with the retail, hospitality and tourism sectors covering 250,000 opportunities for young people not in education, employment or training. On delivery, he said that “more than 175 are now open”. He also said that a fuller Government response to the Milburn and Timms reviews would be issued in the new year.

Members including Tessa Munt, Iain Duncan Smith, Ashley Fox, Rebecca Smith and Sarah Olney argued that employment taxes, business costs, wages or national insurance could discourage recruitment. McFadden referred to the existing national insurance exemption for employers of workers under 21 and rejected reducing young people’s pay. Other contributions concerned rural transport, further education colleges, apprenticeships, work experience and access for small businesses. Helen Whately argued that youth-guarantee trailblazers had performed poorly and should be abandoned; McFadden rejected that position, saying “we will not abandon it, because we believe in young people and want to support them”. The stated next steps were the wider rollout of the jobs guarantee, the response to the Milburn review and further detail in the “Rewiring the State” delivery plan.

### Work and Pensions questions: PIP, welfare spending, migration and delivery
Stephen Timms said the final recommendations of his review of personal independence payment (PIP) would be published the following day. He said the review would call for a fundamental overhaul of PIP, retain it as a cash-first benefit and consider additional services alongside awards. He acknowledged delays and other problems with the current assessment system, stating that “the current assessment is not fit for purpose”, and said that disabled people’s organisations would be closely involved in developing changes.

Lilian Greenwood said that the proportion of universal credit claimants who were foreign nationals had fallen from 16% to 15.4% between August 2025 and August 2026. She said earned-settlement reforms were initially estimated to save billions from welfare spending, with fuller details to accompany the immigration-rule changes. Andrew Western said employment support would be devolved to mayors, including tailored support for the long-term unemployed, with further detail to come in the “Rewiring the State” delivery plan. Members also raised official-error overpayments, welfare fraud, rural access to employment support, disability-benefit assessments, carer’s allowance and child-maintenance enforcement. Ministers offered meetings or further consideration in individual cases, but no general division or immediate policy decision was recorded.

### UK-EU summit statement
Andy Burnham reported on meetings in Berlin and Brussels and defended an agreement covering security and defence, food and agricultural trade, emissions-trading links, possible electricity-market cooperation, youth experience arrangements, touring artists, border cooperation and industrial protections. He said the agreement provided for a “stronger Europe in a stronger NATO”, and presented it as meeting the Government’s three tests concerning its manifesto red lines, improvement on the existing Brexit arrangements and benefits for communities in the UK. He cited a Treasury estimate of an increase of up to 0.5%, or £17 billion, in annual UK gross domestic product. He said the European partnership Bill would be introduced on Thursday and that the Government would seek its Second Reading before the Christmas adjournment.

Kemi Badenoch argued that the agreement was insufficiently detailed, could make the UK a rule-taker and would not necessarily reduce food or energy costs. Members also questioned tuition fees, rules of origin, Northern Ireland checks, digital identity, animal welfare, fishing, the youth scheme’s age limit, electricity-market costs and parliamentary scrutiny. Burnham said the tuition-fee discussions were separate, denied that the agreement committed the UK to joining the European Union or adopting its digital identity system, and said it would remove 90% of sanitary and phytosanitary checks in the Irish Sea. In response to a question about rejoining the EU without a referendum, he said: “Yes, it would be unthinkable”. Immediate next steps included publication and parliamentary consideration of the Bill, and further negotiations on tuition fees, electricity, touring, rules of origin and other matters.

### Myanmar and the Rohingya genocide
Ed Miliband formally recognised the genocide committed against the Rohingya following the International Court of Justice judgment of 19 November. He announced the expulsion of Myanmar’s defence attaché, sanctions against four Majors General and a Brigadier General, proposed legislation to prevent the export and supply of aviation fuel used in attacks, a £3 million package for victims and survivors, doubled support for the UN investigative mechanism for Myanmar and an additional £5 million in humanitarian assistance for approximately 350,000 people. He also said that the UK would raise the matter at the UN Security Council, work with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations and press for an immediate pause in hostilities, humanitarian access and an inclusive political settlement. The Government’s position was that “the United Kingdom formally acknowledges the genocide committed against the Rohingya”.

Wendy Morton condemned the atrocities but questioned the legal reasoning, referring to the Court’s 9–6 division and asking about proof of genocidal intent, evidence and implications for defence policy. Miliband defended reliance on the International Court of Justice as the competent court, saying that “a majority view is the view of the Court”. Members sought further sanctions, action to implement the judgment, support for Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh, education and livelihoods, and measures against the Myanmar military’s financing and communications networks. Miliband undertook to maintain diplomatic pressure, examine further sanctions, continue engagement with Bangladesh and report to the House.

### Points of order
Paul Holmes asked the Chair for advice on pursuing a delayed response to a case that had been sent from the Ministry of Justice to the Department of Health and Social Care. Nusrat Ghani said that the timeliness of ministerial correspondence was not a matter for the Chair, but that Holmes’s comments had been heard by the Treasury Front Bench. Al Pinkerton then challenged a claim made during the UK-EU statement about the depth of the UK’s trade agreement with the European Union. Ghani did not rule on the substance, reminding the House that “Members, not the Chair, are responsible for the content of their statements”. No further procedural action was recorded.

### Finance Bill: Second Reading and programme
The Government presented the Finance Bill as providing short-term support while maintaining fiscal discipline and promoting growth. Dan Tomlinson and James Murray highlighted a £100 household energy payment for 20 million households in council tax bands A to D in England, business-rates relief, a temporary increase in the bank corporation tax surcharge, changes to machine games duty, support for founders and research and development, a higher trading allowance, an electric-vehicle excise duty from 2028, tax-gap measures and a high-value council-tax surcharge. Tomlinson said: “Every policy in our Budget is fully funded”.

The Conservative reasoned amendment argued that taxes, borrowing and debt were too high and called for tax reductions, including lower employer national insurance contributions for young people and the abolition of stamp duty and inheritance tax on the family home. Harriett Baldwin and Mel Stride argued that higher taxes on savings, landlords, banks and businesses could reduce investment, employment and housing supply. Liberal Democrat Members supported some business measures but said the Bill was not a growth Budget, proposed more targeted energy support and advocated closer economic integration with the European Union. Other Members discussed tax complexity, start-up finance, procurement, energy costs, infrastructure, devolution, student loans and the distributional effects of the measures.

The reasoned amendment was defeated, with 98 Ayes and 317 Noes. The Bill then received a Second Reading, with 311 Ayes and 164 Noes. Its programme provided that specified clauses would be considered by a Committee of the whole House over two days and that the remainder would be committed to a Public Bill Committee, including that “The remainder of the Bill shall be committed to a Public Bill Committee”. The programme motion was agreed.

### Formal committee changes
The House ordered changes to several bodies without debate. Lee Dillon was appointed to the House of Commons Commission in place of Bobby Dean, and Bobby Dean was discharged from the Modernisation Committee with Lee Dillon added. Cameron Thomas was discharged from the Digital, Culture, Media and Sport Committee and Alison Bennett was added; Matt Bishop was discharged from the Justice Committee and Alex Barros-Curtis was added. The relevant motions were ordered as formal business, with no debate or further decision recorded.

### Adjournment debate: VAT and Leweston School closure
Edward Morello raised the closure of Leweston school, describing its effects on 550 pupils, 54 staff, parents who had paid fees in advance, pupils with special educational needs and disabilities, and local authorities arranging alternative places. He called for investigations into the school’s ownership, finances, governance, handling of pupil records, staff pay and redundancy, site security and nursery funding. He also proposed stronger regulation of independent-school ownership, financial viability checks, early-warning requirements, minimum notice periods and funded closure plans. He concluded: “Leweston’s closure should never have happened in this way”.

Josh MacAlister said that private-school proprietors were responsible for decisions about ownership, financial sustainability and closure, but that the immediate closure and the communication with families were not acceptable. He said that all pupils with special educational needs and disabilities had secured alternative places and that most remaining pupils had secured places in other private schools, with Department for Education regional staff working with Dorset and Somerset councils. On VAT, he said closure rates since its introduction were below the ten-year average and defended the policy as supporting investment in state education. The motion for the House to adjourn was agreed and the House adjourned.
