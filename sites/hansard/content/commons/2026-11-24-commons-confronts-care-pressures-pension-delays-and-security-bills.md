## High-Level Summary
The day began with Health and Social Care oral questions on dentistry, fertility treatment, NHS reorganisation, maternity services, social care, waiting times and the proposed national care service. An urgent question examined delays in Capita’s administration of civil service pensions; the Government defended its recovery plan while continuing contingency work and considering future insourcing options. The House then heard statements on the Timms review of Personal Independence Payment and the outcome of COP31, followed by debates on funeral-sector regulation, the Armed Forces Bill, cyber security and Saltend Chemicals Park. Divisions rejected specified Lords amendments to the Armed Forces Bill and the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, while a ten-minute rule Bill on registering funeral directors received leave to be introduced.

## Detailed Summary
### Health and Social Care oral questions
Health and Social Care questions involved Yvette Cooper, Diana R. Johnson, Karin Smyth, Alison McGovern, James Frith and other Ministers, with Members raising dentistry, NHS-funded fertility treatment, NHS reorganisation, maternity services, local healthcare, carers, waiting times, the NHS App, the proposed national care service, ill-health prevention and support for women affected by diethylstilbestrol. Ministers said that NHS-funded fertility provision was being assessed across integrated care boards; NHS England would return to the Department of Health and Social Care next April, subject to the Health Bill; and the Government would publish maternity reforms and an action plan. On North Devon maternity services, the Government attributed the continued closure to recruitment problems and said NHS England was meeting the trust daily: “We will continue to support the trust and to maintain that pressure and work in order to get those services safely opened, as rapidly as possible.”

On the proposed national care service, Yvette Cooper said Baroness Casey’s independent commission would recommend next summer how it should proceed. She also referred to the Government’s plan for free personal care and an adjustment to the triple lock: “The Prime Minister has set out a plan for free personal care.” Other stated next steps included imminent results from the ICB assessment of fertility provision, a decision by the St Helier trust board at the beginning of December, and further work on maternity, social care and neighbourhood support. No division is recorded in the oral-questions section of the transcript.

### Civil service pension scheme and Capita
The urgent question focused on delays, incorrect calculations, communication problems and delayed retirement and bereavement payments in Capita’s administration of the civil service pension scheme. Richard Tice said that the number of outstanding cases had risen from about 86,000 to almost 160,000 and argued that Capita’s financial position required detailed contingency planning, including possible insourcing. Other Members raised individual cases, compensation, contractual penalties, future procurement and the treatment of urgent or vulnerable cases.

Sally Jameson said that Cabinet Office officials now had full access to Capita’s systems and data, that a surge team of about 150 civil servants remained in place, and that a five-workstream recovery plan was operating. The Government had withheld nearly £10 million in contractual payments, while Capita had accepted a voluntary 10% reduction in monthly payments. The Minister said: “My priority at this point is making sure that the service is restored as quickly as possible.” She also said that officials were working on contingency arrangements and future options, including insourcing; that the House would be updated on the McCloud remedy before the end of the month; and that compensation would be considered further. No firm date was set for clearing all cases, and detailed contingency arrangements were not disclosed in the Chamber. No division is recorded.

### Timms review of Personal Independence Payment
Stephen Timms presented the final review of Personal Independence Payment (PIP), which had been co-produced with disabled people, their representative organisations and other experts. The review heard from more than 40,000 people and organisations. It concluded that the existing system was not fit for purpose because assessments did not adequately consider needs, aspirations, barriers or additional costs, handled fluctuating and cumulative conditions poorly, and offered too little scope for personalised support. Its central conclusion was that “the system is no longer fit for purpose and requires a fundamental reset.”

The review recommended that cash should remain central while claimants could choose practical services as part of their support. As an initial step, the Government said they would agree a fast-track route for people with serious, well-evidenced conditions, including a distinct route for people at the end of life, and avoid repeated award reviews where lifelong or degenerative conditions were unlikely to improve. The Government will consider the review alongside Alan Milburn’s review of young people and work, publish a combined White Paper early next year and bring forward legislation afterwards. Helen Whately and other Members questioned costs, eligibility, evidence and the relationship with the Milburn proposals. Timms said the full cost implications and assessments would be set out in the White Paper, and confirmed that PIP is intended to remain available to people in work as well as those out of work. Further engagement with disabled people and their organisations was promised.

### COP31 climate summit
Miatta Fahnbulleh reported that COP31 produced progress in technical negotiations and the action agenda, including work on adaptation, just transition, electrification, methane, forests, oceans and climate finance. However, parties did not reach consensus on the proposed cover decision. The Government declined to support it because, in its view, it did not reaffirm the transition away from fossil fuels or keep 1.5°C within reach: “the substance that was proposed in the final cover decision did not meet the moment.” The UK also cited support for a clean electrification road map, an additional £20 million for coral reefs and marine protection, a climate security envoy, £400 million for the AMPLIFi programme and wider international climate finance.

The Opposition questioned the effect of UK climate policy on energy costs, industrial competitiveness and energy security, while Ministers argued that dependence on global fossil-fuel markets was itself a source of vulnerability and that clean electrification offered economic and security benefits. The Government said it would work through its forthcoming G20 presidency, with international partners and with the Ethiopian presidency ahead of COP32 in Addis Ababa, while remaining within legally binding carbon budgets. On Rosebank, the Minister declined to prejudge the quasi-judicial process but said that any consenting decision would have to meet the climate and environmental test: “there is a climate and environmental test for any consenting decision.” No division is recorded.

### Register of funeral directors and related services
Caroline Dinenage sought leave to introduce a Bill requiring a register of funeral directors, private mortuaries, direct cremation services and certain other people or organisations that transport or hold deceased people. She argued that the funeral sector lacked statutory minimum standards, professional licensing, inspection and dedicated legislation, and referred to the Gosport case involving 46 bodies and the Fuller inquiry’s recommendations for regulation. She presented registration as an initial means of identifying operators and improving transparency: “A register does not mean enforcement, but it does at least mean that people could look up their funeral director.”

The motion was agreed to without a recorded division. The Bill was presented by Dame Caroline Dinenage and the named supporters, read the First time and scheduled for Second Reading on Friday 15 January 2027. The Deputy Speaker informed the House that the Gosport case had been referred to the Court of Appeal under the unduly lenient sentence scheme and was subject to stated sub judice restrictions.

### Armed Forces Bill: Lords amendments
The House considered Lords amendments on military accommodation, the governance of the Reserve Forces and Cadets Associations (RFCAs), armed forces covenant guidance, the service-justice victims’ code, reservist communications and immigration fees for eligible armed forces personnel and their families. The Government accepted Lords amendment 15 on immigration-fee exemptions and said that the relevant regulations would be implemented before the end of September 2027. It opposed Lords amendments 2 and 18 on military accommodation, arguing that single living accommodation required a distinct military standard and that the Elphicke Ross review should inform the approach. The Liberal Democrats and Opposition argued for enforceable minimum standards, citing the review’s description of accommodation as “safe”, “warm”, “clean”, “functional”, “secure” and “private”.

The Government also opposed Lords amendments 11 and 25, defending a single non-departmental public body for the RFCAs while proposing regional councils and national representation. The House divided on the motion to disagree with Lords amendment 2: 299 Ayes to 87 Noes, and on the motion concerning Lords amendments 11 and 25: 296 Ayes to 171 Noes. Lords amendments 1, 3 to 10, 12 to 17 and 19 to 24 were agreed, with Commons financial privilege waived in respect of Lords amendment 15. A committee was appointed to draw up reasons for disagreeing with Lords amendments 2 and 18.

### Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill: Lords amendments
The debate addressed the scope and operation of the proposed cyber-security regime for essential services, including reviews of the regime, foreign-state threats, vendor risk, incident reporting, managed service providers, sovereign capability and artificial-intelligence-related risks. The Government opposed Lords amendment 107, which would have required a comprehensive annual review, arguing that the regime needed time to bed in. It relied instead on a three-year post-implementation review and undertook to table an amendment in the other place requiring a statement within 12 months on further activities that should be specified as essential, with advanced manufacturing, electoral infrastructure and political parties to be considered within 24 months. The Government also committed to publishing the national cyber action plan before Royal Assent.

The Government opposed Lords amendment 108, which would have created a statutory register of foreign powers, favouring case-by-case assessment supported by expert advice and guidance: “A statutory register of foreign powers would upset that balance.” Members also debated whether the provisions on loss of control of an artificial-intelligence agent should instead cover autonomous systems. The Government supported the Lords amendments on vendor risk management, incident reporting and AI-related information gathering, but the House rejected an Opposition amendment to Lords amendment 33 by 93 Ayes to 278 Noes. Lords amendment 108 was disagreed to by 260 Ayes to 98 Noes; Lords amendment 107 was also disagreed to, without division figures being recorded in the transcript. The remaining specified Lords amendments were agreed, with Commons financial privilege waived in respect of Lords amendments 48 to 52 and 54.

### Saltend Chemicals Park and INEOS Acetyls
In an Adjournment debate, Graham Stuart said that three INEOS chemical plants at Saltend had stopped production while all 245 staff remained employed. He argued that the stoppage threatened UK production capacity and could affect the wider chemicals cluster, including Mitsubishi Chemical and Triton Power. He identified gas, used as a feedstock, as the principal competitiveness problem and asked for a timely route to temporary operating support, as well as clarification on whether the £350 million critical chemicals resilience fund could support operating costs as well as capital projects. His central question was: “what is the Government’s route to a timely decision on a temporary operating bridge for INEOS at Saltend?”

Blair McDougall said that the Government recognised the strategic importance of the chemicals sector and were in regular dialogue with INEOS. He confirmed that the company would be considered for eligibility under the critical chemicals resilience fund and that the rules would be published and the scheme opened before the end of the year. He did not commit to operating-cost support from the fund, saying that it was focused on long-term resilience and tilted towards capital expenditure, but said that wider discussions were under way to help restart production. No funding decision was announced. The motion was agreed and the House adjourned.
