## High-Level Summary
The sitting considered e-petition 764553, which called for sentence reductions to be removed where a defendant in a controlling or coercive behaviour case initially pleaded not guilty and later pleaded guilty. Members described the prolonged uncertainty and distress experienced by victims, while also recognising that guilty-plea discounts can avoid trials, reduce delay and spare victims cross-examination. Minister Jake Richards said the Government would examine how discounts operate for this offence, but was not persuaded at present that they should be abolished. He also undertook related follow-up on non-fatal strangulation sentencing, victims’ support, family-court issues and special-measures data; the question was agreed to and the sitting adjourned.

## Detailed Summary
### E-petition 764553: sentence reductions for guilty pleas in controlling or coercive behaviour cases
Irene Campbell, who introduced the debate, said that e-petition 764553 had attracted more than 105,000 signatures and argued that a late guilty plea could prolong a victim’s distress. The petition called for legislation preventing a sentence reduction where a defendant initially pleaded not guilty and later changed the plea. Campbell outlined the existing framework: generally one-third for a plea at the first stage, reducing to one-quarter later and to a maximum of one-tenth on the first day of trial. She also explained that the established rationale is to encourage early pleas, save court resources and spare victims from giving evidence. She said, “The principle of reducing a sentence for a guilty plea is long-standing in England and Wales”.

Liz Jarvis, Matt Bishop, Ian Sollom, Maria Eagle, Connor Naismith, Amanda Martin, Richard Quigley, Jessica Brown-Fuller and Kieran Mullan expressed support for examining, and in several cases changing, the operation of late-plea reductions. They cited the distinctive nature of coercive control and the possibility that prolonged proceedings could extend a perpetrator’s control. Members also raised judicial and court-staff training, better information and support for victims, standard special measures, perpetrator programmes and earlier opportunities to change pleas. They recognised a counter-consideration: a late plea may still spare a victim cross-examination. Matt Bishop said, “even a late plea can spare someone a distressing cross-examination”.

Jake Richards, the Minister, acknowledged the difficulty of balancing incentives for guilty pleas with victims’ interests. He confirmed that the Government would examine the operation of guilty-plea discounts in controlling or coercive behaviour cases, but said, “we are not currently persuaded that the evidence suggests that victims would be best served by a scrapping of discounts just for this particular offence”. He declined to address in this debate whether controlling or coercive behaviour was excluded from the early-release programme. He confirmed that updated statutory guidance on the offence would be published “early next year”, and offered to meet Paris Shears or arrange a meeting with the Victims Minister.

The Minister also said he would raise non-fatal strangulation sentencing with the Sentencing Council and ensure that Maria Eagle’s points on the victims code reached the Victims Minister. He undertook to provide Ian Sollom with a written answer on the use of the special-measures data protocol. He referred to £550 million for victim and witness support services over three years, £13 million for a national centre for violence against women and girls and public protection, and automatic multi-agency public protection arrangements for people convicted of controlling or coercive behaviour and sentenced to 12 months or more from February 2025. The transcript records, “Question put and agreed to”. The House then resolved the e-petition and the sitting was adjourned.
