## High-Level Summary
Westminster Hall considered three items: a Select Committee statement on children in the social care system in England, a Backbench Business debate on vaccination rates in England, and a debate on the national security implications of global biodiversity loss. The Joint Committee on Human Rights’ statement highlighted troubling outcomes for care‑experienced children and recommended legal and policy reforms; Members asked about implementation and practical barriers. The vaccination debate examined falling coverage, access versus hesitancy, data and accountability; the Minister set out targets, funding and service changes, and digital improvements. The nature security debate discussed the Government’s assessment of ecosystem risks to national security; the Minister confirmed cross‑government work, restoration activity and a £400 million UK investment in tropical forest protection. Both debates ended with the House resolving that it had considered the topics; the Select Committee item was for statements and questions only.

## Detailed Summary
### Select Committee statement: Human Rights of Children in the Social Care System (England)
On behalf of the Joint Committee on Human Rights, Peter Swallow outlined findings and recommendations from its report. He said, “the outcomes of children in and around the care system are too often marred by challenge and difficulty, and too often end in tragedy.” He cited a Minister‑commissioned review: “In 2025, the death rate for care leavers aged between 18 and 24 was three to four times higher than that of the general population.” The Committee called for more targeted support, extendable beyond age cut‑offs where needed, and for legal reform including incorporation of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child as “the convention remains unincorporated into domestic law in England.”

Recommendations included a single comprehensive piece of statutory guidance for disabled children’s social care and an “explicit duty to assess where need is identified.” The Committee backed national eligibility criteria, updating the Children Act definition of disability to Equality Act language, clarifying kinship care law, and tackling a “fundamentally broken” placements market alongside monitoring new Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 measures on Ofsted fines, regional care co‑operatives and sibling contact. In questions, Swallow agreed to share the report with relevant persons in Northern Ireland (“I am sure that the Committee can send a copy of the report to all relevant persons in Northern Ireland.”), said “the proof of the pudding will be in the implementation” regarding sibling contact and practical barriers, highlighted proposed complaints and advocacy changes (“they all have an opt-out right to a legal advocate.”), and noted mixed evidence on making ‘care‑experienced’ a protected characteristic (“The evidence was mixed.”). He acknowledged the attendance of the Under‑Secretary of State for Education. No decision was sought; this was a Select Committee statement followed by Member questions.

### Backbench Business: Vaccination Rates in England
Opening the debate, Danny Beales warned that coverage has fallen below the World Health Organisation’s 95% benchmark for all routine childhood programmes, with “only 83.7% of five-year-olds in England had both doses of the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine” and “Tragically, three children died this year from measles—a totally preventable illness.” He said recent data show further declines: “The 6-in-1 vaccine dose three coverage is down, the meningitis B dose three coverage is down, and the rotavirus dose two coverage is also down.” He argued access failures and hesitancy must be tackled together, calling for restored targets, resourcing, outreach, better data linkage and clear accountability.

Members raised misinformation (“We definitely need tougher regulation of social media companies.”), returning to WHO‑level targets (“When will that target be reintroduced?”), delivery via pharmacies and schools, and case data (“England had recorded 970 laboratory-confirmed measles cases this year alone.”). Others highlighted funding and practical barriers in deprived and rural areas, and proposals such as lifetime parental consent for school vaccinations. The Shadow Minister asked about meningitis B recommendations, RSV for older adults, and public information to parents.

Minister Diana R. Johnson stated “the Government have not dropped vaccination targets… 95% for routine pre-school immunisations and 90% for routine school-age immunisations.” She announced higher GP payments “from £10.06 to £12.06” per routine childhood vaccination and revised incentives, pilots with health visitors and expanded use of community pharmacies, and school‑age improvements including digital consent via the Mavis—manage vaccinations in schools—scheme, “used by 72% of school vaccination teams” by September 2026. On data, she said, “Through new digital systems, we are joining those records together.” Beales urged a timeline to regain measles elimination and reach WHO targets. The motion was agreed to without division: “Resolved, That this House has considered vaccination rates in England.”

### Backbench Business: Global Biodiversity, Ecosystems and National Security
Adrian Ramsay introduced the debate on the Government’s nature security assessment, asserting that “Climate breakdown and nature security are the most immediate and severe national security threats of our age.” He criticised limited transparency—“the only document made public is a redacted summary of the assessment, published after a freedom of information request”—and summarised its warnings that “global ecosystem collapse and the loss of biodiversity threaten national security, human life and economic prosperity.” Members discussed food security, nature as critical infrastructure, adaptation, and publication of the full assessment. During the debate, the Government’s plan to increase UK support for tropical forests was noted and later confirmed by the Minister: “today the Government have announced that they intend to invest £400 million in the Tropical Forest Forever facility.”

Minister Jenny Riddell‑Carpenter declined to discuss drafting history but stressed the conclusions: “nature security is national security, and nature stability is global stability.” She said, “Our assessment is the first published national security assessment of its kind in the world.” Actions outlined included launching “the first ever taskforce on the national security and resilience risks of a changing climate and nature loss”, commissioning “a three-year nature security research and development programme”, “investing more than £2.7 billion a year in farming and nature recovery”, and that “We have restored nearly 23,000 hectares of peatland since 2020”. The House resolved, without division, that it had considered the assessment.
