## High-Level Summary
Westminster Hall debated lobbying transparency, flood access for remote communities, disclosure of RFA Sir Galahad inquiry records, driverless-road-vehicle regulation and protection of the Rohingya. Members generally supported greater transparency, accountability and safety, while also emphasising proportionality, privacy, security, legitimate engagement and local needs. Ministers described ongoing reviews, consultations, funding and further engagement, but did not announce immediate policy changes on the principal outstanding issues. Three motions were agreed; the driverless-vehicles debate has no recorded outcome in the transcript, while the Rohingya motion lapsed and the sitting adjourned without the question being put.

## Detailed Summary
### Lobbying transparency and public confidence
Phil Brickell (Bolton West) argued that the statutory lobbying regime covered only a small proportion of lobbying and that departmental transparency releases were fragmented. He called for a single accessible platform linking Government disclosures with the lobbying register, wider coverage of lobbying activity, stronger penalties, disclosure rules for relevant parliamentary actors, tighter rules on Members’ outside employment and changes to House of Lords arrangements. He said the register “covers at most 4% of individuals and entities engaged in lobbying activity”. Lisa Smart supported comprehensive disclosure, including communications beyond meetings; Charlie Dewhirst supported proportionate reform that would not obstruct legitimate representations; Brian Leishman focused on the defence-industry lobby; and Jim Shannon stressed that legitimate constituency and sectoral engagement should not be treated as improper lobbying and that security and burdens on smaller organisations should be considered.

Minister Hamish Falconer said the existing system “is not good enough”. He said the Government were considering the Ethics and Integrity Commission’s recommendations, including replacing the consultant-lobbyist register with a register of all lobbying activity, and would present their response to Parliament “once ready”. He did not give a timetable or make a legislative commitment. The motion was agreed to: “That this House has considered lobbying transparency and public confidence.”

### Floods and emergencies: access to remote locations
Sarah Edwards (Tamworth) argued that rural communities could be disadvantaged by flood funding and population-based assessments, leaving some villages without reliable access to emergency services and essential facilities. She proposed a “one route in and one route out” approach, identifying and protecting at least one usable route into and out of vulnerable settlements, with measures such as raised road sections, drainage and depth gauges. Members also raised satellite communications, local emergency plans, support for farmers providing flood storage, the impact of new development and the need to work with devolved Administrations. Edwards said that a protected route “could have high impact locally for little cost”.

Minister Emma Hardy said local resilience forums were encouraged to develop multi-agency flood plans and that “communities must be at the centre of emergency planning”. She outlined Environment Agency proposals for Tamworth intended to protect approximately 2,600 residential and 360 commercial properties, with the strategic outline case forecasting approximately £900 million in economic benefit. She also discussed regional water councils and offered further engagement, including a possible meeting with Edwards. She did not commit to adopting the one-route system nationally. The motion was agreed to: “Question put and agreed to.”

### RFA Sir Galahad Board of Inquiry: records management and disclosure
Jessica Morden (Newport East) led a cross-party call for earlier disclosure of records from the 1982 RFA Sir Galahad Board of Inquiry. Members, including Iain Duncan Smith, Jo Stevens, Jim Shannon, Chris Evans, Priti Patel, Nia Griffith, Liz Saville-Roberts, Bernard Jenkin, Ian Roome and Stuart Anderson, supported the veterans’ and bereaved families’ requests for witness statements and related records. They raised the proposed closure of some records until 2065, apparent differences from disclosure decisions in other Falklands inquiries, and reports that some transcripts and recordings were missing. Morden quoted a veteran’s assessment that “We’ve got parts of the truth, but we haven’t got the full truth.” Iain Duncan Smith attributed the failures in the operation to “rank incompetence” among those responsible for the plan, while Members repeatedly stated that the Welsh Guards were not to blame.

Minister Louise Jones said that 12 files were held at the National Archives: five were open, five remained closed because of personal data, and two redacted copies of previously closed files had been reinstated in the open record after a May 2024 review. She referred to section 40 of the Freedom of Information Act 2000 and obligations under the Data Protection Act 2018. She acknowledged an apparent discrepancy with other inquiries and said the Government were investigating it, encouraged campaigners to use the independent appeal process for freedom of information requests, and committed to further meetings and answers. She did not announce early release of the remaining records or a new review. She restated that “the Welsh Guards were absolutely not to blame in any way”. The motion was agreed to: “That this House has considered the management and disclosure of records from the RFA Sir Galahad Board of Inquiry.”

### Driverless road vehicles: regulation
Siân Berry (Brighton Pavilion) questioned whether fully autonomous passenger services addressed a clearly defined public need. She raised safety, congestion, empty journeys, accessibility, safeguarding, employment and the suitability of evidence from United States trials for UK roads. She argued that human drivers performed wider safety and assistance functions, and called for independently analysed UK data covering near-misses and malfunctions, high safety requirements and greater local or mayoral control over vehicle numbers and service conditions. The Parliamentary Advisory Council for Transport Safety stated that “simply matching human performance should not be treated as sufficient”. Peter Prinsley and Lee Pitcher highlighted potential benefits for rural isolation, tired drivers, older people and disabled travellers.

Minister Justin Madders said the Government supported innovation but would proceed gradually, with safety, accountability and local needs at the centre. Under the Automated Vehicles Act 2024, the safety principles were intended to ensure that authorised vehicles “achieve a level of safety equivalent to, or higher than, that of careful and competent human drivers”. He said the final statement of safety principles was expected to be laid before Parliament the following year, and that local licensing authorities or franchising bodies would consent to automated passenger services and could set limits on vehicle numbers. He also referred to accessibility, safeguarding, worker concerns and possible new employment. The transcript does not record a question being put or the motion being agreed to.

### Government support for the protection of the rights of the Rohingya
Rushanara Ali (Bethnal Green and Stepney) opened the debate following the International Court of Justice finding on 19 November that Myanmar was responsible for genocide against the Rohingya. Members called for enforcement of the judgment, further sanctions on Myanmar’s military and financial and energy sectors, accountability through international courts, humanitarian support for refugees in Bangladesh, protection for women and children, and safe, voluntary and dignified return. They also raised abuses by the Arakan Army, discriminatory citizenship laws, religious persecution, education and recognised qualifications for Rohingya young people. David Reed, the shadow spokesperson, said the Opposition supported protection for the Rohingya but questioned the evidential basis of the judgment and referred to judicial dissent. Minister Kirsty McNeill responded that “a competent court has established that the threshold has been passed”.

McNeill said the UK supported the judgment, which she described as “final and binding”, had sought a United Nations Security Council discussion, expelled Myanmar’s defence attaché, sanctioned senior military figures and planned legislation to stop the export and supply of aviation fuel to Myanmar. She said the UK had provided more than £457 million since 2017 for refugees and host communities in Bangladesh, more than £115 million in Rakhine state and an additional £5 million in humanitarian assistance. She said returns would be supported only when the Rohingya were safe, and that further measures against armed actors would depend on the evidence. The motion lapsed and the sitting adjourned without the question being put: “Motion lapsed, and sitting adjourned without Question put (Standing Order No. 10(14)).”
