## High-Level Summary
Westminster Hall debated the financial sustainability of higher education, including public and private funding, international recruitment, student support, regional access and institutional risk. The Minister outlined indexed tuition-fee-cap increases, research funding, the Lifelong Learning Entitlement and the international student levy, while the motion lapsed under Standing Order No. 10(6). Members then considered support for rugby league clubs, sentencing for causing death by dangerous or careless driving, and the performance of HCRG Care Group’s community-health services in the south-west; the rugby league and HCRG motions were agreed. The final debate concerned the national scheme of delegation of planning functions, which the Government defended as a means of improving consistency and speed, although the motion lapsed without a question being put.

## Detailed Summary
### Higher Education Funding
Abtisam Mohamed opened the debate by arguing that universities provide economic, civic, public-service and research benefits but face an unstable funding model. She referred to the real-terms erosion of tuition fees, reliance on international student income, staff reductions and regional effects, and asked for a long-term strategy connecting higher education with growth, skills, research and regional development. She opposed using an international student levy to fund maintenance grants, arguing that “any solution to the sector’s funding crisis and support for the most disadvantaged home students should not further destabilise university finances”. Other Members raised redundancies and funding pressures in particular institutions and regions, barriers to participation and living costs, student-finance reform, support for vulnerable providers, protection for a broad range of subjects and a special-administration regime for failing universities. Ian Sollom cited the Office for Students’ evidence that “24 institutions could exit the market within a year”.

Rebecca Smith, responding for the official Opposition, recognised the sector’s economic and social contribution and asked how the Government would support institutions at risk of insolvency, whether Plan 2 loans would change, whether Ministers had met financially uncertain institutions and whether further fee increases would be ruled out. Georgia Gould said providers remained independent but acknowledged the effects of frozen fees. She said indexed fee-cap increases would provide “£6 billion-worth of safeguarded indexed increase in income”, and outlined £86 billion of planned research and development investment from 2026-27 to 2029-30, the Lifelong Learning Entitlement from January 2027 and a £925 international student levy per student per year from 2028-29, with an allowance of 220 students per provider. She also said that “we will publish an updated impact assessment ahead of the Finance Bill”. Targeted maintenance grants of up to £1,000 a year were due from 2028-29, and interest rates on Plan 2 and Plan 3 loans were capped at 6% for 2026-27. The motion lapsed under Standing Order No. 10(6).

### Government Support for Rugby League Clubs
Jon Trickett argued that rugby league is an important community institution, particularly in former mining and working-class areas, but receives insufficient visibility, broadcasting access and public support compared with rugby union. He referred to the cost of accessing televised matches, the number of university rugby-league sides and the difference in Covid support. He focused on Featherstone Rovers, which had entered administration, and described efforts involving the Rugby Football League, Wakefield Council and the Featherstone Pride in Place board to acquire the stadium and associated assets and revive the club. He asked the Government to consider funding, support the local partnership and review the availability of major rugby-league matches on free-to-air television, saying: “The problem for rugby league is that the feedstock has been cut off”.

Sports Minister Stephanie Peacock described the community and social benefits of rugby league. She said Wakefield Council had proposed £500,000 of emergency funding, matched by the RFL Resilience Fund, with the aim of returning the team to the field by 2027. She said she supported local consideration of Pride in Place funding and would raise it with the relevant Minister, noted Sport England’s commitment of £15.7 million to rugby league up to 2029, and offered to discuss the RFL’s funding bid without making a spending commitment. On broadcasting, she said the Challenge Cup final remained protected on free-to-air television and acknowledged concerns about the limited BBC coverage of the World Cup. She also referred to £330,000 of investment in Rugby League Cares. The question was put and agreed.

### Causing Death by Dangerous or Careless Driving: Sentencing
Alex Barros-Curtis opened the debate with the case of Keely Morgan, a 15-year-old killed in Cardiff, and argued that some sentences for fatal driving offences did not reflect the harm caused or provide sufficient public confidence and deterrence. Members including Tessa Munt, Siobhain McDonagh, Jim Shannon, Josh Dean and Ben Maguire described other cases and called for stronger custodial penalties, licence suspension during investigations, longer or permanent disqualification, graduated driving licences, improved investigation and prevention, and changes to the treatment of deaths caused by knowingly unlicensed driving. Members also supported considering the addition of causing death by careless driving to the unduly lenient sentence scheme. Barros-Curtis summarised his position by saying: “The debate is not about revenge; it is about justice, accountability and prevention”.

Justice Minister Jake Richards distinguished the legal thresholds for dangerous and careless driving and said sentencing was a matter for the independent courts, while acknowledging that victims’ families often felt let down. He said the Government were discussing driving disqualification with the Department for Transport and that he had asked the Sentencing Council to look again at the use of bans. He committed to considering whether causing death by careless driving should be added to the unduly lenient sentence scheme and agreed to write to the Law Commission about that specific issue. He said graduated licensing was a Department for Transport matter, undertook to seek written responses on road-safety issues outside his portfolio and offered to meet campaigners. He also acknowledged: “Too often, families feel let down by the justice system”. The question was agreed and the motion was resolved.

### HCRG Care Group: Performance in the South-west
Sarah Gibson raised concerns about HCRG Care Group’s community-health contract covering Bath and North East Somerset, Swindon and Wiltshire. She referred to the single point of access, telephone delays, missed callbacks, referral problems, staffing pressures, increased caseloads, reduced administrative support and reported patient-safety concerns involving palliative care, wound care, post-operative physiotherapy and wheelchair services. She questioned whether the oversight arrangements provided sufficiently independent accountability and asked for comparable pre- and post-transformation performance data, broken down locally where possible, and for engagement with the Care Quality Commission. She said: “Patients do not really care how many committees or meetings are called; they want answers”.

Health Minister Karin Smyth said community services were central to the Government’s neighbourhood-health plans and referred to a target that, by 2028-29, at least 80% of community-health activity should take place within 18 weeks. She confirmed that the integrated care board was responsible for managing the HCRG contract and was increasing oversight of delays, callbacks and referrals, with engagement involving patients, carers, staff, local communities, Members of Parliament and Healthwatch. She said she had been assured that there were no plans to reduce or withdraw hospital-at-home capacity, while acknowledging that the service was under review; Sarah Gibson disputed that statement, saying: “It is just that that point is not true”. The Minister outlined changes to integrated care board accountability from 2026-27, said she would write about available performance data and the Care Quality Commission, and referred to the Health Bill’s proposed changes to local accountability. The question was put and agreed.

### National Scheme of Delegation of Planning Functions
Wendy Morton and other Members argued that the national scheme of delegation would reduce elected councillors’ ability to bring applications before planning committees and could weaken local scrutiny of green-belt and speculative development, neighbourhood plans, houses in multiple occupation and infrastructure provision. Members questioned the evidence that committees caused delay, noting that most applications were already decided by officers, and sought clearer referral rules, published reasons for refusing referrals, safeguards for neighbourhood plans and article 4 directions, protection for planning officers, public guidance and an assessment of the scheme’s impact. Olly Glover, Paul Holmes, Victoria Collins, Vikki Slade and John Milne argued that delays were more closely associated with planning-department capacity, construction and infrastructure constraints than with committees. Milne said: “Faster planning decisions should come from having properly resourced planning departments”. Members referred to the scheme taking effect on Saturday; the responding Minister said it would come into force on Sunday.

Sally Jameson, responding for the Government, defended the scheme as a way to create consistency, enable officers to determine smaller applications more quickly and allow committees to focus on larger and more complex proposals. She said consultation arrangements for individual applications would not change, and that records of referral decisions, outcomes and reasons should be reported to planning committees and published on council websites. She said local plans should provide the main opportunity for democratic input and undertook to provide written information on houses in multiple occupation, websites and other unanswered points. She also said the regulations required a review within two years of coming into force and would pass on the request for an earlier or interim assessment. The motion lapsed and the sitting adjourned without a question being put under Standing Order No. 10(14).
