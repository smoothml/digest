## High-Level Summary
The Westminster Hall debate considered e-petition 757233, which opposes the proposed ban on social media access for under-16s. Members broadly agreed that children face significant online harms and that social media companies should be subject to stronger duties, but differed over whether a platform-based ban, a functionality- or harms-based approach, or a combination would be most effective. Concerns included age assurance, privacy, circumvention, support networks, educational access, online gaming and the risk of a cliff edge at 16. The Minister, Ian Murray, confirmed that the first regulations would be laid before the Christmas recess, with restrictions expected to take effect in late spring; the motion was agreed to without a division.

## Detailed Summary
### Petition and case for immediate restrictions
Tony Vaughan (Folkestone and Hythe), introducing the debate, said that e-petition 757233 had 217,682 signatories and acknowledged that its initiator, Leo Rhodes, opposed a ban because social media could provide friendship, community and support. Vaughan nevertheless argued that current services exposed children to addictive design and harmful content. He supported an immediate ban as a precaution while the Government developed a route for safer services, saying, "It is right that the Government impose a circuit-breaker ban now". He also argued that any off-ramp should require companies to demonstrate that their services were safe, with the burden of proof shifted towards them; the timing and detailed mechanism of that off-ramp were not settled in the transcript.

The debate also referred to Ofcom's finding that children's exposure to harmful content had not significantly reduced after the Online Safety Act's child-safety duties came into force. Vaughan said the report found that "there had been no significant change in the proportion of children encountering harmful content", alongside reported exposure to self-harm, eating-disorder and bullying content.

### Arguments against a blanket platform ban
Sam Carling, Josh Dean, Cat Eccles and Anna Sabine argued that social media could provide connection, civic engagement, educational material and support, particularly for LGBTQ+ young people, people in rural communities and those awaiting traditional services. Carling said a blanket ban could drive children towards less regulated services and advocated restricting harmful functions instead: "I support a functionality-based approach". Dean similarly said that policy should focus on "the features, the business models and the failures of regulation" rather than placing the burden of companies' failures on young people.

Members questioned whether a named-platform model could keep pace with changing services and whether it would preserve access to educational content such as YouTube. They also raised the risks of a sharp transition at 16, privacy concerns around age checks and the possibility that children would move to gaming, messaging or fringe platforms. No alternative motion was put. Members requested flexible, regularly reviewed and harms-based regulation, but the transcript does not record a specific commitment on those points beyond the Government's wider proposals.

### Arguments supporting the ban and wider safeguards
Laura Kyrke-Smith and Fred Thomas supported the proposed restrictions, while Scott Arthur supported action to reduce children's exposure to harmful content but retained reservations about whether the ban should be complete. Speakers cited harmful content, addictive design, bullying, mental-health risks and the difficulty many parents faced in managing access individually. Kyrke-Smith argued that a common age limit could reduce pressure on families and establish a shared norm: "It ensures that all children have the same protections, regardless of their background or family situation". Thomas rejected the argument that exposure before 16 necessarily prepares children for later use, saying, "exposure is the same thing as preparation—it is not".

Supporters also stressed that restrictions would need to sit alongside measures addressing addictive features, artificial-intelligence chatbots, stranger contact, location sharing, age-inappropriate material and online gaming. Arthur said that restrictions must not allow companies to avoid their wider responsibilities: "A ban cannot be used as an excuse by companies not to comply with their responsibilities". Speakers also called for a transition that avoided a cliff edge when restrictions took effect.

### Ministerial plans: regulations, scope and implementation
Ian Murray, responding for the Government, said that the Online Safety Act formed part of a wider package and that companies would be required to use highly effective age assurance backed by Ofcom's enforcement powers. He confirmed that the first regulations would be laid before Parliament before the Christmas recess and that restrictions would take effect in late spring: "The first regulations will be laid before Parliament before the start of the Christmas recess". The Government's proposed approach was described as an Australia-plus model, extending some feature and function restrictions to gaming and other services, including restrictions on livestreaming and communication with strangers. Default protections for 16- and 17-year-olds would include restrictions on autoplay and personalised feeds, overnight curfews and muted push notifications.

Murray said that the regulations would not name platforms directly; Ofcom would apply the criteria and determine which services were in scope. He indicated that an off-ramp was intended for services that redesigned themselves to comply with the rules, but later said that its timing was not settled and that it would probably be developed alongside the features-and-functions measures after spring: "We don't quite know yet, but probably not". The Government were still considering whether age assurance should operate at app-store, device or individual-platform level. Murray also referred to digital resilience, media literacy, youth provision and offline alternatives as part of the wider response, including investment in youth facilities and enrichment schemes.

### Outcome of the debate
In his winding-up speech, Tony Vaughan said there was broad agreement that the status quo was unacceptable and that companies should address harmful and addictive features. He also supported a possible safe-product off-ramp, summarising the balance between the petitioners' concerns and the case for immediate action: "I support a ban, but with a possible off-ramp where, if social media companies can show that their products are safe, we should allow children to access them".

The question was put and agreed to without a recorded division. The House resolved that it had considered e-petition 757233, and the sitting was adjourned.
