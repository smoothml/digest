## High-Level Summary
Westminster Hall considered manufacturing skills, long-duration electricity storage, Heathrow airport expansion, statutory paid employment leave for kinship carers and House of Lords reform. Members discussed skills shortages, regional development, infrastructure, community benefits, environmental effects and constitutional arrangements, with differing views on the appropriate policy responses. Ministers described existing programmes and the relevant consultation, funding and review processes, while indicating that some matters would be considered further or addressed in later correspondence. The transcript records each motion as agreed to; the sitting adjourned after the House of Lords reform debate.

## Detailed Summary
### Manufacturing skills
Richard Quigley (Isle of Wight West) secured the debate and argued that advanced manufacturing required stronger routes into apprenticeships, vocational education and technical training, including for people who did not take the university route. Members including Catherine Atkinson, Luke Myer, Connor Naismith, Baggy Shanker, Jim Shannon, Sureena Brackenridge, Gideon Amos, Lee Pitcher, Allison Gardner, Perran Moon and others raised skills shortages, ageing workforces, reduced manufacturing apprenticeship starts, college funding, regional provision and the difficulty smaller manufacturers faced in training apprentices. Quigley summarised the case for investment in skills by saying that “investing in the skills that power it is what turns ambition into long-term economic success”.

Lilian Greenwood, responding for the Government, described investment of more than £1 billion in sector skills packages, including £182 million for engineering. She referred to vocational GCSEs, T-levels, foundation routes, apprenticeship units and technical excellence colleges. She said apprenticeship training would be fully funded for eligible apprentices under 25, with smaller employers receiving a £2,000 hiring payment when recruiting an apprentice under 25. She also said local and strategic authorities would help shape skills systems, and that the Skills Minister could meet Members about specific issues. The motion was agreed to: “That this House has considered manufacturing skills.”

### Long-duration electricity storage systems
Angus MacDonald argued that pumped-storage hydropower could help the Highlands store renewable electricity, reduce constraints on the electricity system and bring investment to the region. He referred to three Scottish sites provisionally selected by Ofgem, together representing 4 gigawatts of capacity, and called for local employment, supply-chain participation, housing and continuing community benefits. He said, “When we ask communities to host major infrastructure, it should be beneficial to them.” Jim Shannon raised a proposed storage project in Northern Ireland; other Members discussed tidal-range, geothermal and smaller storage technologies such as sand batteries.

Energy Minister Michael Shanks supported considering a range of technologies and said long-duration storage was needed to use renewable electricity when required, reduce reliance on gas and lower system costs. He said Ofgem had provisionally selected 16 projects under the cap-and-floor scheme, including three Scottish pumped-storage projects, with final decisions due by the end of the year. He cited projected construction employment and community funding at specific sites, and said the Government were considering community benefits and shared ownership while balancing them against consumer costs. He also described discussions with the Scottish Government about roads and other local infrastructure. The motion was agreed to, and the sitting was suspended: “Ofgem will announce its final decision on those projects by the end of the year.”

### Heathrow airport expansion
Fleur Anderson (Putney) opened the debate by opposing a third runway and arguing that the economic case had weakened since 2015. She and other opponents, including John McDonnell, Will Forster, Danny Beales, Munira Wilson and Sarah Olney, raised concerns about noise, air quality, carbon emissions, displaced jobs, surface-transport funding, the effect on regional airports and the impact on communities near Heathrow. Supporters, including Jim Shannon and Richard Holden, argued that Heathrow’s capacity constraints limited UK growth and regional connectivity, particularly with Northern Ireland, and cited potential jobs, investment and supply-chain benefits. Anderson said, “The projected economic gains are smaller than promised.”

Simon Lightwood, responding as Minister for Local Transport rather than Aviation Minister, said the Government were considering the responses to the draft Heathrow Expansion National Policy Statement (HENPS), while parliamentary scrutiny continued. More than 27,000 consultation responses had been received. He said any finalised HENPS would require parliamentary consideration, including a debate and vote, before acquiring legal effect, and that it would not itself grant development consent. He set out the four tests concerning economic growth, climate change, air quality and noise, including the proposed scheduled night-flight ban, and said any expansion would require a credible surface-access strategy and private financing. The Government would consider the consultation responses and Transport Committee recommendations before setting out next steps. The motion was agreed to; no division is recorded in the transcript.

### Kinship carers: statutory paid employment leave
Melanie Onn (Great Grimsby and Cleethorpes) called for a statutory right to paid employment leave for kinship carers. She argued that children could arrive with little notice and might have experienced trauma or have additional needs, while carers could otherwise face job loss, financial insecurity and long-term detachment from work. Members including Jim Shannon, Perran Moon, Munira Wilson, Alistair Strathern, Mark Sewards, Ian Lavery, Gareth Snell and Julie Minns supported greater protection and compared the position of kinship carers with that of foster, adoptive and other working parents. Onn said, “eight in 10 kinship carers who leave their work never return to paid employment”. She also cited analysis that paid kinship leave could return more than £2 to the economy for every £1 invested.

Kate Dearden, responding for the Government, recognised the emotional, practical and financial pressures on kinship carers and said the issue was being considered across employment, welfare, education and social-care policy. She confirmed that the parental leave and pay review, launched in July 2025, was examining the needs of working families who did not qualify for existing leave and pay entitlements, including kinship carers. The review is due to conclude in early 2027 with findings setting out next steps for implementing any reforms. Dearden said she could not pre-empt its recommendations but would keep Members informed and committed that the Government were “moving at pace”. The motion was agreed to; no division is recorded in the transcript.

### House of Lords reform
Simon Opher (Stroud) argued that the House of Lords lacked democratic legitimacy, was too large and unrepresentative, and required reform of appointments, participation, transparency and regional representation. Members including Phil Brickell, Andrew George, Kim Leadbeater, Ellie Chowns, John Slinger and Lisa Smart supported substantial reform, with proposals including an elected or partly elected second Chamber, a citizens’ assembly, expert members and citizen senators. Andrew Rosindell argued for retaining a non-elected Chamber focused on expertise, experience and continuity, warning that an elected second Chamber could rival the Commons and create deadlock. The debate also covered lobbying, hereditary membership, bishops, peerages, attendance and the Lords’ handling of the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill. Opher said, “We need experts to look at how our democracy works and to devise a better way of doing it.”

Mark Ferguson, responding for the Government, described a staged approach. He said legislation removing the right of hereditary peers to sit and vote had passed; the Government were considering a retirement age and participation requirement following the Retirement and Participation Committee’s report; and a later stage would consult on replacing the Lords with an alternative second Chamber representing the nations and regions. He said the Government would respond substantively to the Committee’s recommendations later in the year, while further details on legislation to remove peerages would be announced in due course. The motion was agreed to and the sitting adjourned: “That is why the Government are focused on conducting a staged approach to House of Lords reform.”
