## High-Level Summary
Westminster Hall considered five topics: the constitutional role of MPs, domestic abuse of older people, a second Scottish independence referendum, railway infrastructure in the South West, and Government support for upland farming. Members debated whether MPs should have a formal job description and greater local powers; the Minister instead defended flexibility while setting out routes for MPs to engage with local accountability arrangements. The Government opposed a second Scottish independence referendum and restated that position, while Members sought a legal route to hold one. Ministers also addressed support for older victims of domestic abuse, rural rail schemes and the transition between upland farming-support agreements. All five motions were agreed to without a recorded division in the transcript, and the sitting was adjourned after the upland-farming debate.

## Detailed Summary
### Constitutional role of MPs
John Slinger (Member for Rugby), who moved the motion, argued that the role of an MP was ill-defined, under-resourced and insufficiently empowered, creating a gap between public expectations and MPs’ formal authority. He cited the absence of a statutory description: “there is no statutory job description for MPs and so how they each carry out their role may vary.” His proposals included requiring public bodies to consult MPs on major changes, creating a central fund for local infrastructure and community projects, allocating more time to private Members’ Bills, and introducing professional development. Peter Bedford prioritised making and scrutinising legislation; Christopher Vince highlighted constituency casework and respectful disagreement; Jim Shannon stressed representation and equal constitutional authority for Northern Ireland MPs; and Rachael Maskell called for stronger scrutiny of mayors, agencies and services.

Richard Burgon and Phil Brickell supported restrictions on highly paid outside employment, while Lisa Smart distinguished professional registrations from paid advocacy and proposed conduct, lobbying and electoral reforms. Mike Wood opposed a statutory job description, arguing that it would turn a flexible constitutional office into a checklist and that the electorate should judge MPs. The Minister, Mark Ferguson, likewise defended flexibility but said that the Government had committed to defined routes for MPs to engage with local accountability arrangements, including submitting questions to local scrutiny committees and publicising constituents’ petitions: “This will include the ability to submit questions to local scrutiny committees, which hold mayors to account, and to publicise and raise the profile of petitions on behalf of constituents.” No further Government action on the wider proposals was announced. John Slinger called for public education about the role of MPs, and the question was agreed to: “That this House has considered the constitutional role of hon. Members.”

### Domestic abuse of older people
Sonia Kumar (Member for Dudley) raised the under-reporting of domestic abuse in later life and the barriers faced by older victims, including isolation, dependency, economic abuse, caring responsibilities and abuse by adult children or other relatives. She illustrated the issue through Tina’s account of prolonged abuse, quoting: “I thought it was normal.” Kumar called for older victims to be reflected in wider domestic-abuse and violence-against-women-and-girls work, for specialist and age-appropriate services, for training for frontline professionals, and for improved data collection. Members also discussed the difficulty of leaving long-term abusive relationships and the effect of financial ties to perpetrators.

Satvir Kaur, responding for the Government, referred to funding for Hourglass and advocacy services, training for frontline professionals, accommodation support, the Steps to Safety initiative and safeguarding duties under the Care Act 2014. She said that domestic abuse protection orders would be rolled out in England and Wales “as of tomorrow” and that further work on data about older victims was expected to be published in the coming weeks: “we are hoping to publish in the coming weeks.” The Government did not expressly accept each of Kumar’s specific recommendations. The question was agreed to and the sitting was suspended.

### Second Scottish independence referendum
Seamus Logan (Member for Aberdeenshire North and Moray East) argued that Scotland should have a legal route to hold a further independence referendum. He presented this as a democratic right and criticised what he described as broken or changing promises concerning Brexit, public spending and the economy. Chris Law called for legislation amending the Scotland Act 1998 to set out conditions for a referendum, arguing that reliance on a section 30 order placed the decision in the hands of the Prime Minister. He said: “The Northern Ireland Act 1998 contains provision for a referendum on leaving the United Kingdom, but no equivalent provision exists for Scotland.” Pete Wishart called for a process to resolve the continuing constitutional impasse, while other SNP Members argued that public opinion and political circumstances had changed since 2014.

Members opposing a further referendum, including Imogen Walker, John Lamont, Lillian Jones, Douglas Lumsden, Kenneth Stevenson, David Mundell and Jim Shannon, said that the 2014 result should be respected and that attention should instead focus on health, education, infrastructure, the economy and public services. Susan Murray supported a federal United Kingdom and argued that another referendum would perpetuate division. Minister Melanie Ward stated the Government’s position: “this Government do not support Scottish independence or another referendum.” She said that there was no current consensus comparable with that in 2014, referred to additional funding for Scotland, and supported further devolution and local empowerment. Seamus Logan said in his closing remarks that the Minister had not answered his question about a legal route. No legal route or change in policy was announced, and the question was agreed to without a recorded division in the transcript.

### Railway infrastructure in the South West
Sarah Dyke (Member for Glastonbury and Somerton) called for improved rural rail connectivity, including a station at Somerton and Langport, better accessibility, resilience works on the West of England line and fairer treatment for passengers facing reduced services. She said that the restoring your railway fund had been scrapped before her constituency’s proposal was assessed and asked what route and funding would now be available. She also sought a duty for Great British Railways to restore connectivity and increase passenger numbers in rural and underserved communities, and asked for a meeting with local campaigners and Somerset Council. A constituent’s question captured her argument: “If it can be done in Bristol, then why not here?”

Justin Madders, responding for the Government, said that local or combined authorities were best placed to develop and take forward business cases for new stations. He offered to ask the Rail Minister to meet Sarah Dyke and the local authority, and said: “as a Department we will work with her and the local authority to ensure that it can put the best case forward.” He also referred to investment in railway assets and climate resilience, and said that Great British Railways was intended to bring tracks, trains and stations together under public ownership. He undertook to pass on a request concerning Bodmin Parkway to the Rail Minister and to seek a response on the impact of work at Old Oak Common. The question was agreed to and the sitting was suspended.

### Government support for upland farming
Ben Maguire (Member for North Cornwall) and other Members argued that upland farms contribute to food production, landscape management, biodiversity, carbon storage, water management and wildfire prevention, while facing lower yields, higher costs and uncertainty over environmental land-management schemes. They called for improved payment rates, access for common land, more flexible locally designed schemes, support for peatland and wildfire management, and arrangements to prevent expiring higher-level stewardship agreements from creating a funding gap. Rebecca Smith and Tim Farron particularly emphasised the risk of a transition cliff edge; Robbie Moore also raised the exclusion of commoners and restrictions on controlled heather burning. Members argued that food production and environmental restoration should be complementary. Maguire said that “food security and environmental protections should go hand in hand and not be pitted against each other”.

Jenny Riddell-Carpenter, standing in for the Under-Secretary of State for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs, said that payment rates for moorland actions had increased and that Natural England was prioritising farmers whose agreements were approaching expiry. She said that the Government would not allow commons in existing Countryside Stewardship Higher Tier or higher-level stewardship agreements to fall outside the arrangements without a mechanism for transition, and that access to schemes for common land was being improved through a trial of a more manual application process: “we are trialling access to the countryside stewardship higher tier with farmers on common land, including our upland commons”. She also said that the relevant Minister would write to Members on specific points raised in the debate. She did not announce the requested extension of all higher-level stewardship agreements to December 2028 or confirm that no farmer would face a funding gap. Ben Maguire welcomed the cross-party support and again sought confirmation that there would be no cliff edge. The question was agreed to and the sitting was adjourned.
