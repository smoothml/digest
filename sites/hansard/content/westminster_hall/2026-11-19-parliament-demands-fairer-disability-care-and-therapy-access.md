## High-Level Summary
The day comprised two Backbench Business debates on disability and public services. The first considered support for NHS patients with learning disabilities, including health inequalities, reasonable adjustments, staff training, specialist nursing, family involvement and the use of restrictive practices. The second examined unequal access to therapy for disabled children, including waiting times, regional variation, workforce pressures, travel and the costs borne by families. Both motions were agreed after the debates; the transcript records no division.

## Detailed Summary
### NHS patients with learning disabilities
Toby Perkins (Chesterfield) opened the debate by describing concerns about the treatment of patients with learning disabilities, including the failure in some cases to recognise communication needs and the supportive role of family carers. Members linked these concerns to premature mortality and avoidable deaths. Perkins cited a report finding that “39% of deaths of people with learning disabilities and autism were classed as avoidable in 2023”, while Helen Morgan (North Shropshire) referred to adults with learning disabilities dying a median “19 years earlier than everyone else”. Jim Shannon (Strangford), Jennifer Craft (Thurrock), Morgan and Neil Shastri-Hurst (Solihull West and Shirley) discussed staff training, learning disability nurses, reasonable adjustments, person-centred care, annual health checks and national accountability. Other issues included diagnostic overshadowing, inappropriate restraint, decisions not to attempt resuscitation, the detention of people with learning disabilities or autism in mental health hospitals, and the involvement of families, carers or advocates. Craft said, “Allowing longer times for appointments should be standard”, and Perkins called for hospital policies allowing carers to stay where their presence would materially improve care.

James Frith, Minister for Health Innovation, said that the health inequalities experienced by people with learning disabilities were unacceptable. He described blanket “do not resuscitate” decisions as “totally unacceptable” and said that treatment and care should be decided individually, in consultation with families, carers or advocates. He confirmed that learning disability nurses would be included in the 10-year workforce plan, which the Government said would be published in the new year. He also referred to the rollout of the reasonable adjustment digital flag and said that the Government intended to publish a new patient-level dataset annually to inform local and national action. After Shannon raised the Muckamore Abbey hospital inquiry, Frith said he would study the report and ensure that the recommendation was raised with colleagues. The Government also outlined reforms concerning care and treatment reviews, community support and expectations on integrated care boards. Perkins said that Members would monitor delivery. The question was agreed to and the House resolved the motion.

### Disabled children: access to therapy
Daniel Francis (Bexleyheath and Crayford) introduced the debate by describing unequal access to physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy, mental health support and specialist rehabilitation for disabled children. Members discussed long waits, locally commissioned and variable provision, workforce shortages, travel—particularly for families in Northern Ireland—and the cost of private therapy. Francis said, “Too often, access to support depends not on the needs of the child but on a postcode lottery”. Gregory Stafford (Farnham and Bordon), the Opposition spokesperson, said that access too often depended on “luck, not need”. He questioned the pace, funding and accountability of the proposed special educational needs and disabilities (SEND) reforms, sought publication of waiting lists by integrated care board, and asked for assurances that existing Education, Health and Care Plan protections would not be weakened. Other Members called for national standards, better transition from paediatric to adult services, improved workforce planning, clearer communication, better broadband connectivity and support based on clinical need rather than family income. Francis quoted a parent’s view that “a child’s quality of life should not be determined by their parents’ finances”.

Karin Smyth, Minister for Health, said that children’s community therapies were commissioned locally by integrated care boards, but that local responsibility should be accompanied by clear expectations and accountability. She referred to NHS England’s 2025 commissioning framework for children and young people with cerebral palsy, which includes a named care co-ordinator or key worker and early access to therapy. She also cited £15 million of investment in speech and language therapy advanced practitioners and related support, and said that NHS England was reviewing specialist paediatric neurorehabilitation provision, with the review to be published in due course. Smyth confirmed that the NHS workforce plan would be published in the new year, with allied health professionals a key focus. On SEND, she said that the Government would set out next steps following the public consultation, including more joined-up support across education, health and social care. Francis asked that the transition from paediatric to adult services, including the cliff edge at 18, be addressed. He concluded by calling for stronger cross-departmental working and improved local delivery. The question was agreed to and the House resolved the motion.
