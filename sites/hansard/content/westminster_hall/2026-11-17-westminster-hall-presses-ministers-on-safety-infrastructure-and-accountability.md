## High-Level Summary
Westminster Hall considered the performance of the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), infrastructure and proposed housing development in Faversham and Mid Kent, the economic impact of hostile-state activity, prisons and sentencing in Wales, and the Department for Work and Pensions’ Synergy contract. Members raised concerns about patient safety, transparency, regulatory independence, water capacity, cyber-resilience, devolved justice, outsourcing and accountability. Ministers outlined existing measures and undertook some written or meeting follow-up, but several requests—including redress, reviews and a standalone estimate of hostile-state costs—remained unresolved in the transcript. The housing, hostile-state activity, Wales prisons and Synergy motions were agreed, while the MHRA motion lapsed under Standing Order No. 10(6).

## Detailed Summary
### Performance of the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency
Esther McVey, with Graham Stringer as a co-sponsor, argued that concerns about medicines and medical-device regulation had not been resolved. Members discussed Primodos, sodium valproate, surgical and other mesh, breast implants, metal hip replacements, puberty blockers, covid-19 vaccines and antidepressants. They raised concerns about transparency, post-marketing surveillance, the Yellow Card scheme, patient involvement and conflicts of interest. McVey described a constituent who became ill and died after receiving a Moderna booster, and said that the family’s complaint remained unanswered: “To date, the family has still not had a reply to the complaint.” Stringer questioned whether the MHRA could be reformed and proposed a Government investigation into its future role: “I have come to the conclusion that there should be a serious investigation and assessment of the MHRA by the Government.” Other Members called for mandatory reporting, stronger patient representation, implementation of the Cumberlege recommendations and redress. Simon Opher focused on antidepressant risks, withdrawal and informed consent; Anna Sabine raised access to new treatments and cannabis-based medicines for severe epilepsy; and Jim Shannon raised medicine shortages, particularly in Northern Ireland.

James Frith, Minister for Health Innovation, cited improvements in the MHRA’s operational performance while acknowledging the need to rebuild trust. He said regulatory fees were intended to recover costs rather than generate profit, that 99% of national medicines licence applications and 97% of clinical trials and investigations had been assessed within target, and that the MHRA assessed every Yellow Card report involving a suspected adverse reaction with a fatal outcome. He said there was limited evidence that mandatory reporting by healthcare professionals would improve detection, while adding that “the patient is not in the loop.” He committed to write to Dr Caroline Johnson about vaccine damage payments, said that new scientific information on Primodos would be considered, and agreed to meet McVey. The motion lapsed under Standing Order No. 10(6): “Motion lapsed (Standing Order No. 10(6)).”

### Infrastructure and proposed housing development in Faversham and Mid Kent
Helen Whately argued that more than 20,000 homes were in the pipeline in Faversham and Mid Kent despite water shortages and pressure on roads, sewage, healthcare and schools. She focused on the proposed 8,400-home Highsted Park development, the 5,000-home Heathlands proposal and other schemes, and argued that infrastructure should be delivered before or alongside housing. She asked whether water companies should become statutory planning consultees and sought assurance that development would not proceed without adequate water, saying: “there is not enough water in my constituency for existing residents.” She also asked that a former Housing Secretary’s statement about not building homes without water infrastructure be taken into account when Highsted Park was decided.

Jim McMahon said he could not comment on individual planning applications, but accepted that development and infrastructure should be considered together. He referred to the new National Planning Policy Framework, early engagement between planning authorities and water companies, the water delivery taskforce and Government action concerning South East Water. He did not give a specific assurance about the developments in Whately’s constituency, but said: “New homes cannot be considered in isolation from the infrastructure that makes communities work.” The Government reaffirmed their target of delivering 1.5 million homes during the Parliament. The question was put and agreed to.

### Impact of hostile-state activity on the economy
Graeme Downie argued that hostile-state activity imposed an unmeasured economic burden, which he termed the “Putin tax”. His report estimated that it cost the UK economy £2 billion to £2.5 billion a year, while a Moody’s model suggested a roughly one-in-ten annual chance of a Russian state-backed cyber-attack costing UK businesses at least £4 billion. Members discussed ransomware, attacks affecting Royal Mail and NHS suppliers, threats to undersea cables and satellites, industrial espionage, foreign interference, school bomb-threat hoaxes in Northern Ireland, transnational repression, energy dependence, artificial intelligence and quantum computing. Downie called for the Treasury to lead an annual assessment and said: “The official figure for what hostile states cost Britain should come from the Treasury.”

Lucy Rigby, Economic Secretary, outlined measures on cyber and infrastructure resilience, sanctions, illicit finance and the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill. She said the National Cyber Security Centre already published annual assessments and that the Government’s cyber-security breaches survey included economic costs. However, she said that a standalone Government estimate was not currently planned for national-security reasons, describing the task as follows: “quantifying the full economic impact of hostile-state activity is, as my hon. Friend will appreciate, inherently complex.” She agreed to consider whether incident-level cost ranges could be published where security permitted, and referred to the Be Ready campaign, funding for AI-enabled cyber risks and the migration to post-quantum cryptography. The question was put and agreed to, and the motion was resolved.

### Prisons and sentencing in Wales
Liz Saville-Roberts discussed the Welsh Affairs Committee report and Cardiff University’s annual fact file on prisons and sentencing in Wales. She raised court delays, racial disparities, short custodial sentences for women, staffing pressures, prison health funding, Welsh-language provision, maintenance backlogs and post-release housing. She called for a royal commission on prisons and the wider criminal justice system, sought reconsideration of the proposed residential women’s centre in Swansea, and asked whether justice should be devolved to Wales. She highlighted the interaction between reserved criminal justice policy and devolved health and housing responsibilities, saying: “This is another example of where the jagged edge does damage.”

Catherine McKinnell said the Government were implementing the Sentencing Act 2026, investing £700 million in probation, expanding prison capacity and conducting an independent review led by Amber Rudd. She said Swansea magistrates court would become an intensive supervision court and that £1.9 million would support women’s diversion services in Wales. The Government would continue considering options for a residential women’s centre, but she gave no timetable for devolving justice, saying: “Conversations are ongoing between her Government and the UK Government here.” She said the Criminal Justice Board for Wales would discuss imprisonment rates in December and described continuing co-operation with the Welsh Government on health, housing, equality and rehabilitation. The question was put and agreed to.

### Department for Work and Pensions’ procurement of the Synergy contract
Andy McDonald and other Members questioned the decision to award Capita a business-process services contract covering payroll, human resources, finance and purchasing for more than 250,000 civil servants, in light of problems with Capita’s administration of civil service pensions. They cited backlogs, delayed pension payments and hardship, and called for a review of outsourcing, published safeguards, contingency arrangements and consideration of bringing the work in-house. McDonald also requested meaningful trade-union negotiations, while Sarah Green asked whether an independent review of shared-services delivery would be completed and published before the planned transfer to Capita in August 2027. Members stressed the importance of payroll reliability: “Civil servants should not have to worry about whether the company responsible for their salaries is capable of paying it.”

Lilian Greenwood said the procurement had followed a two-year process, with evaluation weighted 70% to quality and 30% to cost, and that insourcing had been considered. She argued that changing course would cause disruption, delay and significant cost, and said Capita was currently performing against its Synergy contractual obligations. The Government would retain an in-house shared-services hub, conduct technical and payroll testing before implementation, and retain existing supplier arrangements until October 2028. Contractual protections included step-in rights, payment deductions and limits on costs and profit. Greenwood said the alternatives to the selected model involved “significant legal, commercial and delivery risk”. She did not agree to pause the contract, but said the Government would continue to review assurance and contingency arrangements and engage with recognised unions. The question was put and agreed to, and the motion was resolved.
