## High-Level Summary
The Westminster Hall debate considered e-petition 762640, which called for a referendum on returning the water sector to public ownership. Members discussed sewage pollution, leaks, rising bills, debt, poor customer service and inadequate investment, but differed over nationalisation, mutual or co-operative ownership, regulation and the referendum proposal. Several Members called for Thames Water to enter a special administration regime, while others warned that public ownership could transfer substantial financial risks to taxpayers and would not by itself guarantee better performance. The Minister, Emma Hardy, said that the Government were examining all possible solutions and that a later 10-year plan would set out proposals for stronger public control; the question was agreed to and the motion was resolved.

## Detailed Summary
### Petition and arguments for public ownership
Paul Davies opened the debate on e-petition 762640, which he said had received more than 208,000 signatures and called for a binding national referendum on public ownership of the water sector. He argued that the privatised model had failed economically, environmentally and in public-health terms, citing dividends, debt, leakage and sewage discharges. He said that public ownership could redirect money towards infrastructure, water poverty and public accountability, arguing that “public ownership is essential if we want to restore public trust.”

Martin Wrigley, Clive Lewis, Rebecca Long-Bailey, Richard Burgon and other Members also supported public ownership or greater public control, while several advocated mutual or co-operative structures. Wrigley argued that mutual ownership would enable customers to invest in their own system rather than shareholders’ returns: “They would then be investing in their own future, not in somebody else’s profits.” No change of ownership was decided during the debate. The question on the petition was agreed to, while the Government’s stated next step was to develop further proposals on public control and reform.

### Alternative models, regulation and the case against automatic nationalisation
Members differed on the form that any change should take and on whether a referendum was necessary. Olly Glover supported mutually owned public-benefit corporations rather than automatic nationalisation, while Helena Dollimore and other Members supported co-operative or mutual models with customer and community accountability. Several Members argued that ownership change alone would not resolve infrastructure and regulatory problems. Gregory Stafford said that nationalisation would not itself repair the network or remove the need for regulation: “Nationalisation does not repair pipes, make treatment works cheaper or remove the need for investment.”

Irene Campbell supported public ownership in England and Wales but cited the performance of Scottish Water as a reason to ensure that any new structure had “robust accountability and governance”. Olly Glover and Darren Paffey opposed the referendum proposal, preferring direct parliamentary action or a cross-party approach. Glover said: “we should add this to the Prime Minister’s list of things on which he needs to forge a cross-party consensus.” The debate did not reach agreement on a preferred ownership model or on whether a referendum should be held.

### Thames Water, special administration and financial arrangements
Thames Water’s debt, outages, pollution, infrastructure and customer service were recurring issues. Barry Gardiner argued that existing legislation allowed intervention for poor performance or insolvency and said that the company should enter a special administration regime (SAR), stating: “The Secretary of State can apply to put a company into a special administration regime if it has contravened, or is likely to contravene, any principal duty.” Fleur Anderson, Anna Dixon, Bambos Charalambous, Josh Dean and Roz Savage also called for special administration or a route towards public ownership. Other Members, including Gregory Stafford and Olly Glover, warned that public ownership would not remove the need for substantial investment or effective regulation.

Neil Hudson said that the Opposition did not want Thames Water to fail because of the potential consequences for customers and taxpayers: “we do not want to see Thames Water fold.” He asked whether special administration would be pursued as individual action or as part of a nationalisation programme. The Minister did not announce a decision on special administration, but said: “We are looking at all of the sector and what can be done right across it.” No immediate action concerning Thames Water was agreed in the debate.

### Government response and next steps
Emma Hardy, the Minister responding, said that reform needed to address pollution, financial resilience, ageing infrastructure, customer representation, climate pressures and public health. She referred to tougher regulation, increased Environment Agency inspections, asset ‘MOT’ tests, catchment-based management and the Government’s public control taskforce. She said that the Government would consider all possible solutions and that “later in the year our 10-year plan for Britain will set out plans for stronger public control over essentials such as water.”

The Minister did not give a specific timetable for nationalisation, a referendum or special administration, explaining that the Government were still considering the options and that “There is no single easy fix”. Paul Davies concluded that Members broadly agreed that the current model was failing, although they differed on the solution. The Chair then put the question, which was agreed to, and the motion was resolved: “That this House has considered e-petition 762640 relating to a referendum on public ownership of the water sector.” No division is recorded in the transcript.
