## High-Level Summary
Westminster Hall debates covered consumer‑led flexibility in the energy system, recognition for wounded service personnel, water scarcity, creative education, and rural rail timetables. Members argued that flexible demand could cut costs and emissions, while others cautioned against over‑reliance on demand shifting. Proposals for a medal for those wounded in combat were explored, with Ministers noting current recognition and considering options. On water scarcity, MPs highlighted pressures from drought, leakage and abstraction; the Minister outlined investigations, targets and forthcoming policy, including a water White Paper and building‑regulations consultation. All motions—framed as “That this House has considered…”—were agreed without a division, with several commitments to consult or report back.

## Detailed Summary
### Net Zero Transition: Consumer‑led Flexibility
Claire Young argued that flexible demand can reduce grid constraints and bills, noting constraint costs and curtailment: “we all pay producers to turn off wind turbines in Scotland”; “Those constraint payments have already cost us nearly £1.3 billion this year”. She promoted smart charging, vehicle‑to‑grid and time‑of‑use tariffs: “Unlocking just 10 GW of consumer-led flexibility by 2030 would be the equivalent of a third of the UK’s entire gas power station capacity”, and called for market openness, consumer protections and accountability, saying the flexibility commissioner “needs to be given teeth”. Supporters stressed benefits for jobs and fuel poverty; Sharon Hodgson said she “fully support[s]” the Government’s Clean Flexibility Roadmap, and Martin Wrigley urged, “Flexibility first is the way to go”. Greg Smith cautioned that “Flexibility should support the system, not prop up its structural weakness”.

Responding, the Minister, Michael Shanks, said flexibility is central to the clean power mission: “The clean power action plan outlined 10 GW to 12 GW of consumer-led flexibility”. He cited progress on smart meters—“70% of meters across Great Britain are now smart or advanced meters”—and take‑up of the Demand Flexibility Service by “over 2 million households and businesses”. He committed to attend “the first clean flexibility road map quarterly forum” that afternoon and said the flexibility commissioner would be announced “very soon”. Outcome: motion agreed—“That this House has considered consumer-led flexibility for a just transition”.

### Medal for Wounded Service Personnel
David Baines proposed an official medal for those wounded in combat, noting that “there remains no official recognition for those wounded in service”. He cited Ministry of Defence statistics since 2006—“a total of 2,644 personnel have sustained battle injuries”—and highlighted Corporal Andy Reid’s injuries and service, arguing that an operational medal “records where he served, not what he sacrificed”. He suggested industry funding could help minimise public cost.

The Minister, Louise Jones, explained medals are awarded under the Royal Prerogative and said those injured and evacuated from qualifying operations are “automatically awarded the relevant medal”. Officials are examining “whether a defined injury medal would be the best method of recognition…[and] whether such an award would be viable”. She could not commit to implementation now, saying it was “not possible at this time”. Outcome: debate concluded; “Question put and agreed to”.

### Water Scarcity
John Milne warned of a growing supply‑demand gap driven by hotter, drier conditions, leakage and abstraction, referring to drought impacts—“Farmers endured a devastatingly poor harvest and lost £800 million to drought”—and projections of “a national supply-demand deficit approaching 5 billion litres a day by 2050 unless action is taken”. He highlighted leakage—“Fully one fifth of the water that enters the system is lost”—and called for efficient housing standards, on‑farm reservoirs, flexible abstraction and a single national strategy with clear accountability. Members raised chalk streams, the resilience of South East Water, farmers’ needs and the effect on canals and growth planning.

The Minister, Emma Hardy, called the Tunbridge Wells disruption “completely unacceptable” and said “the Drinking Water Inspectorate will do a full investigation”. She set leakage‑reduction targets—“20% by 2027, 30% by 2032 and 50% by 2050”—and said she is “looking at” making it easier for farms to store water. She trailed a water White Paper, including “one powerful regulator” and a joined‑up approach. She also highlighted a building regulations consultation proposing to lower average per‑capita use and consider “water reuse systems in new developments”, alongside mandatory water‑efficiency labelling expected to save “23 billion litres…over 10 years”. Outcome: motion agreed—“That this House has considered water scarcity”.

### Creative Education
Simon Opher welcomed curriculum reforms to create space for the arts, citing participation trends: “participation in arts subjects at GCSE has fallen by 42% since 2010”. He linked creative education to better wellbeing and attendance, urged universal primary instrument tuition, and noted both the role and limits of music hubs—“There are 43 music hubs in the UK”—alongside teacher capacity and affordability issues.

The Minister, Olivia Bailey, confirmed an independent curriculum and assessment review and said academies will be required to teach the reformed national curriculum, “ensuring that creative education is not subject to a postcode lottery”. She announced a new National Centre for Arts and Music Education “by September 2026”, with procurement “in the new year”, continued support for 43 music hub partnerships with “£76 million” this year, a targeted music opportunities pilot with £2 million plus “£3.85 million” co‑funding, and £36.5 million for the dance and music scheme. Outcome: “Question put and agreed to”.

### Network Rail Timetable Changes: Rural Communities
John Lamont warned that the new East Coast Main Line timetable reduces LNER calls at Berwick‑upon‑Tweed “to just one every two hours”, harming commuters, disabled passengers and the local economy. He cited punctuality—“just 65.6% of services there arrived on time”—and, quoting a constituent, argued that for disabled passengers “direct services are not simply a convenience; they are often the only practical and dignified way to travel long distances”.

The Minister, Simon Lightwood, said the 14 December timetable change will add “60,000 additional seats per week” and “improve journey times between London and Edinburgh by 15 minutes”, with hourly regional links via other operators. He emphasised that “no timetable is ever the final one”. On reform, he said Great British Railways will be the “directing mind” and that timetables will be “more responsive to local needs”. Outcome: motion agreed—“That this House has considered the impact of Network Rail timetable changes on rural communities”.
