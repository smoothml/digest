## High-Level Summary
Westminster Hall considered five motions on community hospitals and step-down care, rural telecommunications connectivity, Government support for reindustrialisation, the Croydon Area Remodelling Scheme on the Brighton Main Line, and water companies’ role in planning applications. Members raised concerns about access, capacity, resilience, investment, skills, infrastructure and accountability, often using constituency examples. Ministers outlined existing policies and indicated areas for further discussion, but several requested measures remained unresolved. All five motions were agreed, with no division recorded, and the final sitting was adjourned after the water-planning debate.

## Detailed Summary
### Community Hospitals and Step-down Care
David Chadwick moved the debate and focused on proposed reductions in community hospital services in Powys and their possible effects on patients using services across the England–Wales border. He cited average bed utilisation of 91% in Powys and proposals to reduce physical health inpatient beds from 148 to 60, with possible effects on minor-injury, diagnostic, mental health, palliative, rehabilitation and step-down services. He questioned whether expanded care at home could be delivered with sufficient staff, coverage and funding, saying: “even if the board delivers everything it proposes, patients will still need community hospital beds.” Other Members discussed under-used or threatened services, discharge delays, poor estates and preventive and rehabilitative care. Monica Harding cited 23,695 lost bed days in Surrey and Sussex in one month and sought support for Molesey Hospital and Princess Alice hospice; Roz Savage said the integrated care board had reassured her that Cirencester Hospital’s future was safe, while calling for better communication, consultation and planning for population growth.

Diana R. Johnson, the English Health Minister, said that decisions on NHS Wales services were for the Welsh Government and the relevant health board, but recognised cross-border use and said discussions among the relevant authorities were taking place. For England, she linked community hospitals and intermediate care to neighbourhood health, including 250 planned neighbourhood health centres and a target for at least 80% of community health service activity to take place within 18 weeks by 2028-29. She said intermediate care should be planned locally with engagement from patients, staff and communities, and agreed to examine the Surrey and Sussex concerns. She did not provide a detailed account of the cross-border discussions, stating: “I do not have a detailed read-out of the results of those discussions.” The motion was agreed and the sitting was suspended; no division was recorded.

### Rural Connectivity
Henry Tufnell argued that inadequate broadband and mobile coverage was affecting work, education, public services, economic activity and emergency resilience in rural areas. He cited his constituency’s ranking of 444th out of 650 for mobile service quality and 1% coverage for high-quality stand-alone 5G, and referred to persistent mobile notspots in Tenby, where eight outdoor small cells were announced. Members also discussed slow mast upgrades, digital exclusion, the digital switchover, vulnerable users’ emergency devices, access to mast sites and resilience in island communities. Torcuil Crichton called for stronger enforcement of back-up power requirements, saying: “The digital back-up provided to vulnerable customers—a hybrid battery pack—simply is not adequate.”

Ian Murray said that the Government aimed for high-quality stand-alone 5G connectivity everywhere by 2030 and expected this to be delivered commercially. He referred to planned mast upgrades in Pembrokeshire, the migration of more than 96% of landlines from analogue to digital, free battery back-up devices and Ofcom requirements concerning emergency access during power cuts. He said he was willing to examine Crichton’s proposal concerning category 1 responder status, consider concerns about mast-site access, and discuss whether the planned 2028 Pembrokeshire upgrade could be accelerated. The Government’s Project Gigabit ambition remained 99% coverage by 2032. The motion was agreed, with no division recorded.

### Government Support for Reindustrialisation
Andy MacNae argued that reindustrialisation should create well-paid employment, strengthen communities, improve resilience and spread growth beyond established economic centres. Members discussed energy prices, public procurement, finance, skills, critical minerals, steel, cement, defence, automotive manufacturing and the proposed target of one million additional jobs in manufacturing, mining and agriculture by 2036. Several Members called for an expansion of the British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme (BICS), while others sought place-based investment and stronger domestic supply chains. Members differed over the zero-emission vehicle mandate: some called for its 2030 target to be reduced to 50%, while Perran Moon argued that weakening it would harm the automotive transition and reindustrialisation. The debate also included calls for public ownership and support for Grangemouth, alongside concerns about specialist steel tariffs and their effect on downstream manufacturers. MacNae identified the institutional question as: “who is doing the work?”

Blair McDougall, the Minister for Reindustrialisation, said the Government supported a high level of ambition and had asked the Industrial Strategy Advisory Council to help develop measures for productive jobs, while continuing to work with the Reindustrialisation Research Group. He said the Government were seeking a stronger sense of place in industrial policy, were encouraging manufacturers to use BICS, were reviewing energy-intensive industry support, and wanted public spending, including defence procurement, to support British jobs and skills. On Grangemouth, he referred to discussions about site preparation and engagement with Syngenta. He said reindustrialisation had to be an entire-Government effort and a national priority: “we will back the industries where Britain can win”. The motion was agreed, with no division recorded.

### Brighton Main Line: Croydon Area Remodelling Scheme
Peter Lamb sought Government support for the Croydon Area Remodelling Scheme (CARS), arguing that the East Croydon bottleneck constrained capacity and caused delays across the Brighton Main Line and the wider Thameslink corridor. He said peak use had returned to pre-pandemic levels and that the line could reach full capacity in a little over three years. Members linked the scheme to Gatwick and Luton airport expansion, the Universal Studios UK project and other development, and cited the potential for economic benefits, jobs, homes and reduced commuting time. Natasha Irons also raised the lack of step-free access at Norwood Junction and proposed using planned renewals as an opportunity to deliver improvements, saying: “Step-free access is not just a nice-to-have”.

In response, Justin Madders said that CARS had been cancelled at the 2021 spending review and was not prioritised in the 2025 spending review because of limited funding and other recent investment on the line. He did not commit to restoring the full scheme, but said the Department was examining whether elements could be delivered in phases alongside planned renewals. This could improve reliability and potentially provide step-free access at Norwood Junction, without ruling out the full scheme in future. He said the next spending review would provide an opportunity to make the case for investment and welcomed consideration of alternative funding models. The motion was agreed, with no division recorded.

### Water Companies and Planning Applications
Jessica Brown-Fuller argued that water and wastewater capacity should be considered more directly before housing developments were approved, and called for water companies to become statutory consultees on planning applications. Members cited sewage discharges, water shortages, flooding, speculative development and other infrastructure constraints. Supporters argued that water companies held relevant technical information and that development should be matched by sufficient water supply, drainage and wastewater-treatment capacity. Charlie Maynard discussed Grampian conditions and called for water companies to provide full and accurate information, while John Milne argued that statutory consultee status alone would not be sufficient because water companies remained under a statutory duty to connect developments. The Opposition raised concerns that adding statutory consultees could increase delay, cost and bureaucracy and could allow commercial water companies to act as gatekeepers. Gareth Bacon nevertheless acknowledged possible benefits for scrutiny and public confidence, saying: “The truth is that the answer is far less clear than the problem is apparent.”

Matthew Pennycook said national planning policy already required new housing to be accompanied by appropriate water, drainage, wastewater and flood-risk infrastructure. He argued that planning should not duplicate other regulatory regimes, although infrastructure constraints could be material considerations where there was evidence that those regimes were not operating effectively. The Government would maintain a moratorium on creating new statutory consultees because blanket requirements could cause duplication, regulatory burden and delay; water companies could already make representations on individual applications and were required to be consulted during local-plan preparation. The Minister said strategic capacity issues were better addressed at the plan-making stage and that strengthened national policy supported sustainable drainage. He offered to discuss the specific issues further and write to Members. The motion was agreed, with no division recorded, and the sitting was adjourned.
