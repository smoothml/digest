## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords considered oral questions on access to Child Trust Funds, vocational GCSEs, LED lighting and the Independent Football Regulator. It approved an Enterprise Act order, received the Commons answer to an Urgent Question on electronic monitoring, and began Committee scrutiny of the Health Bill, including the abolition of NHS England, supply-chain governance, health inequalities, diabetes and patient involvement. A motion on Northern Ireland rail interoperability regulations was agreed after a regret amendment was defeated by 20 votes to 63. Several Health Bill amendments were agreed, withdrawn or not moved; debate on patient involvement and unpaid carers was adjourned without a ministerial response.

## Detailed Summary
### Child Trust Funds for adults who lack mental capacity
Lord Young of Cookham asked about progress in enabling adults who lack mental capacity to access their Child Trust Funds. He cited figures of approximately £250 million and 80,000 young people, and criticised the cost and delay of applications to the Court of Protection. Lord Lemos said the Government were considering all options, rejected banks’ exceptional procedures as inconsistent with the Mental Capacity Act 2005 and common-law principles, and said officials were working towards a practical solution: “The Government are very hopeful of making progress in the coming weeks.”

Baroness Levitt argued for a bespoke process based on trust, while Lord Lemos said parental support had to be balanced with the adult’s agency and protection from abuse, noting that “not every adult has loving parents”. Peers also raised unclaimed funds, possible scams, a Court of Protection fast track, Financial Services and Markets Bill amendments and automatic release at 21. Lord Lemos agreed to place information about the Court of Protection arrangements in the Library and said a Government announcement would come in the coming weeks; no final model was announced. He also acknowledged that the issue had continued for too long: “We will do so, because it has gone on too long”.

### Vocational GCSEs and safeguarding against disadvantaged pupils being steered
Baroness Barran asked what empirical evidence supported the assertion that vocational GCSEs would not disproportionately steer disadvantaged pupils towards vocational or non-academic routes, and what safeguards would prevent lowered expectations. Baroness Smith of Malvern said the policy was intended to widen choice rather than separate pupils at 14, and that vocational qualifications would have parity of esteem with traditional GCSEs. Baroness Barran questioned the evidence and referred to higher levels of young people not in education, employment or training in Wales and Scotland. Baroness Smith replied that existing technical awards were taken slightly more often by disadvantaged pupils and rejected the suggestion that knowledge would be removed from the curriculum: “knowledge is a key element of the curriculum.”

Peers asked about advice for parents, industry input, design and technology, and monitoring take-up by background and region. Baroness Smith said: “We will consult on the content and nature of vocational GCSEs very soon, this autumn.” She also said that English, mathematics and science would remain compulsory, and that development, monitoring and evaluation would address the risk of pupils being steered rather than offered wider opportunities. Consultation and subsequent evaluation were the stated next steps; no final qualification specification was announced in the debate.

### LED lighting, headlights and health effects
Lord Davidson of Glen Clova asked about developing evidence on adverse health effects from LED lighting. Baroness Merron said that “The risk to the general population is considered low.” She said the Government were monitoring evidence, publishing guidance on flicker reduction and healthy lighting environments, and supporting research and international standards. On the new Moorfields hospital, she said its design integrated daylight with LED lighting and that the Government were seeking confirmation from the trust about daylighting studies before writing to him: “When I get that information, I will be very pleased to write to my noble and learned friend.”

The discussion also covered glare from vehicle headlights, advertising claims for home LED beauty devices, daylight exposure in intensive care, artificial light at night and children’s screen use. The Department for Transport was undertaking research and had updated advice in the Highway Code. Baroness Merron said regulations were intended to prevent misleading claims and that an evidence-based review programme on artificial light and health was being developed. She also said wider guidance for five to 18 year-olds would be published in January. No specific new regulation or assessment was announced in the debate.

### Independent Football Regulator levy and budget
Lord Harlech asked what oversight the Government would exercise over the Independent Football Regulator’s proposed annual levy of £20.3 million, which he said was about twice the original estimate. Baroness Lloyd of Effra said that “The Independent Football Regulator, led by its board, rightly sets its budget independently of government.” She said the proposed levy represented 0.2% of football’s £9 billion annual revenue, and that the regulator was subject to standard controls and would lay its annual report before Parliament. Lord Harlech and Lord Hayward questioned the increased operating and set-up costs and the accuracy of the original impact assessment; the Minister said the assessment had been produced under the previous Government and was caveated.

Other speakers argued that financial instability, governance problems and losses across the football pyramid justified regulation. The Minister said the proposed revenue-based model would place higher costs on Premier League clubs and lower costs on National League clubs. She said the regulator’s chief executive had written to all clubs before a consultation that would run until 4 December. The Government would not intervene in the Manchester City proceedings because “It is not for the Government to intervene”. No change to the proposed levy or budget was announced.

### Enterprise Act 2002 order
Baroness Anderson of Stoke-on-Trent moved approval of the Enterprise Act 2002 (Specification of Additional Section 58 Consideration) Order 2026. The instrument had been considered in Grand Committee on 27 October, and the motion was agreed without debate: “Motion agreed.”

### Electronic monitoring of offenders
The Lords received the House of Commons answer to an Urgent Question given on 27 October. The Government said they were expanding electronic monitoring with £100 million of investment, applying a presumption that eligible offenders released under the earned progression model would be tagged, and using licence conditions, probation supervision and recall powers alongside electronic monitoring. They said the relevant quarterly statistics, including figures for October, would be published in January: “will be publicly available in January.”

Peers asked how many people released under early-release arrangements were not tagged, whether tagging at source could replace home tagging, how probation resources would be used, and how GPS and sobriety tagging would develop. Lord Lemos said tagging at source was being rolled out and that, by Christmas, probation officers in every region would know the whereabouts of people on tags within 10 minutes. He did not provide the requested proportion or reoffending figure, saying that tagging and reoffending statistics would be published in the normal way. He acknowledged that problems remained with the service, while saying that the arrangements with Serco were being better managed.

### Health Bill: devolved consent amendments and transition plan for NHS England
The Committee considered government amendments requiring the consent of the relevant devolved Government before a transfer scheme moved property, rights or liabilities from NHS England to a Scottish, Welsh or Northern Ireland public body. Baroness Merron said the amendments followed engagement with the devolved Governments and provided a safeguard: “any transfers to their bodies can take place only with their agreement.” Baroness Pidgeon welcomed the protection but argued that devolution should be considered earlier in the legislative process, while Earl Howe supported the amendments. Amendment 1 and consequential Amendments 2 and 3 were agreed, and Clause 2 as amended and Clause 3 were agreed.

Earl Howe’s Amendment 4 sought a published and independently reviewed transition plan covering the objectives, costs, savings, staffing, risks, continuity and accountability of abolishing NHS England. Supporters sought greater clarity about the proposed savings of more than £1 billion a year, transition costs, specialised commissioning, local decision-making, staff transfers and the timetable. Baroness Merron opposed putting the detailed requirements in the Bill, but said the Government remained on track to deliver the stated savings and confirmed that 1 April remained the intention, subject to the Bill’s passage. In response to a question about whether staff would be transferred into the department or a stand-alone body, she said: “The intention is the former, but I will gladly write further to the noble Lord on this point.” Earl Howe withdrew Amendment 4, which was not put to a division.

### Health Bill: Supply Chain Coordination Limited and value-based procurement
Earl Howe’s Amendment 5 sought a report to Parliament before NHS England’s shareholding in Supply Chain Coordination Limited was transferred, covering future ownership, governance, continuity of supply, risks, staffing and independent assurance. Lord Hunt of Kings Heath supported scrutiny but also criticised NHS Supply Chain’s current performance and called for stronger relationships with local NHS bodies and greater use of value-based procurement. Baroness Finlay of Llandaff and Lord Lansley stressed that procurement should consider clinical outcomes, patient experience and longer-term costs rather than simply the lowest price. Lord Scriven raised performance indicators, spending outside the central system and the risk of transferring responsibilities during a wider reorganisation.

Baroness Merron said the shareholding was intended to transfer to the Department of Health and Social Care and that “The transfer of shares will have no impact on the services delivered by the company.” She said operational responsibilities and contractual arrangements would remain in place through the transition, and that NHS Supply Chain was committed to value-based procurement through a “comply or explain” model. She also said the department would commission independent monitoring and evaluation of its guidance, with annual reports over three years. Amendment 5 was withdrawn. No formal disposition of the related Amendment 211 is recorded in the transcript.

### Health Bill: health inequalities and prevention
The Committee debated whether the Bill should replace the Secretary of State’s duty to have regard to health inequalities with stronger duties requiring action, and whether it should require a cross-government strategy, reporting and specific consideration of inclusion health groups, coastal and urban communities, general-practice distribution and wider determinants such as housing and employment. Baroness Jones of Moulsecoomb, Lord Patel, Baroness Tyler of Enfield, Lord Hunt of Kings Heath, Baroness Gerada and others argued that stronger statutory duties would improve accountability and prevention. Baroness Jones said that “a duty of ‘must have regard to’, as is currently in Clause 4, is not much of a duty.” Lord Lansley noted that existing provisions of the National Health Service Act 2006 already addressed aspects of public health, while distinguishing those provisions from the duties in the Bill.

Baroness Merron said the Government’s 10-year health plan already placed tackling inequalities at its core, and pointed to neighbourhood health centres, GP recruitment and training, the review of the Carr-Hill funding formula, annual reporting and the health inequalities dashboard. She argued that the existing statutory framework was more flexible and proportionate than adding population-specific duties and a new strategy to primary legislation: “our biggest challenge is to deliver that.” Baroness Jones withdrew Amendment 6. Amendments 7 to 11 and 13 to 20 were not moved; no new statutory health inequalities strategy was agreed.

### Northern Ireland rail interoperability regulations
Lord Hendy of Richmond Hill moved approval of regulations implementing the United Kingdom’s obligations under the Windsor Framework and updating domestic legislation under the Convention concerning International Carriage by Rail. He said the regulations would establish a dedicated interoperability framework for Northern Ireland, reflecting the different rail gauges and technical characteristics of the railways on the island of Ireland, and would support cross-border services and joint procurement. Baroness Hoey moved a regret amendment because there had been no formal public consultation and argued that the regulations would create regulatory divergence and impose rules made by the European Union. Other speakers raised constitutional accountability, possible business costs, supply-chain effects and the limited parliamentary and Assembly control.

Baroness Suttie supported the regulations, while also describing the lack of wider consultation as regrettable. Lord Hendy said the Government had conducted targeted engagement with the Department for Infrastructure, Translink, the Office of Rail and Road, the Rail Safety and Standards Board, conformity assessment bodies and the Railway Industry Association. He said the review of Great Britain’s arrangements would be published by March 2027. The regret amendment was defeated, with “Ayes 20, Noes 63.” The main motion was then agreed.

### Health Bill: diabetes
Lord Rennard’s Amendment 12 would have made diabetes-related inequalities an express consideration in Clause 4, including variation in access to structured education, glucose monitoring technology and insulin pump therapy. He and other speakers highlighted regional variation in diabetes outcomes, access to continuous glucose monitoring and insulin pumps, prevention, early diagnosis and the continuity of national programmes during the abolition of NHS England. Baroness Merron said that diabetes-related inequalities were already covered by the general duty and referred to the NHS Diabetes Prevention Programme, the NHS Type 2 Diabetes Path to Remission Programme and the National Diabetes Audit.

The Minister opposed disease-specific provisions in primary legislation, arguing that they could restrict the ability to respond to new evidence and technologies and could duplicate existing monitoring arrangements. She said the transfer of functions would not alter service provision: “a transfer that will not impact service provision.” Lord Rennard withdrew Amendment 12. Amendments 13 to 17 were not moved, Clause 4 was agreed, and Amendments 18 to 20 were not moved. The transcript records discussion of future diabetes amendments numbered 225 and 226 but does not record their formal disposition.

### Health Bill: patient involvement, health and care partners, and unpaid carers
Lord Hunt of Kings Heath moved Amendment 21, supported by amendments in the group, to provide for a patient-nominated health and care partner and to strengthen the position of unpaid carers. He argued that a person chosen by the patient could support communication, decision-making and care in hospitals, care homes and mental health settings, drawing on problems exposed during the Covid-19 pandemic. Baroness Finlay of Llandaff supported the proposal and described the practical expertise that relatives and carers could provide for people with complex disabilities.

Baroness Tyler of Enfield proposed duties on integrated care boards to identify unpaid carers, promote their health and well-being, and provide meaningful respite. Other speakers discussed under-identification, poorer health among carers, the particular needs of young carers, and support for disabled people with high support needs in hospital. The debate was adjourned before the Minister responded and before Amendment 21 was decided: “Debate on Amendment 21 adjourned.” The House adjourned at 10.13 pm.
