## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords considered two Private Members’ Bills at Second Reading. The first concerned consultation on replacing the House of Lords with an alternative second Chamber, with debate focusing on democratic legitimacy, regional representation, elections, expertise and the primacy of the House of Commons. The Government expressed sympathy with reform but opposed the Bill’s proposed timetable as premature; the Bill was nevertheless given a Second Reading and committed to a Committee of the Whole House. The second Bill sought safeguards concerning sovereignty, Chagossian participation, resettlement, the Diego Garcia base and environmental protection; the Government opposed it, but it too was given a Second Reading and committed to a Committee of the Whole House before the House adjourned at 1.38 pm.

## Detailed Summary
### House of Lords (Alternative Second Chamber) Bill [HL] — Second Reading
Baroness Smith of Llanfaes introduced the Bill as a means of giving effect to the Government’s manifesto commitment to consult on replacing the House of Lords with an alternative second Chamber. She said it would require consultation, a published response and a statement of the Government’s intended next steps, with wider public engagement including citizens’ assemblies. She described the Bill’s purpose as follows: “The Government promised to consult on that future; this Bill simply asks them to honour that commitment.” Supporters, including Lord Scriven, Baroness O’Grady of Upper Holloway, Lord Wigley, Lord Thomas of Cwmgiedd, Baroness Bennett of Manor Castle and Lord Pack, discussed democratic and geographic legitimacy, possible elected or partly elected models, proportional representation, regional representation, the retention of Cross-Bench expertise and safeguards for the Commons’ primacy. Lord Scriven argued that appointment remained dependent on patronage and said: “After 115 years, that is hardly a leap in the dark.”

Other speakers supported reform but differed over its scope and risks. Lord Blunkett favoured a constitutional commission and warned that direct elections could produce a party-controlled list system. Lord Bourne of Aberystwyth argued for reform rather than abolition, including changes to appointments and representation. Lord Burns discussed limits on the size of the House, term limits and proportional representation, while Baroness Taylor of Bolton stressed the need to preserve a functioning Parliament and avoid unintended consequences. Lord Jackson of Peterborough opposed the Bill, arguing that it lacked detail and assumed abolition; Baroness Finn questioned whether another broad consultation was the best use of Government resources. Baroness Twycross said that the Government had sympathy with the Bill’s central principle but considered it premature, stating: “A meaningful consultation on such an important issue demands a careful and thought-through plan.” In response to Lord Scriven, she said she could not state when the consultation would begin and that the Government did not want to be “bounced into a 12-week consultation”. Baroness Smith maintained that the Bill was modest and that the public should have a say. The Bill was read a second time and committed to a Committee of the Whole House.

### British Sovereignty Protection (Chagos Islands) Bill [HL] — Second Reading
Lord Weir of Ballyholme said that the Bill was intended to provide greater certainty for the Chagos Islands and greater control for Chagossians following the previous proposed agreement with Mauritius and the change in the United States’ position. Its principal safeguard was a “triple lock”: any transfer of sovereignty or lease of the islands would require an Act of Parliament, approval by a representative Chagossian Government if one existed, and a referendum of eligible British Indian Ocean Territory citizens. The Bill would also provide for the status of Diego Garcia, disclosure of financial arrangements, permits to work on the base, resettlement rights, and consultation on economic, social, cultural and environmental matters. Lord Weir said that the measures would put Chagossians “at the heart of any level of discussion”.

Supporters, including Lords Lilley, Murray of Blidworth, Hannan of Kingsclere, Bellingham and Callanan and Baroness Meyer, argued that the International Court of Justice opinion was advisory, that the legal mechanisms discussed did not compel a transfer of sovereignty, and that the proposed agreement raised strategic, financial and constitutional concerns. They also discussed the Diego Garcia base, the Treaty of Pelindaba, the feasibility of resettlement and marine protection. Baroness Winterton of Doncaster, responding for the Government, said that Diego Garcia was important to UK and US security and that the previous Government had conducted 11 rounds of talks before the Labour Government inherited a draft treaty. She maintained that “Doing nothing is simply not an option and threatens the UK’s safety.” She said that the Government continued to engage with Chagossian groups and that the contact group had met the previous week. She rejected a referendum on the grounds that treaty decisions were matters of national security, and said that the proposed treaty would enable Mauritius to develop a resettlement programme on islands other than Diego Garcia and would establish arrangements intended to protect the marine environment, including a ban on commercial fishing. The Government opposed the Bill because, in Baroness Winterton’s words, “it seeks to tie their hands in protecting the base”. Lord Weir rejected the Government’s position and argued for an approach centred on Chagossian self-determination and resettlement. The Bill was read a second time and committed to a Committee of the Whole House. The House then adjourned at 1.38 pm.
