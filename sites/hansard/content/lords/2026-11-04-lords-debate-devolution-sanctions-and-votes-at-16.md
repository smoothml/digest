## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords questioned the Government on Wales’s devolution, local housing allowance, the role and reform of the House, and prison recalls. It approved the Private Rented Sector Database Regulations, considered the position of Poundland and its workforce, and agreed the Global Human Rights and International Humanitarian Law Sanctions Regulations after agreeing Lord Leigh’s regret amendment by 94 votes to 90. The second day of Committee on the Representation of the People Bill covered election administration, electoral-law complexity, votes at 16, civic education, nationality qualifications, registration, information for young voters and implementation costs. Several amendments were agreed, withdrawn or not moved, while the Government gave undertakings to provide further information on some matters.

## Detailed Summary
### Wales: constitutional and policy divergence
Baroness Wilcox of Newport asked about the effect of constitutional and policy divergence on the integrity of the United Kingdom. Lord Katz said devolution allowed different approaches while retaining shared resources and collective strength, and agreed that the practical test was “what matters is what works”. He referred to cost-of-living support, growth, jobs, defence, steel, compound semiconductors and life sciences as areas in which Wales contributed to the United Kingdom.

The questions also covered further devolution, regional empowerment and intergovernmental relations. Lord Katz said Wales was at risk of becoming highly centralised and supported passing power from Westminster through Cardiff Bay to regions and communities, including through employment support, city-region deals, investment in Ynys Môn and transport links in Deeside. He said the Government would not support independence and wanted devolved Governments to focus on public services. In response to a question about the number of interministerial meetings, he referred to the new National Economic Council and the Prime Minister’s offer of partnership. The next meeting was expected to be hosted in Cardiff the following spring; no further decision was recorded in the transcript.

### Local housing allowance freeze and homelessness
Baroness Lister of Burtersett asked whether the freeze in local housing allowance (LHA) should be lifted and what effect that might have on homelessness and temporary accommodation. Baroness Sherlock cited £38 billion a year in housing support, more than £40 billion over 10 years for social and affordable housing, and more than £4 billion over three years for homelessness and rough-sleeping services. She said that “the only way to do that is to secure an adequate number of decent affordable homes”, and referred to an additional £1.5 billion for about 10,000 homes in England and discretionary local-authority support for housing-cost shortfalls.

Peers argued that LHA rates had fallen below market rents, restricting access to private accommodation and contributing to temporary-accommodation pressures. Baroness Sherlock said the Government did not recognise the cited figure that fewer than 2% of properties were affordable, but acknowledged affordability pressures and said the LHA rate would remain under review. She also discussed housing supply, homelessness services, substance misuse and the public-health consequences of poor housing, including enforcement against substandard landlords and Awaab’s law. No decision to lift the freeze was announced in the transcript.

### Relevance and reform of the House of Lords
Lord Mann asked about the relevance of the Second Chamber. Baroness Twycross said the Government valued the House of Lords as a revising Chamber and were pursuing reforms intended to improve its effectiveness and public confidence. In response to questions about artificial intelligence, the Commons, regional representation and democratic legitimacy, she said a modernised House would “continue to have a purpose for many years”.

The discussion also covered an elected Chamber, the size and composition of the House, appointments and retirement. Baroness Twycross said the Government wanted a smaller Chamber with improved national and regional balance. She said the immediate focus was the Retirement and Participation Committee’s recommendations and that the Government would respond later in the year. She also said the appointments process would be reformed, transparency had been strengthened, and the House of Lords Appointments Commission would continue to vet and nominate Cross-Benchers. No final reform model or further appointments timetable was given.

### Prison recalls for breach of licence
Lord Farmer asked what action the Government were taking in response to recalls to prison for breach of licence. Lord Lemos described recall as an essential public-protection tool and said the Sentencing Act had replaced 14- and 28-day short-term recalls with a fixed 56-day recall to allow time to address risks and resettlement needs. Peers raised the links between recalls and homelessness, debt, substance misuse, administrative breaches and release arrangements. Lord Lemos said administrative procedures and technology should help avoid recalls for administrative reasons, and that the community accommodation service had housed 36,000 people.

Several questions concerned imprisonment for public protection (IPP) sentences. Lord Lemos agreed that action was needed and said the Government intended to end the IPP sentence and the licence period without compromising public protection. He accepted the need to improve access to drug treatment and said recall data would be published in the normal way, but considered it too early for meaningful attribution to the earned progression scheme. No specific timetable or detailed breakdown of recalls was announced.

### Private Rented Sector Database Regulations 2026
Baroness Taylor of Stevenage moved approval of the draft Private Rented Sector Database Regulations 2026, which had been considered in Grand Committee and had been drawn to the House’s special attention by the Secondary Legislation Scrutiny Committee. The motion stated, “That the draft Regulations laid before the House on 9 September be approved”. The motion was agreed. No further debate or next step was recorded in the transcript.

### Poundland and wider high-street conditions
The House considered the Commons answer to an Urgent Question on Poundland, followed by questions about its workforce, suppliers, ownership and trading conditions. The Commons answer stated that “Poundland is not currently in administration and the business continues to trade”, and described contact with USDAW, Poundland’s managing director and Gordon Brothers. It said the Government wanted to protect jobs but would not influence ongoing commercial negotiations. In the subsequent Lords answer, however, Lord Leong said that Poundland “has appointed an administrator and there is creditor protection for 10 days”. The transcript therefore contains differing descriptions of the company’s status, which are reported here without resolving them.

Peers attributed the difficulties variously to employment costs, business rates, regulation, private-equity debt and the company’s longer-running financial problems. Lord Leong referred to an earlier restructuring and to measures affecting high-street businesses, including £210 million for community-asset programmes. He advised suppliers to examine their contractual terms, including any Romalpa clause, and said there were no immediate plans for worker directors. Asked whether the Government would rule out a specific Poundland subsidy or nationalisation, he replied, “The answer is no”. The Government said they would continue to monitor the negotiations and support affected workers if required.

### Representation of the People Bill: election administration and electoral-law complexity
The second day of Committee considered overnight counts, poll cards, undue influence, protections for under-16s and the structure of electoral law. Lord Fuller, Lord Hayward and Lord McCobb opposed making overnight verification or counting a statutory requirement for local elections, citing multiple contests, different voting systems, staffing pressures and the need for accuracy. Lord McCobb said, “accuracy matters more than speed”. Lord Frost supported the principle of prompt counts, arguing that delays gave parties and commentators more opportunity to shape the narrative before all results were known. Baroness Taylor said decisions about when to begin verification and counting were best left to returning officers. No formal outcome for Amendment 124 was stated in the transcript.

Peers also called for electoral law to be consolidated and simplified. Baroness Taylor acknowledged that consolidation was “a key issue” and said she would take the matter back to the department and return to it later. Amendment 12, concerning transitional canvass arrangements for 14- and 15-year-olds, was agreed; Schedule 1 as amended and Clauses 7 to 14 were agreed. Amendment 10 was withdrawn, Amendment 11 was not moved, and Lord Hayward later withdrew Amendment 13 after the Minister offered further discussion and a written response on consultation with the Electoral Commission and the Association of Electoral Administrators.

### Global Human Rights and International Humanitarian Law Sanctions Regulations 2026
Lord Wood of Anfield moved regulations revoking and re-enacting the 2020 global human-rights sanctions regime with expanded purposes and designation criteria. The changes covered serious violations of international humanitarian law, the right to liberty and security of person, obstruction of humanitarian assistance and designation by description. Lord Wood described them as “a strong enhancement of our existing powers”.

Lord Leigh of Hurley moved a regret amendment seeking clearer definitions, safeguards for lawful trade with Israel within its pre-1967 lines, designation criteria, an impact assessment and annual reporting. Supporters raised concerns about indirect involvement, association, charities, banks, medicines, trade, religious activity and consistency between different territories. Other Peers supported the regulations as a global accountability tool, including in relation to humanitarian-law violations and aid obstruction. Lord Wood said, “These regulations do not affect that trade”, that designations would be ministerial and evidence-based, and that a de minimis impact assessment had found the expected additional impact on UK businesses negligible. He undertook to provide written answers and place them in the Library. The regret amendment was agreed by 94 votes to 90, and the motion as amended was agreed.

### Representation of the People Bill: votes at 16, civic education and safeguarding
The Committee debated the implications of extending registration and voting to 16- and 17-year-olds, including civic education, political impartiality, peer pressure, looked-after children and contact by campaigners. Lord Fuller and others sought statutory neutrality requirements for information given to young people. Baroness Lister, Lord Wallace and Lord Rennard argued for stronger citizenship, political and media education to address unequal confidence and access to political learning. Lord Farmer and Baroness Meyer sought an assessment of maturity and independent decision-making before implementation; Baroness Taylor said voting rights were not based on cognitive ability and that the Bill implemented a manifesto commitment.

Baroness Taylor said the Government had conducted 32 focus groups and a survey of more than 3,500 young people, were working with the Electoral Commission and other organisations, and were supporting a democratic-education framework whose formal launch was planned for March 2027. She said existing law required political impartiality in schools and that a campaigning code of conduct would be developed with the Speaker, the Electoral Commission and political parties. Amendment 18 was withdrawn, Clause 15 was agreed, and Amendment 19 and Amendments 20 to 24 were not moved. The Minister also agreed to provide further information on the research and said she would update Peers on transitional education support as that work progressed.

### Representation of the People Bill: nationality, registration and the composition of the electorate
Lord Frost proposed removing voting and registration rights based solely on Commonwealth citizenship, while preserving British, Irish and retained EU rights and offering an alternative based on reciprocity. He argued that the current rules were historically derived, insufficiently reciprocal and unusual internationally, stating that “Only British and Irish citizens should have the right to vote in national elections”. Supporters raised questions about students, temporary residents and the absence of accurate figures. Lord Pack argued that the wider issue should be considered as part of the treatment of resident voters rather than by singling out Commonwealth citizens.

Baroness Taylor opposed the amendments, saying resident Commonwealth citizens’ voting rights reflected historical ties and contributions and were not based on reciprocity. She clarified that a visitor without a home in the United Kingdom would not be eligible to vote and said, “The Government will not be removing Commonwealth citizens’ voting rights”. Lord Frost withdrew Amendment 25, and Amendments 26 to 31 were not moved. Amendments concerning voting by Members of the House of Lords and extending voting rights to certain residents were also debated. The Government maintained that sitting Lords’ direct parliamentary role justified their exclusion from UK parliamentary elections and rejected a residence-based franchise. Amendment 32 was withdrawn; Amendment 33 was not moved; and Amendments 41 to 44B were not moved.

### Representation of the People Bill: electoral boundaries, automatic registration and implementation costs
Baroness Pinnock proposed a review of the effects of adding 16- and 17-year-olds to constituency and council electorates, citing differences in age profiles and ward sizes. Lord Fuller and others questioned whether automatic registration could create duplicate or inaccurate entries, particularly for people without established documentary or data records. Baroness Taylor said the Local Government Boundary Commission for England was considering how to incorporate newly enfranchised electors into its forecasts and did not anticipate a different approach to electoral reviews. She also said pilots would test datasets and registration processes, with independent Electoral Commission evaluation, while registration officers would continue to assess eligibility.

Lord Hayward moved Amendment 40, seeking a report on the costs of the duties to raise awareness and provide assistance. He challenged the impact assessment’s treatment of non-monetised costs, while other Peers raised possible expenditure on staff, information technology, policing and local-authority administration. Baroness Taylor said impact assessments followed Treasury Green Book guidance, undertook to write to Peers on the detailed financial questions, and said that Government funding should be provided where new burdens were imposed on local government. Amendment 40 was withdrawn, Amendments 35 to 39 had earlier not been moved, and Amendments 45 to 51 were not moved. The House resumed and adjourned at 6.59 pm.
