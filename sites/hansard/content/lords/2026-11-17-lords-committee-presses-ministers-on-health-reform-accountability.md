## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords Committee considered amendments on children’s health, voluntary and community provision, adult gender services, integrated care board (ICB) governance, women’s centres, prison healthcare, condition-specific services, neighbourhood health plans and NHS regional teams. Ministers generally relied on existing duties, guidance and national frameworks, while offering meetings, correspondence, further guidance or updates on several matters. The Committee withdrew or did not move the amendments under discussion, agreed a number of clauses and agreed Amendments 103 and 104 on Special Health Authorities. Oral questions addressed private parking, electricity demand, official development assistance and public-sector cyber security; the House adjourned at 10 pm.

## Detailed Summary
### Children’s health, waiting times and digital harms
Baroness Cass moved Amendment 66 and spoke to a wider group intended to give children a more explicit place in the Bill. She argued that waiting times could affect children’s development and that children’s performance data needed to be measured separately. She said: “Only 48% of children and young people are seen within 18 weeks for community services”. Other amendments concerned children’s outcomes, adopted children’s NHS numbers, communication with families when a child is near death, training for health professionals on children’s digital environments, digital medical technology and reporting emerging digital harms. Baroness Penn proposed guidance and training for health professionals and consideration of digital environments in healthy-child reviews and neighbourhood health plans; Baroness Barran proposed safety and risk assessments for digital medical technologies used by children.

Baroness Merron said that the Bill’s duties applied universally and that existing waiting-time standards covered children and young people. She referred to NHS England’s publication of elective waiting-list data with demographic breakdowns and information on children’s community-service waits. She said that existing guidance and surveillance arrangements addressed aspects of digital harms, while new statutory reporting systems would require clear criteria and co-ordination with existing regulation. On adopted children’s NHS numbers, she said work was continuing and agreed to meet the Bishop of Leicester; she also said that a fixed statutory rule could remove flexibility where a new number might be needed to protect a child’s identity. She agreed to write to Baroness Penn and said she would take account of Baroness Kidron’s request for a way for clinicians to record concerns. Amendment 66 was withdrawn, Amendments 67 to 69 were not moved and Clause 16 was agreed.

### Voluntary, community and social enterprise organisations, stalking and anonymous access
Baroness Barker’s Amendment 70 concerned the involvement of voluntary, community and social enterprise (VCSE) organisations in commissioning, including the possible reservation or prioritisation of contracts. Supporters described VCSE organisations as providers with knowledge of underserved communities and as sources of innovation. Baroness Royall and Lord Russell also spoke to Amendment 91 on a public-health role for the NHS in tackling stalking, drawing on a multi-agency model in Cheshire. Baroness Royall said: “where health services become active partners, risk is managed, victims are better supported and money is saved”. Amendment 163 concerned anonymous access and confidentiality for sexual-health and related services.

Baroness Merron said that the provider selection regime already allowed ICBs to take account of social value and to award contracts directly to well-performing voluntary providers, but argued that a statutory preference by provider type could conflict with provider neutrality. On stalking, she referred to forthcoming multi-agency statutory guidance and said that ICBs were expected to commission specialist stalking services; she considered a separate statutory health duty potentially duplicative. She said that existing legal protections covered anonymous-access providers and that detailed arrangements would address confidentiality, and agreed to write to Baroness Barker to clarify the position. Baroness Barker withdrew Amendment 70. Schedule 2 and Clause 17 were agreed, while Amendments 71 to 74 were not moved. Amendments 91 and 92 were subsequently not moved.

### Adult gender services, data and detransition provision
Baroness Davies proposed national accountability for adult gender dysphoria services, mandatory outcome data, a single waiting list, reporting on the Levy review, compulsory data sharing for approved research and a specialist detransition service. She focused on the absence of outcome data and accountability following the planned abolition of NHS England, quoting the Levy review’s conclusion that provision “does not meet the service specification, nor does it meet the needs of the current patient cohort”. Other speakers supported national oversight and reliable data. Baroness Barker said that data collection should be neutral and respect patients’ rights, while Lord Scriven supported evidence-led care, dignity and privacy and asked who would be accountable after NHS England’s abolition.

Baroness Merron said that Ministers, particularly the Secretary of State, remained accountable to Parliament, that an updated national service specification for adult gender services would be published, and that commissioned adult services were expected to collaborate with the Cass data-linkage study. She said that NHS England was developing a clinical pathway for people considering detransition, and that the Department of Health and Social Care would support that work after abolition. She opposed Amendment 279QQ, saying that it would require restrictions on particular medicines and remove an opportunity for independent expert advice before a decision was made. Baroness Davies withdrew Amendment 75. Clauses 18 to 20, Clause 21 and the clause relating to ICB performance assessments were agreed, while Amendments 76 to 80 were not moved. Amendments 275 and 276 were discussed but no formal outcome for them is stated in the transcript.

### Oral questions: private parking, electricity, official development assistance and cyber security
During Oral Questions, Baroness Blake said that the private parking code of practice was under parliamentary scrutiny and that, subject to there being no objections, the relevant protections would be brought into effect after the scrutiny period. She said: “There will then be 21 days to bring in that part of the code of practice”. She also said that a further consultation would cover remaining Competition and Markets Authority recommendations and debt-recovery fees.

On electricity demand, Baroness Curran referred to National Energy System Operator planning and a strategic spatial energy plan covering generation and associated networks for data centres, transport electrification and other demand. On official development assistance, Lord Wood said that bilateral allocations would not be changed and that increased contributions to multilateral institutions, including the World Bank’s International Development Association, would compensate for bilateral reductions to a significant extent. On public-sector cyber security, Baroness Lloyd referred to supplier-concentration risks after the Microsoft outage and said that the national cloud infrastructure programme would seek to diversify suppliers, with formal procurement due to start by the end of the year. She also referred to the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, lessons from the Synnovis incident and the maintenance of key operational services during the outage. No formal division or other decision was recorded for these questions.

### Conversion practices and clinical exploration
When the Health Bill Committee resumed, Baroness Cass spoke to Amendment 279QP concerning the assessment and treatment of gender-related distress in children and young people. She distinguished sexual orientation from gender incongruence or dysphoria and argued that careful exploration should not be treated as conversion practice. Lord Singh supported the amendment, arguing that the draft legislation could create uncertainty around legitimate clinical assessment and ordinary parental conversations. He said: “Distress alone is not evidence of wrongdoing”. Lord Hunt and Lord Scriven also raised concerns about how clinicians would understand the limits of the proposed legislation, while Baroness Barker supported reliable data but questioned assumptions behind some amendments.

Baroness Merron said that children and young people questioning their gender identity deserved compassionate care tailored to their circumstances, and referred to the continuing implementation of the Cass review and the new regional services. The Committee also discussed Amendment 279QQ on prescribing masculinising and feminising hormones to under-18s. The Minister said that existing regulators had powers to investigate unsafe prescribing and that the Government were considering evidence on private and online prescribing. No formal outcome for Amendments 279QP or 279QQ is stated in the transcript.

### ICB membership, primary care, public health and children’s leads
The Committee considered whether ICBs should include representatives of non-mayoral strategic authorities, primary care, public health, allied health professions and children and young people. Supporters argued that boards needed access to practical and clinical expertise. Baroness Gerada argued that primary care involvement should be a duty rather than depend on a chair’s discretion, while Baroness Finlay and Lord Bradley described the contribution of allied health professionals to prevention, rehabilitation and community care. Baroness Cass proposed a statutory ICB executive lead for children and young people aged nought to 25, saying that implementation of the existing role was patchy.

Baroness Merron opposed further detailed membership requirements, citing local flexibility and the need for effective-sized boards. She said guidance would provide for participation by non-mayoral strategic authorities, that ICBs must obtain appropriate professional advice, and that existing duties required engagement with primary care providers. She confirmed that guidance requiring a named executive lead for children and young people would be maintained and reissued, with ICB websites expected to identify the lead up to age 25. Amendment 81 was withdrawn, Amendments 81A to 84 were not moved and Clause 22 was agreed.

### Women’s centres and integration between health and local government
Lord Hunt’s Amendment 85 sought a statutory duty for ICBs to co-operate with women’s centres, particularly in supporting women experiencing multiple disadvantage. The group also discussed broader duties of co-operation and the proposed removal of integrated care partnerships and integrated care strategies. Supporters described women’s centres as trusted, trauma-informed routes into health, social care and criminal-justice services. Lord Hunt said that partnerships with the NHS were “patchy and depends on local priorities and relationships”. Baroness Bennett supported duties promoting collaboration and continuity of care, while Lord Redwood cautioned that statutory collaboration could blur responsibility and leave gaps.

Baroness Merron said that existing duties on reducing inequalities, involving voluntary-sector partners and partnership working, together with the Women’s Health Strategy and a proposed women’s voice partnership, were sufficient. She said that a mandatory relationship with women’s centres could reduce ICBs’ flexibility to design services around local needs. She said that Clause 24 would remove the requirement for ICBs and local authorities to form an integrated care partnership and prepare an integrated care strategy, while health and well-being boards and neighbourhood health plans would provide a more flexible framework for collaboration. Lord Hunt withdrew Amendment 85 and Amendments 86 to 89 were not moved. The relevant clauses were agreed later, after the musculoskeletal-health debate.

### Healthcare for people in prison and under probation supervision
Lord Foster’s Amendment 90 sought explicit ICB duties to improve health outcomes for people in custody or under probation supervision, maintain continuity of care as people moved through custody and the community, and commission mental-health, substance-misuse, addiction and other specialist services. He argued that treatment often failed to follow people after release, saying: “Essential information may not reach the receiving healthcare provider, and it might be that nobody is clear about who is responsible”. Other speakers raised women’s healthcare in prison, terminal illness, cancer treatment, disability, social care and hospice support.

Baroness Merron referred to probation health pilots, updated health and justice service specifications, prison reception-screening work and services such as RECONNECT. She said that ICBs would be required to meet the reasonable health needs of people in detention when commissioning responsibility transferred, and that national rules would identify responsibility when people moved between ICB areas. Lord Foster said that the response did not explain how responsibility would be enforced, and withdrew Amendment 90. Amendments 91 and 92 were not moved.

### Inflammatory bowel disease
Lord Hunt moved Amendment 93 on behalf of Baroness Young of Old Scone. It would have required ICBs to monitor variation in the quality and outcomes of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) care and publish annual reports against national standards. He described geographical variation despite existing guidance and improvement programmes, and said that IBD was a lifelong, relapsing condition affecting more than half a million people across the United Kingdom. Baroness Pidgeon supported a national mechanism for identifying variation and implementing best practice, while Lord Evans supported the objectives but questioned whether every condition required a separate statutory strategy and reporting framework.

Baroness Hyde said that NICE guidance, the NHS England Getting It Right First Time gastroenterology programme and NHS RightCare already supported improvements and the identification of variation. The Government therefore preferred implementation of existing standards and programmes to a new condition-specific statutory strategy, which they said could duplicate existing arrangements and increase administrative burdens. Lord Hunt withdrew Amendment 93.

### Musculoskeletal health, fracture prevention, frailty and rehabilitation
Baroness Hayter moved Amendment 94B on musculoskeletal health and spoke to Amendment 206 on an implementation plan for universal fracture prevention services by 2030. Supporters argued that fracture liaison services could reduce avoidable fractures, hospital use and social-care costs. Baroness Gerada also spoke to Amendment 245 on integrated frailty pathways, while Baroness Finlay spoke about rehabilitation and allied health services. Baroness Hayter said: “Only a quarter of fragility fractures in England are identified by prevention services”.

The Government opposed additional condition-specific statutory duties. Baroness Hyde said that ICBs already had responsibility for commissioning musculoskeletal services and that existing guidance supported prevention, diagnosis and treatment. She confirmed a commitment to roll out fracture liaison services by 2030, referred to investment in DEXA scanners and offered a meeting before Report, but did not commit to the requested statutory implementation plan. She also referred to work on frailty, rehabilitation and intermediate care, including the Better Care Fund. Amendment 94B was withdrawn; Clauses 23 and 24 were subsequently agreed. Amendments 206, 245 and 279QK were discussed but no formal outcome for them is stated in the transcript.

### Neighbourhood health plans
Baroness Tyler’s Amendment 95 sought to require neighbourhood health plans to set out proposed shifts towards prevention and community care, commissioning and workforce capacity, resources, financial incentives, continuity of services and outcome assessment. Other amendments concerned unpaid carers, well-being, people with long-term or fluctuating conditions, neurological conditions, health inequalities, co-production, rehabilitation and retaining joint local health and well-being strategies. Baroness Scott referred to the implementation of updated guidance for severe ME and said: “only 28% of NHS trusts and integrated care boards had implemented the updated NICE guidelines”. Baroness Gerada argued that plans should address wider determinants such as housing, poverty and air quality and involve communities that conventional consultation might not reach.

Baroness Merron said that neighbourhood health plans would be prepared jointly by local authorities and their partner ICBs through health and well-being boards and informed by local population needs. She said that the plans would cover prevention, health inequalities, wider determinants and local priorities, and that guidance would expect involvement from VCSE organisations and clinicians, including allied health professionals. She opposed duplicating joint strategies or prescribing individual conditions in primary legislation, preferring guidance and local flexibility. Amendment 95 was withdrawn, Amendments 96 to 100A and 101 to 102 were not moved, and Clause 25 and Clauses 26 and 27 were agreed.

### Special Health Authorities and NHS England’s regional teams
The Committee debated a proposed Special Health Authority intended to host NHS England’s seven regional teams during the abolition and restructuring of NHS England. Lord Scriven’s probing Amendment 104ZA sought a report covering functions, costs, accounting officers, staffing, savings, accountability and the authority’s lifespan. He said: “We do not know how many staff will transfer, we do not know its budget, we do not know what it will lead for, and we do not know who will lead it”. Lord Stevens and the Earl Howe supported greater clarity on the authority’s powers, size, budget and accountability.

Baroness Merron said that the authority would be established under the existing statutory process, with its general functions, governance arrangements and appointments set out in instruments subject to parliamentary scrutiny. She described it as a smaller body with a narrow regional remit, without a national policy or resource-allocation role, and said that it would be directly accountable to the Secretary of State. She declined to set an arbitrary end date because the future devolution model was still developing, but said that the arrangement was intended to be temporary and that Parliament would be kept informed. Amendments 103 and 104 were agreed and Clause 28, as amended, and Clause 29 were agreed. Amendment 104ZA was withdrawn, and the House adjourned at 10 pm.
