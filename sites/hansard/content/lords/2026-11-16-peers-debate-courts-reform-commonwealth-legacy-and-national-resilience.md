## High-Level Summary
The sitting covered questions on broadcast impartiality, drone threats, farm profitability, security for Jewish community buildings and the resilience implications of a Microsoft outage. The House then considered a Commons Statement on the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting, including trade, climate resilience, democratic participation, development, debt and dialogue on the legacy of slavery. Most of the legislative business was the second day in Committee on the Courts and Tribunals Bill, covering magistrates’ appeals, victims’ protections, jury directions, family-court safeguards, court oversight and listing. The Committee agreed clauses and schedules, amendments were withdrawn or not moved, and the Bill was reported without amendment before the House adjourned at 11.39 pm.

## Detailed Summary
### Ofcom and news impartiality
Baroness Gill questioned Baroness Mackenzie of Sherwood about elected politicians presenting news and current-affairs programmes, citing the Demos comparison and the distinction between news and current affairs. The Minister said the Government were concerned about programmes that could reasonably be interpreted as news or current affairs and were considering stronger rules: “There is a clear rationale to strengthen the rules in the UK”. She also acknowledged that the distinction was not clearly made in the Communications Act 2003 and said the Government would report further on possible action as soon as possible.

Peers also asked about a statement by Nigel Farage on GB News, Ofcom’s independence, a Local News Fund grant to Bylines Network, politicians presenting entertainment programmes and BBC public-trust data. The Minister said Ofcom was independent and should be retained, undertook to respond on the grant relationships, and promised Baroness Stowell a reply on the BBC data: “I will absolutely ensure that I do that before the end of this week”.

### Risks from targeted drone attacks
Lord Farmer asked about civilian vulnerability to hostile drone attacks, including around military installations and political buildings. Lord Hanson of Flint said the potential for hostile drone use was increasing because drones could be used for surveillance, disruption, weapons and crime, and that the Home Office was leading cross-government work to strengthen resilience. The Government said the threat was kept under continuous review and cited £750 million for counter-drone capabilities and £470 million for military-site security: “we have supported £750 million for counterdrone capabilities”.

Peers raised deterrence, foreign and domestic threats, cross-departmental responsibility, police resources, drone identification, supply chains, prison security and resilience exercises. The Minister described co-operation between the Ministry of Defence, the Home Office, the police and United States Air Force personnel during the Lakenheath incident. He also cited investment in counter-drone security at high-risk prisons and undertook to examine matters outside his direct responsibility. No further timetable for the outstanding responses was stated in the transcript.

### Farm profitability
Baroness Hayman of Ullock set out the Government’s Farming Roadmap 2050 and measures on supply-chain fairness, market access, planning, regulation, productivity, equipment, innovation and input costs. Lord Blencathra argued for lower taxation and regulation, protection from low-standard imports and more stable rules. The Minister defended a long-term plan providing “clearer, fairer routes to market” and access to technology, skills and supply chains.

Peers discussed the Groceries Code, commodity speculation, rural connectivity, local food procurement, abattoirs, the Sustainable Farming Incentive (SFI), co-operatives and tenant farmers. The Minister confirmed that an SFI scheme was planned for 2027 but did not give an application date, supported investment in horticulture and co-operation, and highlighted longer tenancies as important for tenant-farmer investment, citing examples of 15-year-plus farm business tenancies.

### Security of Jewish community buildings
Baroness Deech asked whether funding was sufficient for structural security measures at Jewish community buildings. Lord Hanson of Flint cited £32.4 million for protective security in 2026-27 and a further £4 million announced in April. He said future multi-year funding could not be promised before the next spending review: “I cannot give a commitment about future years until we have the CSR later in this Parliament”. He also referred to intelligence and police support.

The discussion covered antisemitism, criticism of Israel, threats linked to Iran, citizenship and extradition, and consistency of protection across the UK. The Minister rejected the suggestion that the Jewish community had brought attacks upon itself and said he would ensure that the issue of protection across the devolved Administrations was examined. He also cited an additional £250 million for police work with Jewish communities over three years.

### Microsoft outage and national resilience
Baroness Lloyd of Effra said the Microsoft outage caused widespread disruption but that there were no major critical-service impacts; 999, accident and emergency departments, transport, borders and basic connectivity remained operational. Baroness Kidron and other Peers questioned reliance on a small number of overseas technology suppliers and sought a sovereign-capability strategy and an assessment of the outage’s cost. The Minister said the Government were working to “understand and manage supplier concentration and strategic dependencies”.

Further questions addressed subsea cables, data centres, digital television and landline switch-off, parliamentary email, cyber-incident plans, possible penalties and sovereign artificial-intelligence capability. The Government said lessons would be learned, supported further data-centre construction and said the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill would bring data centres, managed service providers and large load controllers within scope. Two specific questions on departmental plans and a seven-day outage exercise were taken away for a detailed response.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill: appeals from magistrates’ courts
The Committee debated Clause 6 and Schedule 2, which would replace the automatic right of appeal from magistrates’ courts to the Crown Court with a permission-based appeal focused on the safety of the conviction or sentence. Lord Murray of Blidworth and other Peers argued that appeals against conviction represented only 0.4% of the Crown Court workload and that 56% of contested appeals succeeded: “56% of those contested appeals were successful”. They said the proposed permission stage could increase Crown Court work, place greater demands on magistrates and disadvantage unrepresented defendants, particularly before the Law Commission reported.

The Government argued that full rehearings could cause avoidable trauma to victims and witnesses and that a permission stage would provide a more targeted system. Baroness Smith of Cluny said the reform would provide “appropriate safeguards for defendants while sparing victims and witnesses from avoidable trauma”. The Government said recording, transcription, training and administrative support would be provided, but opposed a mandatory oral renewal process for refused permission applications. Lord Murray withdrew his opposition; Clause 6 and Schedule 2 were agreed, while Amendment 37 and Amendments 39 to 47 were not moved.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill: magistrate training and allowances
Baroness Hamwee’s Amendment 38 sought confirmation that magistrates had completed relevant training, consultation on allowances and a written statement before reforms commenced. Supporters referred to increased workloads, more serious cases, shortages of legal advisers and the need for preparation. The Government described measures to expand capacity through additional sitting days, investment in the court estate, recruitment and training. The Minister said that sentencing powers would not be extended until sufficient magistrates, legal advisers and district judges had completed the necessary training: “sentencing powers will not be extended until sufficient magistrates, legal advisers and district judges have completed the necessary training”.

On allowances, the Minister said magistrates were volunteers who could claim eligible expenses and financial loss, while Clause 18 would make it easier to update reimbursable expenses. Baroness Hamwee said she was not satisfied but withdrew Amendment 38.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill: expert evidence, victims’ rights and special measures
Amendments in this group sought regulation of expert witnesses, broader protections for victims, a right to review certain Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) decisions to discontinue proceedings, and presumptive access to screens, live links or recorded evidence. Supporters argued that unregulated experts could provide unreliable evidence and that routine special measures should not require vulnerable witnesses to make repeated applications. Lord Russell of Liverpool also sought to extend the CPS’s ability to discontinue and reinstate proceedings before trial, referring to the West Midlands victims’ review pilot.

The Government relied on existing Criminal Procedure Rules and judicial control over expert evidence, saying that the rules “provide proportionate standards for judges to consider when determining the admissibility of expert evidence”. It rejected a general extension of the elevated evidential tests in Clauses 8 to 10 and opposed automatic entitlement to special measures because individual needs, fairness and facilities varied. The Minister confirmed that the early victims’ right-to-review scheme had been extended to every CPS area in England and Wales from 13 July. Amendment 47A was withdrawn; Amendments 47B and 52B were not moved; and Clauses 11 to 15 were agreed.

### Jury equity and advocates’ conduct
Viscount Hailsham’s Amendment 48 would have protected advocates from sanction for reminding juries that they could not be compelled to convict. Supporters described jury equity as a principle allowing a jury to acquit according to conscience, while opponents argued that the amendment’s broad wording could create confusion and undermine judicial control of trials. Lord Pannick and others also said legislation would be premature while contempt proceedings connected with a recent case remained pending: “the judicial decision that we are awaiting may establish that counsel has the right to address the jury on jury equity”.

The Government opposed the amendment as a matter of principle, stating that jurors must apply the law as directed by the judge and that advocates should not encourage verdicts based on conscience rather than law: “it would be inappropriate for advocates to remind juries that they can reach verdicts according to their conscience”. Viscount Hailsham acknowledged that he lacked majority support and withdrew the amendment; Amendment 49 was not moved.

### Directions on myths and stereotypes in trials
Baroness Levitt’s Amendment 50 would have required judges in sexual-offence and domestic-abuse trials to give directions on relevant myths and stereotypes, normally at the beginning and again in the summing-up, unless unnecessary in the particular case. Supporters said discretionary guidance was inconsistently applied and that directions should help juries assess evidence rather than assumptions about reporting, injury, distress or continued contact. Opponents accepted the importance of such directions but argued that the Crown Court Compendium, judicial training and appellate guidance already required judges to address them where appropriate, and that statutory instructions risked undermining judicial independence.

The Government did not accept the amendment but announced that the Judicial College would update the Crown Court Compendium so that “directions should be given in all sexual offence trials” at the outset and when summing up, subject to judicial discretion, and would update training. The Minister also said that the Bar Standards Board was developing specialist standards for barristers in rape and serious sexual offence cases. Baroness Levitt welcomed the offer of a meeting before Report but withdrew the amendment; Amendments 51 and 52 were not moved.

### Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting statement
The House considered a Commons Statement on the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in Antigua and Barbuda. The Statement described initiatives on trade and investment, climate resilience, clean energy, debt, democratic resilience, protection of parliamentarians from online abuse and support for Jamaica’s reconstruction. It also set out a proposed, partner-designed dialogue on the legacy of the transatlantic slave trade, while stating that “our position on financial reparations has not changed”.

Peers questioned the handling of reparations, modern slavery, Chinese influence, development-funding reductions, debt relief, Commonwealth participation, youth engagement, democracy, climate change and violence against women in public life. The responding Minister said the structured dialogue would be jointly designed with Caribbean and African partners and could consider a formal apology, but not financial reparations. She said the UK supported the Commonwealth Secretary-General’s work on democratic resilience and would continue work with Australia on online abuse directed at women parliamentarians.

### Private prosecutions and alleged false accusations
Baroness Brinton’s Amendment 52A sought a report on safeguards where a person is privately prosecuted by someone whom they had previously accused of an offence, following a report by the Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women. She argued that the process could remove protections and anonymity from an alleged victim and could be used as retaliation or coercive control through the courts.

The Government said private prosecutions were an established route to justice but accepted the need for scrutiny. The Director of Public Prosecutions can take over prosecutions, magistrates may refuse vexatious summonses, and the Government offered to explore stronger guidance with the Attorney-General’s Office and the CPS: “The Minister, my noble friend Lord Lemos, will be happy to explore with the Attorney-General’s Office and the Crown Prosecution Service how existing guidance on private prosecutions could be strengthened further”. The Minister also reaffirmed statutory anonymity for complainants in sexual-offence cases and offered a meeting; Amendment 52A was withdrawn.

### Criminal Division of the High Court
Baroness Levitt’s Amendment 52C proposed a Criminal Division of the High Court to recognise criminal judging as a distinct specialism and improve the development and deployment of criminal expertise. Supporters referred to the distinctive demands of criminal cases, including jury management, complex evidence and vulnerable participants. Baroness Butler-Sloss opposed the proposal, saying that the existing King’s Bench arrangements, leadership of the criminal courts and Court of Appeal Criminal Division functioned effectively: “the Criminal Division works perfectly well within the King’s Bench Division”.

The Government said judicial deployment and allocation were matters for the judiciary under section 7 of the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, and that structural change should be developed with the senior judiciary. The Minister noted that the amendment had not identified an operational problem requiring change. Baroness Levitt said the amendment was intended to begin a wider discussion and withdrew it.

### Parental involvement and child welfare
The Committee debated Clause 16, which would repeal the statutory presumption of parental involvement in family proceedings. Opponents argued that the presumption was rebuttable, supported children’s relationships with both parents where safe and was important to families outside court proceedings; they proposed a parental-relationship test and evaluation of outcomes. Supporters argued that the presumption could reinforce a pro-contact culture, place the burden on abuse survivors to establish risk and fail to reflect developments in understanding coercive control and post-separation abuse.

The Government said repeal would require an open-minded assessment of each child’s best interests, while safe parental involvement would remain available: “Repealing the presumption means courts will adopt from the outset an open-minded inquiry into a child’s best interests”. The Minister linked repeal with child-focused courts, earlier information-gathering, risk assessment, multi-agency working and attention to children’s views. He said monitoring was already under way, £500,000 had been committed to further research on domestic abuse in family courts, and updated family procedure rules would restrict unregulated experts in relevant children proceedings. Clause 16 was agreed and Amendments 53 to 53D were not moved.

### Court administration, independent review and prisoner transport
Amendment 53F proposed an independent inspectorate for criminal court administration, with particular attention to efficiency, facilities and victims’ and witnesses’ experience. Amendments 54 to 61 sought a broader independent review of the Bill’s effects, including disability, mental disorder, participation, legal representation and formal Government responses. Amendment 62 sought monthly publication of late prisoner-transport data and review of contracts where delays exceeded a specified threshold.

The Government said His Majesty’s Courts and Tribunals Service was already subject to oversight by the Government Internal Audit Agency, the National Audit Office, parliamentary committees and joint inspectorates, and that the National Audit Office had announced a new study. It said Clause 20 was focused on racial and socioeconomic disparities but allowed flexibility in the terms of reference, with wider evaluation supporting scrutiny. On prisoner transport, the Minister cited existing performance reporting, an oversight board and bus-lane access initiatives, but opposed a statutory trigger based on one metric. Amendment 53F was withdrawn; Amendments 54 to 61 and 62 were not moved; and Clause 20 was agreed.

### Criminal court listing and sunset clause
Lord Wolfson’s Amendment 62A sought a review of criminal court listing practices to identify and spread efficient approaches, including possible use of artificial intelligence. He cited different backlog performance between courts and asked whether Ministers should have a role in setting priorities between categories of cases while preserving judicial control over individual cases. The Government replied that “Individual listing decisions are made under judicial responsibility” and that a national listing framework and an HMCTS improvement programme, including AI tools, were already in place. The amendment was withdrawn.

Viscount Hailsham’s Amendment 76 proposed that Clauses 1 to 6 should expire after the Crown Court backlog had remained below 40,000 for four quarters. The Government said the reforms addressed long-term structural pressures rather than being temporary measures: “These reforms are not short-term emergency measures”. The amendment was withdrawn. Clauses 21 to 27 were agreed, the Bill was reported without amendment and the House adjourned at 11.39 pm.
