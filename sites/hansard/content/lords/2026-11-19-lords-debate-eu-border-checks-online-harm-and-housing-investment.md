## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords considered four oral questions on nuclear deterrence, flood-risk modelling, access to clinical trials and investment in young people. The main debate concerned international approaches to removing sanitary and phytosanitary checks at European Union borders, with Members divided between equivalence or mutual recognition and dynamic alignment; the take-note motion was agreed. A short debate examined legal remedies for children facing imminent online harm, including existing criminal and safeguarding routes and possible further redress measures. The House then debated investment in social housing, shared ownership and zero-carbon homes; the motion was agreed and the House adjourned at 5.15 pm.

## Detailed Summary
### Unilateral nuclear disarmament
Lord Coaker said that the Government assessed unilateral nuclear disarmament would remove the United Kingdom’s ultimate security guarantee, weaken NATO’s deterrence posture and increase strategic risks. He said the Government supported gradual multilateral disarmament and would maintain the independent deterrent for as long as the global security situation required: “The only route to ridding the world of nuclear weapons is through gradual multilateral disarmament”. Lord Soames and other Members asked about deterrent renewal, submarine availability and the Dreadnought programme. Lord Coaker cited investment of £64 billion between 2026-27 and 2029-30, and said Dreadnought submarines were expected to enter service through the 2030s.

The questions also covered the United Kingdom and French deterrents’ relationship with NATO, the United States’ role, the balance between nuclear and conventional capabilities, and the planned purchase of 12 F-35A aircraft. Lord Coaker said the F-35A would provide a tactical nuclear capability in response to threats from other tactical nuclear weapons. No division or further decision was recorded in the transcript.

### Environment Agency flood-risk models
Baroness Hayman of Ullock said Environment Agency flood-risk models supported flood-risk management, investment and planning decisions, emergency planning and response, and public information. Insurers and lenders used them alongside other evidence, including commercial risk models. She said the mapping drew on more than 1,000 detailed river and coastal models, more than 200 local surface-water models and information on flood defences. Members asked about property-level data, the effect of flood risk on valuations, insurance and mortgages, the future of Flood Re, development on functional flood plains and the proposed water regulator.

The Minister said Flood Re was intended to complete its mission by 2039. She referred to Build Back Better and flood performance certificates as measures intended to improve household resilience and inform insurance and lending decisions. The Government had no plans to extend Flood Re to businesses, but would continue working with the British Insurance Brokers’ Association on support for business flood cover. She said further details of the proposed water regulator were not yet available, and that data collection was an issue for further consideration with the Environment Agency. No division was recorded.

### Access to clinical trials
Baroness Merron said that, in the first three months of the year, all eligible commercial clinical trials had been set up in fewer than 150 days. She attributed this to reducing bureaucracy, increasing National Health Service accountability and investing in research infrastructure. Members asked whether similar improvements would apply to non-commercial and charity-funded trials, and raised access for people with rare cancers, people from diverse or disadvantaged backgrounds, and patients outside major teaching hospitals.

The Minister said the Government wanted all eligible non-commercial studies to be set up within 150 days or fewer and would publish an update early the following year. She referred to dedicated leadership on rare cancers, stronger clinical academic career pathways, registration through the NHS app, 35 commercial research delivery centres and 14 primary-care sites. On a single patient record, she said its main focus would remain enabling information to travel with the patient, while acknowledging the potential for future uses. No division was recorded.

### Investment in young people: Milburn review
Baroness Smith of Malvern said that the Government had begun with a £2.5 billion youth guarantee supporting nearly 1 million young people, was committing to the review’s proposals for an opportunity and support guarantee, and would respond fully in a White Paper the following year. Members discussed the need for national consensus involving employers, education providers, local and strategic authorities and the voluntary and community sector. They also raised jobs, early intervention, numeracy, social skills, mental-health support and regional differences in employment opportunities.

The Minister said resources would be made available for local youth opportunity services bringing together employment, skills, health and other support. She referred to additional post-16 support and a new level 1 maths stepping-stone qualification. On mental health, she said the Department of Health was developing a plan and that the Government would consider how support for young people should be covered in the youth opportunity service and more broadly. She also cited employers’ pledge of 250,000 jobs for young people who were neither earning nor learning. No division was recorded.

### Sanitary and phytosanitary checks at EU borders
Lord Lilley moved the motion that the House take note of international approaches to removing sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) checks at European Union borders. He and other critics argued for equivalence or mutual recognition, referring to the World Trade Organization SPS Agreement and the EU-New Zealand arrangement. They argued that dynamic alignment could constrain the United Kingdom’s regulatory choices, impose costs on domestic producers and affect imports from non-EU countries. Lord Lilley cited provisions requiring equivalent measures to be accepted and stating that SPS measures should not be used as a disguised restriction on international trade. Lord Frost criticised accepting EU rules without United Kingdom representation and called for a fuller cost-benefit analysis. Lord Verdirame and Lord Roborough also questioned compatibility with the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership and other trade agreements.

Supporters, including Baroness Chapman, Lord Hannay, Lord Murphy and Baroness Grender, argued that dynamic alignment would remove paperwork, certification fees and border checks, improve access to the United Kingdom’s largest agri-food export market and reduce friction affecting Northern Ireland. Baroness Chapman described the approach as “a rational exercise of our sovereignty”, while Lord Murphy said businesses in Northern Ireland had urged the Government to pursue an SPS agreement. Responding for the Government, Baroness Hayman said the proposed agreement was expected to remove export health certificates, 100% paperwork checks and up to 30% physical checks for specified British goods, and could increase exports of major agricultural commodities to the EU by around 16%. She said implementation was expected in spring 2028, that Parliament would have an opportunity to scrutinise the implementing powers, and that an European partnership Bill would be laid shortly after the agreement was signed. The Government said the arrangement would not, in principle, breach World Trade Organization obligations, would preserve biosecurity, and could increase GDP by up to 0.14% annually in the long run, equivalent to £5.1 billion a year. The motion was agreed.

### Online harm: legal remedies
Baroness Berger and other Members argued that families lacked a rapid, independent route to require platforms to act when children faced imminent online harm. They called for injunctive relief, formal standing for parents and guardians, human safeguarding reviews, individual redress and earlier consideration of an ombudsman. Baroness Berger summarised the position as follows: “There is no individual right of action”. Members also discussed sextortion, self-harm material, chatbot grooming, unclear platform complaints procedures, and the timetable for Ofcom’s review of user redress.

Lord Hanson, speaking for the Government, said that parents should contact the police and safeguarding agencies where a child was in immediate danger, and outlined existing Family Law Act, criminal-law and Online Safety Act provisions. He confirmed that Ofcom’s review of reporting and complaints was planned for 2029, while saying that the Government had not ruled out additional redress measures. He said regulations were intended to be laid by the end of the year, with restrictions expected to come into force in spring 2027, including a ban on livestreaming for under-16s and restrictions on unknown adults contacting children. He also said the Government intended to bring currently unregulated artificial-intelligence services within the Online Safety Act’s illegal-contact duties. In a subsequent exchange, he accepted that Ofcom did not handle individual complaints but said complaints could inform collective regulatory action. No division was recorded.

### Housing market: social housing, shared ownership and zero-carbon homes
Lord Stoneham moved a motion on the role of investment in social housing, shared ownership and zero-carbon homes in addressing housing-market challenges. Members generally supported increasing social-housing supply, but differed on delivery priorities, regulation and the operation of shared ownership. Several argued that housing associations could deliver quickly using existing capacity and private finance. Lord Stoneham said that, at their current rate of construction, housing associations could build more than 400,000 social homes over 10 years, exceeding the Government’s 300,000-home target. Lord Fuller and Lord Jamieson raised concerns about tenant protections, service charges, lease terms and the imbalance between shared owners and registered social landlords. Members also linked secure housing with homelessness prevention, employment, health and energy affordability, and considered whether zero-carbon standards could affect viability and delivery.

Baroness Taylor of Stevenage said that the Government remained committed to delivering 1.5 million homes during the Parliament, investing more than £40 billion through the Social and Affordable Homes Programme, supporting delivery through the National Housing Bank and protecting existing social housing. She said that shared-ownership reforms would address long-term affordability, resale, staircasing, service-charge transparency and lease extensions. The future homes and buildings standards were to come into force in March 2027, while the Warm Homes Plan was intended to support up to 5 million homes by 2030. The Minister also cited £874 million for a rough-sleeping programme and more than £4.4 billion for homelessness and rough sleeping over three years. The motion was agreed and the House adjourned at 5.15 pm.
