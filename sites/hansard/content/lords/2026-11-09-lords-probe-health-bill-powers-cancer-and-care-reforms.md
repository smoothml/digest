## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords questioned Ministers on reform of the World Trade Organization, women’s pain, water-supply resilience and the alleged misuse of Interpol procedures by Russia. It also considered jury directions in rape and serious sexual offence trials and agreed a procedural motion concerning the Church Appointments (Repeal of Discriminatory Provision) Bill. Committee stage of the Health Bill covered ministerial powers over integrated care boards, the balance between public and independent provision, cancer outcomes, social care, continuity of care and primary-care services. Several amendments were withdrawn or not moved, while Ministers offered further information or discussions on a number of issues; the transcript records no final decision on several remaining amendments.

## Detailed Summary
### World Trade Organization reform
Lord Lamont of Lerwick asked what proposals the Government had to improve the effectiveness of the World Trade Organization (WTO). Lord Leong said the WTO needed reform, citing improved transparency, stronger disciplines on harmful trading practices and more flexible arrangements among groups of members: “The WTO has failed to keep pace with trading realities and must reform.” He said that the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia were among the 61 members using the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA), which provided an interim route for resolving disputes while the appellate body remained paralysed.

Peers also raised rules-based trade, support for small and medium-sized enterprises using the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership, China’s subsidies and alleged unfair competition, consensus decision-making, steel tariffs, the UK-US economic prosperity deal and the position of least-developed countries. Lord Leong said the Government would continue working with the United States, China, the European Union, CPTPP members and countries in the global South on reform, and would continue using plurilateral and other interim arrangements. In response to Lord Redwood, he did not state that new or higher tariffs would be ruled out; he referred instead to resolving subsidy and dumping issues through the existing system. No further decision was recorded.

### Women and pain
Baroness Nargund asked about research reporting that women reported more pain than men at the body sites studied. Baroness Merron described the issue as a systemic failure and said that patient-reported measures would be introduced in gynaecology services, alongside testing payments linked to women’s feedback, including their experience of pain: “Patient-reported measures will be introduced in gynaecology services.”

The Minister referred to updated National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance, professional education, the Getting It Right First Time chronic-pain workstream, Jess’s rule and Martha’s rule, and a new multiprofessional capability framework for women’s healthcare. She also cited more than £4.3 million for research into endometriosis and pain management, commitments to reduce waiting times, a multidisciplinary model for women’s health centres, and investment in fracture liaison services and DEXA scanners. She said that payments could be withheld and invested in improvements where patient feedback showed that services were not meeting the expected standard. No specific sanctions for individual healthcare professionals were stated in the transcript.

### Reservoirs and water-supply resilience
Baroness McIntosh of Pickering asked about reservoir levels and the resilience of water supplies. Lord Katz said there was no immediate risk to public supplies and reported that, as at 3 November, reservoir storage in England was 60.1%, 17% below the average for the time of year, with eight reservoirs classed as exceptionally low: “there is no immediate risk to public water supplies.” The National Drought Group was meeting weekly, and the Environment Agency was prioritising public supplies in areas facing severe drought.

The Government outlined work to accelerate abstraction-permit decisions, improve digital systems, review high-flow abstraction, trial dynamic abstraction, remove planning barriers and make up to £15 million available for on-farm reservoirs. The discussion also covered leakage, nature-based solutions and water demand from data centres. Lord Katz cited a 30% interim leakage-reduction target by 2032, an expectation that Ofwat would challenge companies to halve leakage by 2050, a 12% reduction in reported leakage in 2024-25 and £720 million for smart technology during the current Ofwat price-review period. He did not commit to reviewing the 2050 target, and said that water legislation would be introduced in due course.

### Interpol red notices and Russia
Lord Garnier asked how the Government were addressing the alleged abuse by Russia of Interpol red notices and diffusions. Lord Hanson of Flint said the Government would work with the National Crime Agency, Interpol and like-minded countries, including through the Notices and Diffusions Task Force, to monitor and strengthen safeguards. He said that all red-notice requests were reviewed before publication and that the United Kingdom participated in Interpol governance structures: “100% of red notice requests are reviewed before publication by the Interpol Notices and Diffusions Task Force.”

Peers proposed prior detailed examination of Russian requests, changes to the appeals process, legislation similar to the United States TRAP Acts, implementation of recommendations on transnational repression, and greater protection for people in the UK. Lord Hanson stressed that Interpol controlled its own governance and procedures, and that any action following a breach was ultimately a matter for Interpol rather than the UK Government acting independently. He undertook to provide Lord Alton of Liverpool with a written update on the implementation of recommendations that the Government had accepted from the Joint Committee on Human Rights report. He also clarified that a red notice is not an extradition agreement but a request for information surrounding an individual.

### Jury directions on rape myths and stereotypes
Baroness Levitt asked whether jury directions on harmful myths and stereotypes should be mandatory in rape and serious sexual offence trials. Lord Lemos said that judges already give case-specific directions where appropriate: “independent judges already can and do give directions concerning rape myths.” He also said that the Courts and Tribunals Bill would restrict the unfair use of previous allegations of rape and compensation-claim information, and that amendments tabled by Baroness Levitt would be debated the following week.

Several peers supported clearer directions and wider education, while Lord Keen of Elie, Viscount Hailsham and Lord Burnett of Maldon emphasised judicial independence and the existing Crown Court Compendium. Lord Lemos agreed that judicial independence was fundamental and said that rape myths should not distort trials or retraumatise victims. The Government also cited independent legal advisers for adult rape victims, specialist rape and serious sexual offence courts, and a new rape justice taskforce, with £6 million of initial funding for legal advisers and £23 million for specialist courts. No decision was taken on mandatory directions in this question; the issue was scheduled for further debate on the Bill.

### Business of the House: Church Appointments Bill
Baroness Smith of Basildon moved a motion to dispense with Standing Order 44 on Monday 23 November. This would allow the Church Appointments (Repeal of Discriminatory Provision) Bill to complete its remaining stages on that day and prevent amendments being moved on Third Reading. She said: “Second Reading will take place tomorrow during the dinner break, at a convenient point after or around 7.30 pm.” The Motion was agreed.

### Health Bill: ministerial powers over integrated care boards
The Committee debated whether the Bill gave the Secretary of State excessive powers to direct integrated care boards (ICBs), particularly following the proposed abolition of NHS England. Lord Scriven proposed replacing broad directions with published national and local outcomes, an accountability framework and a more formal intervention regime. Baroness Bennett of Manor Castle argued for protecting local commissioning decisions, while Baroness Manzoor and Earl Howe sought stronger accountability, consultation and transparency. Other contributions, including those from Baroness Coffey and Lord Hunt of Kings Heath, supported ministerial accountability for a national health service but differed on the extent of the powers. Lord Stevens of Birmingham also raised concerns about the proposed exception relating to treatments on which NICE had not issued guidance.

Baroness Merron defended flexibility, saying that Ministers needed to respond to unforeseen circumstances and that directions would be used sparingly. She confirmed that the Government could share developing examples of directions: “we can commit to sharing examples of developing drafts of directions as soon as possible.” She also said that the Secretary of State would remain responsible if an ICB’s outcomes worsened after following a direction: “the Secretary of State remains responsible when and if an ICB follows the Secretary of State direction.” Amendment 30 and Amendment 32 were withdrawn; Amendment 31 was not moved; and Clauses 8 and 9 were agreed. Later, Amendments 37 to 40 were not moved and Clause 11 was agreed. The Minister offered a further discussion with Members and undertook to make as much information as possible available, but no fixed deadline for an operational model was stated in the transcript.

### Health Bill: public and independent provision of NHS services
The Committee considered Clause 10 and amendments seeking either a presumption in favour of public provision or a patient-outcomes test before changing the balance between public and independent providers. Baroness Bennett of Manor Castle argued that public provision should be the default and raised concerns about profit, capacity and workforce training. Baroness Murphy, Lord Hunt of Kings Heath, Baroness Coffey, Baroness Nargund, Lord Scriven and Lord Kamall argued that ownership should not displace considerations of access, quality, timeliness and patient choice. Lord Stevens of Birmingham said that the clause could allow substantial changes in either direction by future Governments. Baroness Bennett later clarified that, in referring to private provision, she intended to target the for-profit sector rather than charities and other genuinely not-for-profit providers: “by ‘private’ I was referring to for-profit providers.”

Baroness Merron said that Clause 10 generally prohibited deliberately shifting the balance between types of provider but created an exception where the Secretary of State considered action to be in the interests of the health service. She gave examples including rescuing a failing service, reducing dependence on a single external provider and building public capacity. She said: “The clause does not enable privatisation or nationalisation.” She agreed to consider publishing reasons for decisions under the exception and to write further on the clause’s purpose. Amendment 33 was withdrawn; Amendments 34 and 35 were not moved; Clause 10 was agreed; and Amendment 36 was not moved. No amendment was accepted.

### Health Bill: cancer outcomes and treatment
Peers debated amendments on prioritising cancer outcomes, research into cancers with low survival rates, a proposed 62-day treatment right, radiotherapy, genomics, prostate-cancer detection and clinical-trial participation. Supporters cited delays, regional inequalities, workforce and equipment shortages, and the concern that abolishing the NHS England mandate could weaken accountability. Baroness Finlay of Llandaff reported delays in genomic testing and said that only 42% of patients received their first radiotherapy treatment within 62 days in the figures she cited.

Baroness Merron said that the national cancer plan aimed to increase five-year survival to 75% by 2035 and that “our focus on prioritising cancer outcomes remains.” She cited annual reporting, an independent National Cancer Board, £141.6 million of cancer research expenditure in 2024-25, a rare-cancers research priority, international collaboration, a cancer trials accelerator, 5,000 annual training opportunities and £70 million for 28 replacement radiotherapy machines. She said the Government intended to meet the cancer waiting-time standards but considered an additional statutory cancer-prioritisation duty and a specific radiotherapy duty unnecessary. Amendment 41 was withdrawn. The transcript does not record formal decisions on the other amendments in this group. The Minister said she would write to Baroness Finlay about whether information would be supplied directly to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development.

### Health Bill: social care, dementia and ministerial accountability
Peers debated amendments to make the Secretary of State’s responsibility for social care explicit, require dementia data reporting and require more information about a proposed national care service. Supporters argued that health and social care were interdependent and that explicit responsibility, joined-up data and national reporting would improve accountability. Baroness Ritchie of Downpatrick and Lord Weir of Ballyholme emphasised geographical variation in dementia diagnosis and care, while Baroness Pitkeathley and Lord Patel of Bradford described the effects of weak integration on hospital discharge, aftercare and families.

Baroness Merron said that adult social care already had a statutory framework under the Care Act 2014: “adult social care already has its own statutory framework under the Care Act 2014.” She said that the Government would develop a national care service through the independent Casey review and cross-party work. She cited £4.6 billion of additional funding for adult social care in 2028-29, including £500 million for a fair-pay agreement, and said that the reform would not be funded by borrowing: “we will not borrow to pay for reform.” She declined to legislate prematurely for an implementation plan or a dementia annual report, saying that a modern service framework for frailty and dementia was expected by the end of the calendar year. Amendment 42 was withdrawn and Amendments 44 to 47 were not moved. The transcript records no formal decision on the other substantive proposals in this part of the proceedings.

### Health Bill: continuity of care, pharmacies and eye care
The Committee considered a proposed duty to promote continuity of care, measures concerning failing community pharmacies, national sight-test arrangements and targeted eye care for people with learning disabilities. Baroness Neville-Rolfe, Baroness Gerada and Earl Howe argued that continuity could improve outcomes and reduce fragmentation, while Baroness Coffey highlighted the need to balance continuity with timely access. Baroness Gerada described continuity as “fundamental to good medicine.” Lord Scriven sought free sight tests and better commissioned services for people with learning disabilities, citing higher rates of sight loss and the risk of diagnostic overshadowing.

Baroness Merron said that existing arrangements, including named GPs and team-based continuity in hospitals, made a statutory duty unnecessary. She said that ICBs would be responsible for monitoring pharmaceutical services and could issue remedial and breach notices, remove contractors following repeated breaches and support replacement providers. She said that national eligibility and fees for NHS sight tests would remain governed by national arrangements, and that existing routes and reasonable adjustments were available to eligible people with learning disabilities. Baroness Neville-Rolfe withdrew Amendment 43; Amendments 44 to 47 were not moved. The transcript records no formal decisions on the other primary-care, pharmacy and eye-care amendments. The House adjourned at 10 pm.
