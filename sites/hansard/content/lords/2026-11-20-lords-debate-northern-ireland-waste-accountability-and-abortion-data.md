## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords considered three Private Members’ Bills at Second Reading. Debate on the European Union (Withdrawal Arrangements) Bill [HL] concerned Northern Ireland’s democratic representation, constitutional position and trading arrangements; supporters advocated mutual enforcement, while the Government defended the Windsor Framework, and the Bill was committed to a Committee of the Whole House. The Corporate Waste Responsibility Bill [HL] proposed board-level responsibility for waste and recovery of clean-up costs, but the Government raised concerns about its scope, interaction with existing law and practical operation; it was also committed. The Complications from Abortions (Annual Report) Bill [HL] concerned the collation and publication of abortion-complication data from abortion notifications and hospital records; the Government said new legislation was unnecessary, confirmed the planned publication of the 2024 statistics and opposed the Bill’s approach, but it was committed to a Committee of the Whole House.

## Detailed Summary
### European Union (Withdrawal Arrangements) Bill [HL] — Second Reading
Lord Dodds of Duncairn said the Bill responded to what he regarded as Northern Ireland’s continuing democratic deficit under the protocol and Windsor Framework. He argued that EU law applied in Northern Ireland without the effective consent of the UK Parliament or the Northern Ireland Assembly, that the December 2024 Assembly vote had not used cross-community consent, and that trade from Great Britain remained subject to customs and regulatory burdens. He proposed mutual enforcement, under which each jurisdiction would ensure that goods entering the other’s market complied with the destination market’s requirements. He said the Bill addressed “the fundamental problem, which is the denial of democracy and the breach of the Belfast agreement”. Lord Lilley supported mutual enforcement, arguing that it had operated before the EU single market and that “It worked”. Lord Frost, Lord Moynihan of Chelsea, Baroness Hoey, Lord Shinkwin and Lord Weir of Ballyholme also supported the Bill. Their arguments included the restoration of UK-wide democratic control, cross-community consent and the reduction of trade friction. Lord Bew questioned the Bill’s treatment of the Act of Union as the principal reference point, while also calling for the Government to follow up recommendations concerning Northern Ireland businesses and consumers. Baroness Ludford opposed the Bill and instead supported practical measures including a one-stop shop, trusted-trader arrangements, improved parliamentary scrutiny and better regulatory information. Baroness Finn said she would consider constructive changes consistent with Northern Ireland’s position within the United Kingdom, while opposing arrangements that made the UK a rule-taker from the EU.

Lord Katz, responding for the Government, defended the Windsor Framework. He said the Bill could not be reconciled with international law, did not sufficiently address Northern Ireland’s particular circumstances and would create uncertainty for businesses and traders. He argued that mutual enforcement and technological alternatives did not resolve the practical need to protect both the UK internal market and the EU single market. He highlighted the sanitary and phytosanitary agreement being agreed with the EU, the £16.6 million UK internal-market support package and the planned Northern Ireland business support service. The Government also relied on the Assembly’s 2024 decision to retain the arrangements and argued that the consent mechanism was consistent with the Northern Ireland Act 1998. Lord Katz said: “this Government will support only sustainable arrangements for Northern Ireland that work for businesses, protect the UK’s internal market, and uphold our international obligations”. Lord Dodds replied that practical improvements could be only “a patchwork of sticking plasters”. The Bill was read a second time and committed to a Committee of the Whole House.

### Corporate Waste Responsibility Bill [HL] — Second Reading
Baroness Sheehan said the Bill would address illegal waste management by placing responsibility on a named director from the point at which waste was produced until its final destination. It would also make a company responsible for clean-up and remediation costs where waste had been unlawfully managed and the company had failed to exercise due diligence. She said the Bill would “place responsibility firmly in the boardroom”. Supporters, including Lord Grantchester, Lord Krebs, Baroness Grender, Baroness MacLeod of Camusdarach, Lord Bourne of Aberystwyth, Baroness Boycott and Earl Russell, described waste crime as profitable and difficult to detect, and said that landowners, local authorities and taxpayers often bore the resulting costs. They argued that the Bill would apply the polluter-pays principle upstream and complement existing enforcement, carrier and broker reforms and digital waste tracking. Lord Redesdale separately argued that landfill tax contributed to the financial incentive for waste crime. Issues raised for further consideration included the definition of a “large company”, compensation for innocent landowners, liability where waste passed through several intermediaries, the scope of recoverable costs and the interaction with existing penalties.

Lord Sharpe of Epsom and Lord Blencathra questioned whether the Bill would impose additional compliance, notification, reporting and record-keeping costs on responsible businesses without addressing failures to enforce existing law. Lord Blencathra also questioned whether it would address large illegal sites formed from many small deposits and what it added to the existing statutory duty of care. Baroness Hayman of Ullock said that the Government shared the Bill’s objectives but had reservations about its current approach. She identified uncertainty over the scope of “large companies”, the meaning of “reasonable steps”, notification duties, causation, the allocation of liability and the Bill’s interaction with existing regimes. She said the Government’s reservations “are not objections to the principle that businesses should take responsibility for their waste”. The Government said they were examining the wider regulatory and enforcement framework, had committed an additional £45 million for Environment Agency enforcement over three financial years and were exploring insurance options for affected landowners. Baroness Sheehan welcomed continued dialogue and argued that the existing regime was not sufficient to address the problem. The Bill was read a second time and committed to a Committee of the Whole House.

### Complications from Abortions (Annual Report) Bill [HL] — Second Reading
Lord Moylan proposed requiring the Secretary of State to publish an annual report combining abortion notification system data with hospital episode statistics on complications. He argued that the increased use of medication abortions, including abortions undertaken at home, meant that complications arising after discharge might not be recorded by abortion providers. He referred to the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities’ 2023 comparison of the two data sources, which found higher complication rates in hospital data than in the abortion notification system. Supporters, including Lord Weir of Ballyholme, Baroness Lawlor, Baroness Maclean of Redditch, Baroness Meyer and Lord Jackson of Peterborough, argued that reporting should reflect current practice and provide better information for clinicians, women and policymakers. Lord Moylan said legislation was necessary while the Government failed to publish “the statistics that women and their clinicians want and need in order to make an informed choice”.

Baronesses Thornton, Brinton, Freeman of Steventon, Sugg and Barker, and Baroness Bennett of Manor Castle, opposed the Bill or questioned its necessity. They argued that abortion complications were already recorded through different data systems, that the existing statistics met the relevant standards, and that a dedicated statutory report could stigmatise abortion or create confidentiality risks. Baroness Merron said the Government considered that the Bill’s aims could be achieved through existing mechanisms and that “the Government have no plans to issue a similar publication annually”. She confirmed that the 2024 annual abortion statistics were due to be published on 3 December, while publication dates for the 2025 and 2026 statistics would be announced in due course. She also said that abortion data would not be included in the proposed single patient record and that the Office for Statistics Regulation’s April 2026 review had concluded that the abortion statistics continued to meet the relevant standards. The Bill was read a second time and committed to a Committee of the Whole House. The House then adjourned at 2.50 pm.
