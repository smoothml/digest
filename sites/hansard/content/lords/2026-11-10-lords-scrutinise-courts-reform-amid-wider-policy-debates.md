## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords considered oral questions on the automobile industry and the Zero Emission Vehicle Mandate, neighbourhood health services, climate resilience and interest rates. It agreed a Private Business Standing Order motion and then considered the Courts and Tribunals Bill in Committee, covering allocation and jury-trial election, reclassification, legal aid, judge-only trials, delegated powers and magistrates’ sentencing powers. The Church Appointments (Repeal of Discriminatory Provision) Bill received a second reading and was committed to a Committee of the Whole House. The Sporting Events Bill was returned from the Commons with amendments, and the House adjourned at 9.34 pm.

## Detailed Summary
### Automobile industry: Zero Emission Vehicle Mandate
Lord Woodley asked about compliance targets, possible job losses, the timing of the consultation outcome and whether technologies such as mild hybrid vehicles would have a future role. Baroness Dacres of Lewisham said that the Government had invested more than £7.5 billion in the automotive sector, had closed a consultation reviewing the Zero Emission Vehicle (ZEV) Mandate and aimed to publish the outcome by the end of the year: “We have just closed a consultation reviewing the ZEV mandate.” She said the department had discussed mild hybrids with manufacturers and that their future role would form part of the review.\n\nPeers also raised policy certainty, charging infrastructure, access for renters and leaseholders, grid capacity and investment risks. The Minister cited £600 million for charging infrastructure and a further £100 million over two years for on-street public chargers. No decision was taken in the Question; the Government said that they would publish the review outcome and continue working with industry, the devolved Governments and other stakeholders.

### Neighbourhood health services
Lord Lansley asked how neighbourhood health services would improve patient outcomes and reduce pressure on hospitals. Baroness Merron said that success would be measured against the national goals in the neighbourhood health framework, with early local evidence including reductions in emergency-department use and emergency admissions: “Early local reported evidence from neighbourhood health approaches is encouraging.” The 43-place national implementation programme and an independent national evaluation commissioned through the National Institute for Health and Care Research would inform wider implementation.\n\nDiscussion covered community diagnostics, general practitioner capacity, funding flows, neighbourhood health centres, urgent community care, community nursing, the proposed clinical council and pharmacy vaccination. The Minister said there were 2,800 more fully qualified full-time equivalent GPs than in July 2024, and that payment mechanisms would be trialled so that savings from reduced unplanned hospital care could support community services. She also referred to 250 neighbourhood health centres, with 120 to be delivered by 2030, including £50 million for the first 27 sites. The workforce plan was expected in the new year. No division or formal decision was recorded.

### Climate change: resilience
Lord Lennie asked what steps were being taken to improve the United Kingdom’s resilience to climate change. Lord Katz said that the Government intended to develop specific, measurable objectives and delivery plans for the fourth National Adaptation Programme in 2028, in line with the Climate Change Committee’s advice on two degrees Celsius of warming by 2050. He accepted that the third programme had weak targets and limited departmental co-ordination, and said that the next programme would use “a stronger framework, built on specific, measurable objectives”.\n\nQuestions addressed wildfire preparedness, building standards, drought and water supply, working temperatures, energy and airport policy, and land management. The Minister cited £45 million for specialist wildfire teams and protective equipment, and said that RAF Chinook helicopters and A400M aircraft would be equipped to assist. He also referred to £300 million for natural flood management up to 2035, £30 million for coastal adaptation pilots and environmental land management schemes to support resilient landscapes. He undertook to write to Baroness Goldie about rainfall predictions for southern England. No formal decision was recorded.

### Interest rates
Baroness Neville-Rolfe asked about the recent rise in interest rates and its effects on economic growth, inflation and borrowing costs. Lord Pitt-Watson said that the independent Monetary Policy Committee had increased Bank Rate in response to high inflation, while acknowledging that higher rates would weigh on activity and increase borrowing costs. He also said that higher rates should reduce inflationary pressure. He explained that the effect on mortgage holders would vary by mortgage type and noted the rise in Bank Rate from 1% in May 2022 to 5.25% in August 2023.\n\nPeers discussed gilt yields, fiscal rules, index-linked bonds, debt interest, growth, the causes of inflation and public debt. The Minister reaffirmed that “We have an absolute commitment to the fiscal rules.” He also said that interest-rate decisions were for the independent Monetary Policy Committee and that the Bank of England’s relevant decisions were independent. No policy change or division was recorded.

### Private Business Standing Order 70(1)
The Senior Deputy Speaker moved a motion to dispense with Private Business Standing Order 70(1), allowing examination of petitions for Private Bills deposited under Standing Order 38 to begin on 17 December 2026: “That Private Business Standing Order 70(1) be dispensed with.” The motion was agreed. No further action was stated in the transcript.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill: allocation of either-way offences and jury-trial election
In the first Committee group, Baroness Chakrabarti, Viscount Hailsham, Baroness Jones of Moulsecoomb and others questioned Clause 1’s removal of a defendant’s right to elect Crown Court trial for triable either-way offences. Arguments against the clause focused on jury trial as a safeguard, the risk of transferring rather than reducing backlogs, equality impacts, vulnerable witnesses and the position of defendants of good character. Baroness Chakrabarti argued that the Bill could favour defendants with previous convictions over defendants of good character. Other contributors, including Baroness Levitt and Baroness Bi, supported the proposed approach, arguing that magistrates were trusted decision-makers and that allocation should depend on the seriousness and complexity of the case rather than the defendant’s preference.\n\nThe Government said that “Clause 1 will remove the defendant’s ability to choose to elect for a Crown Court trial for all triable ‘either way’ offences”. They argued that Clauses 1 and 2 would prioritise Crown Court time for the most serious and complex cases, while retaining representations on venue and existing routes of challenge. The Government opposed a new appeal right, special treatment for defendants of good character and an exemption for speech offences, maintaining that allocation should be based on the alleged offending rather than personal characteristics. Amendment 1 was withdrawn; Amendments 2 and 3 were not moved; Amendment 5 was not moved; and Clauses 1 and 2 were agreed.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill: reclassification and out-of-court resolutions
Lord Thomas of Cwmgiedd moved Amendment 4, which would have required the Secretary of State to reclassify certain either-way offences as summary-only offences and obtain approval from both Houses for the resulting regulations. He argued that reclassification could provide greater certainty, reduce pressure on the Crown Court and limit regional variation in allocation. Lord Marks of Henley-on-Thames separately argued for greater use of out-of-court resolutions, including after proceedings had begun, particularly in low-level cases associated with health, family, addiction or social difficulties. Lord Macdonald of River Glaven supported that approach and referred to restorative justice.\n\nLord Lemos said that the Government had considered reclassification but concluded that it could restrict investigative powers, reduce maximum sentences and create risks in cases including domestic abuse. He said that the Government supported strengthening out-of-court resolutions and was examining the legislative and delivery framework, but considered that a new statutory duty on courts could duplicate existing processes and cause delay. Amendment 4 was withdrawn. The Minister said that discussions on reclassification and out-of-court resolutions would continue; no further decision on those proposals was recorded.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill: legal aid and representation
Lord Thomas of Cwmgiedd proposed aligning legal-aid eligibility and remuneration for relevant magistrates’ court cases with Crown Court arrangements. Supporters, including Lord Macdonald of River Glaven, Lord Shamash, Lord Jabbar, Baroness Jones of Moulsecoomb and Lord Marks of Henley-on-Thames, argued that low fees and narrower eligibility could lead to unrepresented defendants, hinder recruitment and make hearings less efficient. Lord Thomas said: “without something being done about legal aid, none of this will work”. Baroness Jones cited the Government’s equality statement that more defendants could be ineligible for legal aid if more cases were heard in magistrates’ courts.\n\nLord Lemos said that the Government had accepted in principle the recommendation to increase magistrates’ court income thresholds, but that final levels would be decided after implementation, subject to funding and operational considerations. He opposed immediate full alignment and Crown Court-level fees, citing the different nature of magistrates’ court work and possible administrative burdens, while referring to hardship reviews and the magistrates’ court duty-solicitor scheme. Amendment 6 was withdrawn, and Amendments 7 and 8 were not moved.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill: judge-only trials for complex or lengthy cases
The Committee debated safeguards for Clause 3, which would permit judge-only trials in specified complex or lengthy fraud and financial cases. Viscount Hailsham and others proposed restrictions concerning co-defendants, the meaning of “appropriate” and “public interest”, party representations, judicial oversight, appeals, revocation and written judgments. Lord Marks argued that juries could decide factual and dishonesty issues in complex fraud cases and sought appeal rights. Baroness Jones questioned the estimated saving of 200 Crown Court sitting days a year.\n\nThe Government said that the measure concerned the burden placed on jurors by exceptional, lengthy cases rather than juror competence: “This is about the jury experience, as I said in relation to the last group; it is not about jury competence.” Lord Lemos rejected a fixed five-month threshold because cases shorter than that might still be exceptionally burdensome. The Committee also considered whether defendants should be able to apply for judge-only trials; proponents referred to complex evidence, prejudicial publicity and reasoned judgments, while the Government maintained that the court, rather than the defendant, should determine the mode of trial. Amendment 9 was withdrawn; Amendments 10 and 11 were not moved; Amendment 12 was withdrawn; Amendment 13 was not moved; Amendment 13A was withdrawn; Amendments 13B to 28 were not moved; and Clause 3 was agreed.

### Church Appointments (Repeal of Discriminatory Provision) Bill
Baroness Smith of Basildon moved the Bill’s second reading. The Bill would repeal the statutory restrictions in the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829 and the Jews Relief Act 1858 that prevented Roman Catholics and Jewish people from advising the sovereign on certain Church appointments, and it would come into force on Royal Assent. Supporters, including Baroness Bottomley, Lord Murphy of Torfaen, Lord Wolfson of Tredegar, the Bishop of Manchester and Lord True, described the restrictions as outdated and discriminatory, while explaining that the existing Church-led appointments process would remain unchanged. Lord Wolfson said that “the law makes a person’s religion determine whether he may discharge the responsibilities of a secular office”.\n\nLord Birt and Lord Purvis of Tweed used the debate to argue for wider constitutional reform, including consideration of disestablishment. Lord Moylan questioned the urgency of the Bill and whether the Prime Minister’s role was substantive under current arrangements. Baroness Smith said that repeal of the Jews Relief Act would remove no surviving right or protection, but would remove the remaining disability imposed by that Act. The Bill was read a second time and committed to a Committee of the Whole House.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill: delegated powers for judge-only offences
Lord Keen of Elie moved Amendment 29 to remove the Henry VIII power allowing the Secretary of State to amend by secondary legislation the list of offences eligible for judge-only trials. He and other Peers referred to the Delegated Powers and Regulatory Reform Committee’s description of the power as an “inappropriate delegation of power”. Baroness Chakrabarti argued that future offences could instead be dealt with through primary legislation.\n\nLord Lemos defended the power on the basis that fraud and financial offending could evolve with technology, including artificial intelligence. He stressed that additions would require the affirmative procedure in both Houses and would remain subject to the Clause 3 safeguards. Amendment 29 was withdrawn; Amendments 30 and 31 were not moved; Schedule 1 and Clause 4 were agreed. Lord Keen indicated that the issue would be revisited on Report.

### Courts and Tribunals Bill: magistrates’ court sentencing powers and capacity
Lord Marks of Henley-on-Thames, Baroness Jones of Moulsecoomb, Viscount Hailsham, Baroness Sater and others challenged Clause 5, which would allow regulations to set the general custodial limit for either-way offences in a magistrates’ court at 6, 12, 18 or 24 months. They argued that sentencing limits were matters for primary legislation, that the negative procedure provided insufficient scrutiny and that increased workloads could overwhelm magistrates’ courts, particularly in light of recruitment, training, legal-aid and staffing concerns. Baroness Jones described the proposal as a “dangerous experiment”. Baroness Sater sought safeguards on preparation and annual monitoring.\n\nThe Government said that “Clause 5 does not itself increase those powers.” Instead, it would provide flexibility to respond to changing pressures, with any future decision informed by operational evidence and engagement across the justice system. Lord Lemos referred to the aim of increasing the magistracy to about 21,000 by 2028-29, increasing sitting days and recruiting legal advisers and district judges. He said that a fixed workforce threshold and statutory delay were unnecessary because readiness depended on several factors. Amendment 33 was withdrawn; Amendments 34 and 35 were not moved; Clause 5 was agreed; and Amendment 36 was not moved.

### Sporting Events Bill [HL]
The House was informed that the Sporting Events Bill had been returned from the Commons agreed to with amendments: “The Bill was returned from the Commons agreed to with amendments.” No further debate or decision was recorded in the transcript. The House adjourned at 9.34 pm.
