## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords began with the introduction of the Lord Bishop of Blackburn, followed by questions on pensions, small-business finance, food and drink manufacturing, and women experiencing rough sleeping. Committee stage of the Health Bill covered foundation-trust governance, patient safety, fertility regulation, Healthwatch, medicines and medical devices, NHS funding priorities, regulation and the proposed single patient record. The Government confirmed that Healthwatch England and local Healthwatch would be retained and strengthened, agreed amendments restoring Local Healthwatch consultation in trust special administration, and made several commitments to provide information or hold further discussions. The Church Appointments (Repeal of Discriminatory Provision) Bill completed its remaining stages without amendment and was passed.

## Detailed Summary
### Introduction: Lord Bishop of Blackburn
Philip John, Lord Bishop of Blackburn, was introduced, took the oath, was supported by the Bishops of Gloucester and Chester, and signed an undertaking to abide by the Code of Conduct: “was introduced and took the oath, supported by the Bishop of Gloucester and the Bishop of Chester”.

### State Pension: impact of ending the triple lock
Lord Balfe asked about the annual financial effect on State Pension recipients of replacing the triple lock with a guaranteed uprating linked to average earnings. Baroness Sherlock said the Government were adjusting, rather than removing, the triple lock. From April 2030, the state pension would increase by at least the highest of 2.5%, Consumer Prices Index (CPI) inflation or a higher amount where necessary to preserve its forecast value relative to average earnings in April 2029: “We are adjusting, not removing, the triple lock.”

Peers questioned uncertainty, pensioner poverty, intergenerational fairness, funding and the National Insurance Fund. Baroness Sherlock said future amounts would depend on inflation, earnings and individual circumstances, and said the adjusted arrangement was intended to stop the state pension continually running ahead of wages: “What it will not do is continue to constantly run ahead of wages.” She said legislation would be introduced before the end of the Parliament. She also said pensioner poverty was projected to fall from 14% to 8%, that pension credit remained central, and that automatic data matching to identify unclaimed pension credit was not currently possible because sufficient data was not held. The transcript records no further decision on the question.

### Small businesses: access to finance
Lord McTague asked what action the Government were taking to improve small businesses’ access to finance. Lord Leong said the British Business Bank was central to the Government’s support, including more than doubling the Growth Guarantee Scheme’s lending capacity to £3.35 billion a year and increasing the Bank’s financial capacity to £30.6 billion by 2028-29: “Access to the right finance at the right time is vital.”

Peers raised the cost and risk of scaling up, export finance, pension-fund investment, tax-advantaged schemes, support for ethnic-minority entrepreneurs, the bank corporation tax surcharge and the low proportion of firms becoming scale-ups. Lord Leong referred to the British Business Bank, Mansion House reforms, start-up loans, community finance and the Enterprise Investment Scheme, and said a mandatory code would govern the fair use of personal guarantees. On the corporation tax surcharge affecting challenger and specialist banks, he undertook to obtain further information: “I need to come back to the noble Lord once I get further information from my officials.” No formal decision on the question is recorded.

### Food and drink manufacturing in the Industrial Strategy
Lord Smith of Ranmoor asked whether food and drink manufacturing would be included as a priority within the Industrial Strategy. Baroness Ramsey of Wall Heath said the Government’s sector review had included food and drink manufacturing and had considered growth, regional inequality, net zero, resilience and whether policy could address barriers cost-effectively. She described the sector as “our largest manufacturing sector, rooted in communities across the UK”.

Peers discussed long-term investment, hospitality costs, the agreement with the European Union on sanitary and phytosanitary measures, extended producer responsibility, energy costs and the sector’s position within the eight named industrial-strategy priority sectors. Baroness Ramsey said the EU agreement would make agri-food trade cheaper and easier, and that advanced manufacturing—one of the eight priority sectors—would benefit much of food and drink. She also said the British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme would reduce eligible manufacturers’ electricity costs by up to 25% from April 2027, and referred to lower business rates for qualifying retail, hospitality and leisure properties and a £10 million hospitality grant scheme. No formal decision on the question is recorded.

### Women experiencing rough sleeping
Baroness Nye asked what steps were being taken to provide adequate and accessible support for women experiencing rough sleeping. Baroness Taylor of Stevenage referred to a departmental action plan, the Prime Minister’s national drive to end rough sleeping and more than £4.4 billion over three years for homelessness and rough-sleeping services. She said women could be less visible through conventional outreach: “We recognise that women sleeping rough can be less visible through traditional outreach practices.”

Peers sought a national women’s rough-sleeping census, safer and more suitable accommodation, Housing First, support for women leaving prison and stronger responses to domestic abuse. The Minister said the monthly rough-sleeping survey had included a question on women sleeping rough since June 2025, encouraged local authorities to undertake women’s censuses, and said local areas should determine suitable housing-led approaches. She also said at least 10% of beds in the community accommodation service were allocated to women in women-only properties and that 10 of the 52 prison-based strategic housing specialists worked across the women’s estate. She did not commit to a national roll-out of the census. She also referred to a domestic-abuse and homelessness prevention toolkit under development.

### Health Bill: NHS foundation trusts and local accountability
Baroness Coffey proposed putting the power to remove directors directly into Clause 30 and separately probed whether persistently underperforming foundation trusts should lose their special status. She questioned whether foundation-trust independence was serving patients, asking: “independence on behalf of whom?” Lord Hunt of Kings Heath, Lord Patel of Bradford, Lord Blunkett and Earl Howe opposed abolishing councils of governors. They argued that governors provided local accountability, brought patient, staff and community views to boards, and helped scrutinise chairs and non-executive directors. Lord Patel asked: “If we remove that statutory accountability, what will replace it?”

Baroness Merron said governor effectiveness varied, but that statutory duties concerning public involvement would remain and trusts could retain effective local groups. She said the Secretary of State would inherit NHS England’s oversight and enforcement powers, including intervention, replacement of directors and conversion of failing foundation trusts, while a lower performance segment would not automatically constitute a licence breach: “A trust’s segmentation indicates how well it is performing, but a lower performance segment is not a licence breach.” She said the intended approach to appointments by departmental or regional officials would be set out in a letter before Report and offered to meet Lord Hunt and other Members. Amendment 104ZB was withdrawn; Clause 30 and Schedule 3 were agreed; Amendments 104A, 105 and 105A were not moved.

### Health Bill: patient safety, clinical negligence and fertility regulation
The Committee considered amendments on reporting systemic medical malpractice, a national coronial database, fixed recoverable costs, clinical-negligence reform, non-therapeutic male circumcision, online fertility services, death certification and private diagnostic tests. Baroness Pidgeon proposed statutory duties on NHS trust boards to report evidence of systemic malpractice. The Government said existing Care Quality Commission requirements, NHS provider-licence duties and enforcement powers already addressed the issue: “trusts are already accountable for reporting medical malpractice, including systemic cases.” Amendment 106 was withdrawn. On clinical negligence, the Minister said the David Lock KC review was considering lower-value claims, fixed recoverable costs and double recovery, but could not yet confirm whether the evidence would be published and undertook to keep Members updated.

Baronesses Owen, Deech, Thornton and others sought to extend Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority regulation to online fertility services and to modernise the wider framework. The Government accepted that fertility treatment had changed since 1990, but said reforms required consideration of legal and operational implications, consultation and parliamentary scrutiny. No timetable was given: “Unfortunately, by definition, I cannot give a timetable.” The Minister said there were no current plans for legislation or guidance on non-therapeutic male circumcision, that established mechanisms already supported death certification and coronial learning, and that private medical diagnostic tests were regulated as medical devices. The transcript records the Government’s position and commitments on these issues, but not final decisions on all the amendments debated in this group.

### Healthwatch and personal health budgets
The Government announced that Healthwatch England and local Healthwatch would be retained and strengthened, rather than abolished. Baroness Merron said the reforms would include statutory guidance on minimum standards for performance, data collection and research, subject to Secretary of State approval, alongside a strengthened patient experience directorate: “We have concluded that the right approach is to retain and strengthen Healthwatch England and local Healthwatch.” Peers welcomed the decision but questioned funding disparities, independence, support for seldom-heard groups, commissioning and the ability of Healthwatch to secure responses from NHS bodies. The Government said local authorities would continue to commission local Healthwatch and that Healthwatch England could notify a local authority where duties were not being carried out.

Baroness Campbell of Surbiton, Lord Lansley and Lord Scriven argued for integrated personal health and social-care budgets, with one plan, one lead body and stronger review rights. Baroness Merron said existing legislation already allowed health and social-care funding to be brought together and that the main difficulties were practical. She said individuals should not be forced into services solely for an ICB’s commissioning convenience and undertook to examine Ravi Mehta’s case: “individuals should not be forced into services simply for the commissioning convenience of ICBs.” Government Amendments 106A and 106B, restoring requirements for a trust special administrator to consult and meet relevant Local Healthwatch organisations, were agreed. Lord Lansley also asked for information on Section 75 arrangements, which the Minister undertook to obtain.

### Health Bill: medicines, medical devices and artificial intelligence
The Committee debated hospice access to medicines, medicines reuse and waste, the UK-US pharmaceutical arrangement, injectable medicines, artificial-intelligence regulation, mental-health chatbots, antidepressant prescribing and medical-device traceability. On artificial intelligence, the Government said they intended to consult in spring 2027 on a new approach to classifying and qualifying AI-enabled medical devices and to lay draft regulations by the end of 2027. The Government also supported transparent reporting on implementation of the National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare’s recommendations in principle.

On hospice medicines, the Minister said existing pharmaceutical and waste-management arrangements applied across different care settings and that a hospice-specific statutory approach could create inequity: “A statutory approach focused on hospices alone risks cutting across the broader arrangements.” Baroness Finlay withdrew Amendment 111 and said she intended to return to the issue on Report. Amendment 112 was not moved. The Minister offered a meeting with officials on reclassifying suitable self-administered injectable medicines and said existing guidance and professional duties addressed informed prescribing of antidepressants. She also said an impact assessment of the UK-US pharmaceutical arrangement was not being published because of commercially sensitive and scenario-based material, while the National Audit Office planned a factual overview for early 2027.

### Health Bill: funding priorities for mental health, primary care and prevention
Baroness Tyler of Enfield, Baroness Hollins, Lord Patel of Bradford and others argued for statutory protection of mental-health investment. Baroness Gerada and Baroness Pidgeon argued for a primary-care investment standard, while Lord Babudu and Baroness Manzoor sought greater transparency over preventive spending. The Government accepted the importance of these areas but argued that statutory spending requirements could be too prescriptive and restrict local flexibility: “requiring Ministers to set investment standards for each may risk flexibility and limit local systems’ ability to respond to changes in needs and priorities.”

The Government said mental-health spending was forecast to reach £16.3 billion in 2026-27 and that all ICBs were meeting, or forecast to meet, the existing mental health investment standard. It said overall budget increases would generally be expected to be reflected in primary-care spending, and announced a new ring-fenced preventative department expenditure limit at the 2027 spending review. Amendment 113 was withdrawn; Amendments 114 to 116 and 118 were not moved. The Government also said positive National Institute for Health and Care Excellence recommendations should normally be implemented within 90 days, with any longer period expected to be exceptional.

### Health Bill: regulation of NHS organisations
Earl Howe proposed statutory safeguards requiring regulatory decisions affecting integrated care boards, NHS trusts and NHS foundation trusts to be based on relevant performance information and operationally separated from policy, commissioning and resource-allocation functions. Lord Scriven supported the principle and asked how the new powers would be constrained. Baroness Merron said existing public-law duties, the Ministerial Code and statutory duties already guided ministerial decisions, and that the amendment would create an overly prescriptive framework. She later confirmed that intervention would be based on performance: “It is totally about performance—that is where the Secretary of State would be keen to intervene, if they had to.”

The Minister did not commit to an annual report on the use of intervention and failure powers and undertook to write to Lord Scriven on the proposed operational separation. Earl Howe said he remained concerned that policy and regulatory functions would be located within the same department, but withdrew Amendment 118A.

### Health Bill: the proposed single patient record
The Committee examined the proposed single patient record (SPR). Peers broadly supported interoperable records but sought statutory limits to direct care, clear data-controller responsibilities, cyber-security safeguards, patient opt-outs, audit logs, inclusion of social-care and independent-provider information, and access for palliative-care providers and optometrists. The Government said the SPR would be rolled out incrementally, could include information from social care, palliative care, optometry, voluntary-sector and independent providers where it formed part of direct care, and would be supported by existing information standards and data-protection law. The Minister said: “The clause creates only a new data-sharing gateway for direct care; it creates no new powers to share data for secondary purposes.”

The Government said source organisations such as GP practices would remain controllers for their own data, while the Secretary of State was expected to control how information was combined, presented and audited; the Minister undertook to clarify controller responsibilities and indemnity in writing. Amendment 119 was withdrawn, Amendments 120 to 127 and 129 were not moved, and Government Amendment 128—limiting compulsory disclosure requirements to providers operating in England—was agreed. The debate on Amendment 130 was adjourned when the House resumed; the sitting ended at 11.57 pm.

### Church Appointments (Repeal of Discriminatory Provision) Bill
The Bill completed Committee and remaining stages without amendment. Baroness Smith of Basildon informed the House that the King had consented to place his prerogative and interests, so far as affected by the Bill, at Parliament’s disposal: “His Majesty, having been informed of the purport of the Church Appointments (Repeal of Discriminatory Provision) Bill, has consented to place his prerogative and interests”. The Bill was read a third time and passed.
