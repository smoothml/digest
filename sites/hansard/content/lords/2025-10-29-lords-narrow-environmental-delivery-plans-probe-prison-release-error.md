## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords continued Report on the Planning and Infrastructure Bill, with peers agreeing a major change to Environmental Delivery Plans (EDPs) to limit them to nutrients, water quality/resource and air quality. Amendments to introduce gambling cumulative impact assessments, an SME duty in planning, a play sufficiency duty, and extended entry‑notice requirements were all defeated. Ministers advanced technical amendments on cross‑border operation and Ramsar site protections, and confirmed they will use policy and guidance (rather than building regulations) for features such as swift bricks and bird‑safe design. Oral Questions covered an EU youth mobility scheme, anti‑corruption measures, water regulation, antisemitism in universities, and UK support after Hurricane Melissa; a ministerial Statement set out strengthened release checks and an independent inquiry following an erroneous prisoner release.

## Detailed Summary
### Planning Bill – Amendment 114 (Climate change ‘special regard’ duty)
Lord Ravensdale argued for a statutory duty on Ministers and planning authorities to have “special regard” to climate mitigation/adaptation, to give climate more weight in planning: “would effectively prioritise or upweight climate within the planning system.” [ref: a1322.0/2] He said litigation concerns were unfounded and cited other climate duties [ref: a1322.0/3]. The Government pointed to existing law/policy and an NPPF consultation, warning a new legal duty “risks opening many decisions to potential legal challenges.” [ref: a1328.0/8] They promised clearer national policies via consultation this year [ref: a1328.0/6]. The amendment was withdrawn: “I beg leave to withdraw my amendment.” [ref: a1331.0/4]

### Amendments 115–116: Front‑loading habitats assessments in plans
Baroness Willis proposed requiring Habitats Regulations and strategic environmental assessments at the local plan/spatial strategy stage to steer development and speed decisions: “ensure that compliance with habitats regulations assessments happens earlier in the process” [ref: a1331.3/1]. The Government replied that HRAs already apply to plans and that spatial development strategies cannot allocate sites, so site‑specific HRAs must be done later: “local planning authorities are already required to undertake habitats regulations assessments” [ref: a1335.0/2]; and “the Bill explicitly prohibits such allocations.” [ref: a1335.0/5] Amendment 115 was withdrawn and 116 not moved [ref: a1336.0/4; a1336.0/5].

### Amendment 117: Gambling cumulative impact assessments; SME duty
Lord Foster’s amendment would allow licensing authorities to publish gambling cumulative impact assessments (CIAs) to inform planning refusals, mirroring alcohol licensing—CIAs would act “as grounds for refusing permission for yet more gambling premises.” [ref: a1337.0/3] Ministers said CIAs will be introduced “when parliamentary time allows” but via licensing legislation: “This is a planning Bill, not a licensing Bill.” [ref: a1346.0/13; a1346.0/3] Division result: Ayes 97, Noes 128—amendment disagreed [ref: a1349.4/1]. A separate duty to have regard to SME burdens (Amendment 119) was opposed as unnecessary given ongoing reforms [ref: a1347.4/4–a1347.4/6] and was defeated: Ayes 133, Noes 188 [ref: a1352.1/1].

### Amendment 121E: Play sufficiency duty
Baroness Bennett moved a duty on planning authorities to ensure sufficiency of play. She noted intended support had fallen away: “I believed we would have significant support” [ref: a1355.0/1]. The Deputy Speaker declared the amendment disagreed on a show of voices [ref: a1355.0/2].

### EDPs: Scope limited to key environmental impacts (Amendment 130)
Baroness Willis argued EDPs, as drafted, risked “a further layer of potential bureaucracy” and should focus on known blockages developers face (nutrients, water and air) [ref: a1359.0/2; a1359.0/4]. The Government said EDPs must be evidence‑based and pass an “overall improvement” test, with Natural England advising [ref: a1383.4/4]. The House agreed Amendment 130 to limit EDPs to nutrient neutrality, water quality, water resource or air quality: Ayes 260, Noes 141 [ref: a1388.3/2; a1388.5/1].

### Cross‑border operation and Ramsar protections
Ministers brought technical amendments to manage cross‑border effects (consulting NRW/NatureScot) and to allow the NRF/EDPs to operate for Ramsar wetlands by placing policy protections on a statutory footing: “we are putting Ramsar protections on a statutory footing.” [ref: a1396.2/7] They explained previous wording had referred only to “Ramsar sites in England,” which risked gaps [ref: a1396.2/7].

### Swift bricks, invasive species and biodiversity features in buildings
Lord Roborough sought to require eradication of invasive non‑native species where they threaten EDP environmental features [ref: a1405.2/1]. The Government said control can be a conservation measure where it is the best option [ref: a1411.0/3]. On swift bricks (Amendment 245), Lord Goldsmith urged a building‑regulations mandate—“the average two‑bedroom brick house … uses around 20,000 red bricks … one of those bricks has a hole in it.” [ref: a1405.3/3] Lord Krebs cautioned that swifts “are very specialised aerial feeders and flyers” needing long approach paths, so boxes may often not work [ref: a1407.1/2]. Ministers preferred planning policy, noting building regulations “were not designed to apply to the protection of wildlife,” and said they “will be consulting on a new requirement for integral nest boxes” in the NPPF [ref: a1411.0/7; a1411.0/8]. A broader bid (Amendment 140) to mandate biodiversity features via building regulations was also declined, with Ministers pointing to planning policy and future consultation [ref: a1419.1/3].

### EDP levy and delivery: private sector role and securing measures
Lord Curry’s Amendment 182A sought to require Natural England to procure conservation/monitoring through open competition, favouring private market solutions, warning otherwise of bureaucracy and delay [ref: a1428.3/6–a1428.3/7]. Ministers said Natural England will work with third parties and that guidance will expect competitive procurement “wherever possible,” while retaining direct delivery when necessary [ref: a1431.0/3]. The amendment was defeated: Ayes 52, Noes 113 [ref: a1461.2/1–a1461.2/2]. Separately, a proposal to mandate Section 106 agreements for all EDP landholdings was rejected as “an odd use of Section 106” and too restrictive [ref: a1402.0/4].

### Entry powers and compulsory purchase
Amendments to give farmers 21 days’ notice before Natural England exercises powers of entry were resisted to keep alignment with other statutes; Ministers said the Bill is “consistent with powers of entry requirements in other legislation,” with longer notice only for statutory undertakers [ref: a1463.2/2]. Division: Ayes 50, Noes 115—amendment defeated [ref: a1464.2/1]. A bid to remove Natural England’s CPO powers (Clause 83) was withdrawn after Government confirmed the Crichel Down rules apply where land acquired by CPO is genuinely surplus—surplus land “should be offered back” to former owners [ref: a1466.3/1–a1466.3/2].

### Sustainable drainage (SUDS)
Baroness McIntosh sought to commence Schedule 3 to the 2010 Act and to link sewer connections to SUDS standards, arguing developers currently have an “automatic right to connect … irrespective of whether there is a capacity” [ref: a1467.2/2–a1467.2/3]. Ministers said the updated NPPF already “requires SUDS for all developments that have drainage implications” and that “There is no automatic right to connect to the sewer system.” [ref: a1475.0/3; a1475.0/4] The amendments were withdrawn.

### Heritage trees
A proposed new heritage tree preservation order regime was debated. Government said the existing tree preservation order system “remains a vital mechanism for safeguarding trees,” with historic/cultural/ecological value already considered [ref: a1480.1/2]. The amendment was withdrawn.

### Nuclear power planning derogations (Amendment 202)
A new clause would allow the Secretary of State to disregard habitats/EIA/EDP provisions for nuclear stations over 500 MW and to cap environmental statement size, to accelerate delivery. Proponents called it “a balanced approach that gives the Secretary of State the choice” to prioritise delivery [ref: a1482.0/9]. Ministers resisted, saying it “would create uncertainty for developers and erode public support,” pointing instead to an imminent National Policy Statement (EN‑7) and wider reforms [ref: a1485.1/2]. The amendment was withdrawn.

### Oral Questions: Youth Mobility Scheme with the EU
Ministers said negotiations are under way on a “balanced youth experience scheme” with visa, cap and time limits [ref: a1366.3/1], with further details “in the coming months.” [ref: a1366.5/1] They highlighted existing routes: teaching assistant exchanges and the Turing scheme’s “over 35,000 opportunities” [ref: a1368.0/1], and stressed inclusion—“61% of participants in the Turing scheme in England come from underprivileged backgrounds.” [ref: a1369.0/1]

### Oral Questions: Tackling corruption in low‑ and middle‑income countries
Government reaffirmed efforts against illicit finance via the National Crime Agency’s anti‑corruption units and promised a new strategy: “remain committed to reducing the harms of illicit finance” [ref: a1370.0/1]. Ministers cited returning assets—“We returned millions of pounds recently to Nigeria” [ref: a1371.1/1]—and engaging overseas territories on transparency [ref: a1371.5/1]. On frozen Russian/Libyan assets, they noted current limits: “we do not have the power … to seize those assets in a way that is straightforward.” [ref: a1372.1/1]

### Oral Questions: Water companies and private ownership
Ministers rejected renationalisation as too costly—“at least £100 billion” [ref: a1373.4/1]—and stressed tougher regulation, compensation rules and curbs on executive bonuses [ref: a1373.2/1; a1373.4/1]. They backed a single water regulator per the Cunliffe review [ref: a1374.2/1]. On Thames Water, preparations for special administration were noted, with assurance it would not fall on customers—“we will make sure that it is not at bill payers’ expense.” [ref: a1375.2/1]

### Oral Questions: Antisemitism in universities
The Government condemned the “harassment” of Jewish academics/students—“It is not free speech; it is harassment and it is abhorrent.” [ref: a1376.3/1] They pointed to OFS condition E6 on preventing harassment, ministerial engagement with vice‑chancellors, and support for security/chaplaincy [ref: a1376.5/1; a1377.1/1]. Ministers underlined that protest must not disrupt or intimidate: “we do not believe in a right to disrupt.” [ref: a1379.2/1]

### Oral Questions: Jamaica—Hurricane Melissa response
The UK announced “up to £2.5 million in emergency humanitarian aid” and crisis support, including HMS “Trent” and response teams [ref: a1379.5/1; a1380.0/1]. Ministers stressed working under Jamaican leadership and highlighted resilience measures such as parametric insurance for rapid payouts [ref: a1382.3/1; a1381.0/2].

### Statement: Prisoner Release Checks
Following an erroneous prisoner release, the Minister said “any release in error is one too many” and announced immediate strengthened release checks and “an independent investigation” led by Dame Lynne Owens [ref: a1449.0/3–a1449.0/4]. On a reported £500 payment to expedite deportation, he said it “was an operational decision … far cheaper than booking more flights” [ref: a1449.0/7]. He also set the context of wider prison pressures and reforms to come.
