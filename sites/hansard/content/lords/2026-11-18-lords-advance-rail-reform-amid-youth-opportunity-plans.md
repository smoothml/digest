## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords considered questions on land use, deportation, prison rehabilitation and the age of consent, with Ministers describing current policy and making several commitments to provide further information. It gave a first reading to the Immigration and Asylum Bill, approved three groups of statutory-instrument motions and agreed Commons amendments to the Sporting Events Bill. Report-stage consideration of the Railways Bill covered public ownership, devolution, fares, ticket retailing, funding, industrial relations, staffing, freight, passenger growth and transport integration; a number of amendments were agreed, while others were withdrawn or not moved. The House also considered the Government’s response to the Milburn review, including a £500 million budget for the youth opportunity service and plans for a White Paper followed by legislation.

## Detailed Summary
### Land Use Framework
Lord Roborough asked what the Land Use Framework would deliver on planning costs and decision times. Baroness Hayman of Ullock said it would support development “faster and at lower cost”, through clearer land-use priorities, practical decision-making principles and better spatial data, while safeguarding natural assets and productive farmland. Subsequent questions covered the possible loss of agricultural land to renewable energy and housing, food security, tenant compensation, rewilding grants, brownfield development, affordable housing, water use and the planning system.

The Minister said that 2% of England’s agricultural land was likely to undergo land-use change towards renewable-energy infrastructure by 2050, although some land could continue to produce food. She said planning policy should steer development away from higher-quality agricultural land and that the agricultural land classification system was under review. She also said tenants should receive fair compensation where they lost land to non-agricultural development, and that forthcoming Law Commission work would examine statutory compensation and representation. A monitoring report would be more appropriate after the Framework had had time to “bed in and start making changes”. The transcript does not state a timetable for the Law Commission work or for such a report.

### Deportation of Shabir Ahmed and returns policy
Lord Cameron of Lochiel asked about progress towards deporting Shabir Ahmed and whether visa penalties or withdrawal of foreign aid had been considered. Lord Collins of Highbury said the Immigration and Asylum Bill had been amended so that foreign criminals such as Ahmed could be given deportation orders, and that the Government were pursuing every option, but that it would be inappropriate to provide a running commentary on discussions with other countries. He cited nearly 12,000 foreign national offender returns between 1 July 2024 and 31 July 2026, and said returns had increased by 41% compared with the final two years of the previous Government.

There were differing views on sanctions and aid. Lord Mohammed of Tinsley warned that withdrawing aid could harm women and girls who relied on education and healthcare, while Lord Jackson of Peterborough called for funding and visa arrangements to be reviewed. Lord Collins said dialogue with other countries was necessary for an effective returns process. He confirmed that Ahmed had been deprived of United Kingdom citizenship and that the Government were determined to deport him. The Bill was subsequently brought from the Commons, read a first time and ordered to be printed. Lord Collins said he would write to Lord Anderson of Swansea after being asked for figures on returns to Pakistan and the Rochdale offenders; he did not provide those figures in the debate.

### Prison education, employment and rehabilitation
Baroness Hyde of Bemerton said education and employment were each associated with reductions in reoffending of up to nine percentage points. She said prison education and work-focused activities developed skills, and that the Government were evaluating a new prisoner education service while monitoring education, skills and employment provision. Questions addressed access to teaching in overcrowded prisons, homelessness after release, vocational qualifications, funding, early release and recall, opportunities for young prisoners, addiction support, libraries and care leavers.

The Minister said prison leavers who were homeless had reoffending rates around twice those of people released into settled accommodation. She cited a commitment to halve the proportion of prison leavers released homeless and increase the proportion in settled accommodation three months after release by the end of the Parliament. She undertook to take back a query about core training for teachers, write about training at HMP Highpoint and confirm whether a commitment on Alcoholics Anonymous and Narcotics Anonymous provision had been fulfilled. No division or further decision was recorded.

### Age of consent and sexual exploitation
Baroness Hazarika asked about staggering the age of consent in the context of allegations of sexual exploitation by older and powerful people. Lord Lemos agreed that a wider conversation was needed, described the issue as complicated and said that action could also be taken by broadcasters, online companies and through education. Lord Marks of Henley-on-Thames asked whether the Law Commission’s review of consent in criminal law could include the age of consent. Lord Lemos confirmed that its 14th programme of work would review consent in criminal law, including sexual offences, and that the Government would consider its findings.

Baroness Harman argued that the law should address the power imbalance between older adults and 16-year-olds, while Lord Pannick cautioned against criminalising consensual relationships between 16-year-olds. Lord Lemos said the Government should not inadvertently criminalise consensual relationships between young people, but would look again at sexual relations between 16-year-olds and older people and legislate if necessary. He said the voting age would not be reconsidered because legislating for voting at 16 was a manifesto commitment. On safeguarding young people working or volunteering in the House, he said the matter was for the House authorities and expected them to write to Baroness Uddin.

### Formal business: first reading and statutory instruments
The Immigration and Asylum Bill was brought from the Commons, read a first time and ordered to be printed. The House agreed motions approving the Magistrates’ Courts (Recording and Broadcasting) Order 2026, the Persistent Organic Pollutants (Amendment) Regulations 2026 and the North Atlantic Salmon Conservation Organization (Immunities and Privileges) Order 2026. No debate or additional next step was recorded for these procedural items.

### Sporting Events Bill: Commons amendments
The House considered and agreed Commons Amendments 1 to 60 to the Sporting Events Bill. Baroness Twycross said the amendments would extend the framework to significant recurrent sporting events with international significance, while adding safeguards including case-by-case application, a maximum five-year duration, protection for licensed traders and recovery of certain additional enforcement costs. Baroness Evans of Bowes Park welcomed the wider scope but questioned why recurrent domestic events would bear enforcement costs when one-off international events would not, and asked how additional costs would be distinguished from existing enforcement work.

Lord Fuller opposed the extension, arguing that it could give event organisers monopoly ticketing powers and harm fans and regulated secondary-ticketing markets. Lord Addington and Lord Markham supported the general approach but sought clarification on how events would gain or lose protected status, pop-up parking and healthcare-registration costs. Baroness Twycross said guidance would make clear that cost recovery could not include prosecution costs and that local authorities were expected to make guidance available to licensed traders at least six months before an event. She undertook to write on pop-up parking. Both motions—on Amendment 1 and on Amendments 2 to 60—were agreed.

### Railways Bill: public ownership, governance and devolution
Lord Moylan’s Amendment 1 sought to remove the prohibition on Great British Railways (GBR) ceasing to be wholly Crown-owned. He argued that the provision could constrain a future elected Government and criticised the proposed combination of operational and regulatory responsibilities. Baroness Pidgeon and Lord Hendy of Richmond Hill opposed the amendment, arguing that public ownership was a manifesto commitment and that any future change should require an Act of Parliament. Lord Hendy said the provision did not prevent a future Minister from “considering, taking advice on or legislating on privatising the railways”. Lord Moylan withdrew Amendment 1 after that explanation.

Baroness Jones of Moulsecoomb raised concerns about possible private shareholdings in GBR subsidiaries, delegation of functions, passenger-service contracting, equality duties and regulatory oversight, but withdrew Amendment 2. Lord Hendy said the Government’s approach would allow GBR to “harness private sector investment effectively” while retaining public control and ownership of core functions. The Government’s Amendment 3, clarifying the agency status of GBR companies, was agreed. Amendment 4, requiring GBR to publish reasons for rejecting proposed co-operation arrangements with local government, and Amendment 5 on foundation strategic authorities were agreed. Government Amendments 14 and 15, including separate objectives for Welsh services and wider consultation with strategic authorities, were also agreed.

### Railways Bill: fares, ticket retailing and consumer protection
Lord Moylan argued that directions on fares should be accompanied by publication of their assumptions and objectives, and that fare evasion should feature in GBR’s licence. Lord Hendy said fares objectives would instead be addressed through the business plan and the planned public service contract, while fare-evasion measures were more appropriately set out in the business plan because operational responses needed flexibility. Amendment 7 was withdrawn, Amendment 8 was not moved and Amendment 8A was not moved.

The Government introduced protections requiring fair, transparent and non-discriminatory access for independent ticket retailers to GBR’s products, systems, data and services, with monitoring by the Office of Rail and Road (ORR). Baroness Harding of Winscombe and Lord Dixon of Jericho argued that separate reporting of online-retailing costs and revenues was needed to identify cross-subsidy and protect competition. Although the Government argued that existing competition and subsidy-control law and the business plan were sufficient, Amendment 16 was agreed on division, with 205 votes in favour and 174 against. Amendments 18 and 21, concerning online-retailing cross-subsidy and publication of the ORR’s advice, were also agreed. Government Amendments 9, 12 and 22 on fair access and direct consumer-law enforcement by the ORR were agreed.

### Railways Bill: funding, industrial relations and staffing
Lord Moylan and Lord Harper argued that GBR should minimise reliance on taxpayers, link pay increases to measurable productivity gains, and address minimum service levels, industrial-action ballot thresholds and the possibility of national rail strikes. Baroness Pidgeon opposed the industrial-relations amendments, saying they would create a railway-specific regime after Parliament had repealed the minimum-service-level regime. Lord Hendy argued that a duty to minimise taxpayer subsidy could encourage cost-cutting or fare increases rather than value for money, and that workforce policy should not be fixed in a 30-year strategy.

Amendment 16 had already been agreed in this part of the proceedings; Amendments 18 and 21 were also agreed. Amendment 17 and Amendment 24 were withdrawn, while Amendments 19, 20 and 25 were not moved. The House also debated driver-only operation, particularly in connection with East West Rail. Lord Moylan and Lord Berkeley argued that staffing disputes had delayed passenger services, while Lord Hendy said staffing models had to reflect safety, accessibility and route-specific circumstances and should not be prescribed in primary legislation. The transcript records no final procedural outcome for the other industrial-relations or driver-only-operation proposals discussed in this part.

### Milburn Review: young people and work
The House considered a Commons Statement on Alan Milburn’s final review of young people and work. The Government supported an opportunity and support guarantee for young people not earning or learning, a more personalised locally delivered service, a youth opportunities unit reporting to the Prime Minister and earlier access to a six-month jobs guarantee after 12 months on Universal Credit rather than 18 months. Baroness Stedman-Scott questioned whether the proposals would reduce employer costs and create unsubsidised jobs, while Lord Palmer of Childs Hill asked about funding, staffing, assessment criteria and the timetable.

Baroness Smith of Malvern said that £500 million would be available as a budget for the youth opportunity service in the following year. She said the Government would respond fully after the Timms review, with a White Paper followed by legislation. Questions also covered local delivery, rural transport, technical education, poverty, business support, national insurance and employment rights. The Minister did not commit to an immediate reduction in national insurance, saying that tax and national-insurance decisions would be made by the Chancellor at a Budget.

### Railways Bill: passenger growth, freight and transport integration
After the Statement, the Railways Bill returned to Report. Lord Beith and others argued that rail planning should integrate trains with buses, coaches, trams, light rail and ferries through co-ordinated timetables, ticketing and passenger information. Lord Berkeley sought a more detailed rail-freight target, infrastructure improvements and annual ORR monitoring, while Baroness Pidgeon sought a passenger-growth target. Lord Hendy said the Government’s integrated-transport strategy included 40 commitments, including simpler ticketing, better data and a rural demonstrator programme, and that GBR would have to consider national and local transport strategies.

Lord Hendy said passenger growth was supported by GBR’s duty to promote the interests of “users and potential users of railway passenger services”. He said the freight targets were 40% growth by 2040 and 75% by 2050, and that the business-plan process would require GBR to explain how it would deliver them. The ORR would advise on deliverability and monitor performance; if it considered that GBR was failing to follow its plan or meet the target, it could publish information or refer the matter to the Secretary of State. Lord Beith withdrew Amendment 27. Amendments 28 to 31, 32 and 33 to 38 were not moved. The transcript records that consideration on Report was adjourned and that the House adjourned at 9.02 pm.
