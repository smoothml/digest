## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords considered the Health Bill in Committee, including the single patient record, data governance, social care, patient safety, maternity care, organ donation, disability services and research involving controlled substances. Government amendments were agreed on devolved consent, Healthwatch guidance, the Maternity and Neonatal Commissioner and disclosure offences, while the proposed transfer of the Health Services Safety Investigations Body (HSSIB) to the Care Quality Commission was removed from the Bill. The House also held questions on remote English-language testing, technical education, ADHD, the EDITH breast-screening trial and the Ofgem energy price cap, and considered a Statement on Myanmar. The sitting ended at 10.58 pm after further Health Bill amendments were withdrawn or not moved and two Bills were returned from the Commons.

## Detailed Summary
### Health Bill: single patient record, confidentiality and safeguards
The Committee resumed debate on Clause 52 and amendments concerning consent, confidentiality, sensitive information, access logs, consultation, accessibility and parliamentary scrutiny. Speakers generally supported the principle of a single patient record but raised concerns about public and professional trust, the treatment of psychotherapy records, sexual and reproductive health information, and the proposed power to lift the common-law duty of confidentiality. Baroness Harding said, "I am a huge supporter", but also warned that "Trust is really hard to build and very easily broken". Lord Patel argued that the provision "removes the common law of patient confidentiality".

Baroness Merron said the power was intended to give providers legal certainty when sharing information for direct care, including in arrangements involving private care homes, and was not intended to remove patient–clinician confidentiality. She referred to role-based access controls, logging, auditing, consultation, an equality impact assessment and draft regulations being shared before they were laid. She said regulations were anticipated in 2027 and undertook to examine the technical issue of recording access for research and training. Amendment 130 was withdrawn; Amendments 131 to 133 were not moved; and Clause 52 was agreed. The Minister also undertook further engagement before Report.

### Health Bill: data fields, interoperability and devolved information systems
The Committee debated proposals concerning the recording of biological sex, ethnicity and diagnostic information, women’s health data, anonymisation and pseudonymisation, and interoperability across the four nations. Supporters argued that accurate and appropriately disaggregated data could support diagnosis, screening, prescribing, research and the monitoring of inequalities. Baroness Cash described the single patient record as "the foundation of future medical care", while Baroness Davies quoted the finding that the loss of sex data "poses risks to individuals". Other speakers argued that rigid statutory categories could undermine privacy, dignity and flexibility; Baroness Barker said the amendments were "specifically crafted to exclude those people", referring to transgender and intersex people.

The Government said existing information standards and the unified information standard for protected characteristics provided the appropriate framework, and that the single patient record would draw from source systems rather than being the primary collection point for all such data. The Minister said interoperability work was continuing through contracts and mandatory information standards, and that the Government would continue working with the devolved Governments. Amendments 151 to 155 and 157 were agreed. They require consent from the appropriate national authority for information systems relating to Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland and apply conditions attached to that consent to requests, publication and disclosure.

### Health Bill: research, data sovereignty and artificial intelligence
Amendments 142, 143, 172, 172C, 278 and 279Q addressed research access to confidential patient information, the sovereignty and commercial value of NHS data, national arrangements for evaluating and procuring artificial intelligence, and the protection of neural data. Lord Tarassenko argued that large-scale lawful access was needed to validate artificial-intelligence tools across hospitals and patient groups, citing research using mammograms to predict cardiovascular risk. He said Amendment 142 would require draft regulations within 12 months of the single patient record provisions coming into force, following consultation and with safeguards for privacy and objections. Baroness Kidron argued that the economic and technical value of technology developed from NHS data should be retained in the United Kingdom, while Lord Markham cautioned against treating data sovereignty as a requirement to use UK-supplied technology.

Baroness Merron said the Government planned to consult on changes to the Control of Patient Information Regulations and publish a response. She said the Health Data Research Service board, rather than the Secretary of State, would set its strategy and delivery roadmap. She also said existing data-protection law, NHS standards, role-based access controls and audit arrangements would apply, while broad restrictions could hinder international research collaboration. She confirmed that the recommendations of the Sudlow review would be considered: "I am sure that they will". Amendment 142 was withdrawn; Amendments 143 to 143B were not moved; and Clause 52 was agreed before the Committee moved on.

### Oral questions: remote English-language testing, technical education and ADHD
The House questioned the proposed fully remote Home Office English Language Test, including risks of impersonation, cheating, artificial-intelligence-enabled fraud, international comparisons and independent auditing. The Minister said procurement was live and that the Government would adopt only a system meeting the required security and integrity standards: "we will adopt only a solution that demonstrates it can meet the Home Office’s robust security and integrity requirements". He said the system would be kept under review. On technical education, the Government described proposed technical routes for 14 to 16 year-olds, careers guidance, higher technical qualifications, apprenticeship funding and new vocational GCSEs. The Minister said, "A university degree will be the right choice for some young people, but it is not the only route to a successful career", and acknowledged that employer participation remained insufficient.

Questions on ADHD concerned progress against the independent taskforce’s recommendations, data, workforce development, private diagnoses, co-occurring conditions and the diagnosis of women and girls. The Minister referred to an ADHD data improvement plan, work on commissioning and payment guidance, and a neurodevelopmental credential pilot. She did not provide a complete list of recommendations that would or would not be implemented, saying, "I cannot list them", but said the Government would keep Members updated.

### EDITH breast-screening AI trial
The House discussed the National Institute for Health and Care Research-funded early detection using information technology and health (EDITH) trial. The Minister said it would test whether artificial intelligence could support earlier breast-cancer detection, free specialist time and increase screening, with performance assessed across age, ethnicity and socioeconomic groups. In response to concerns about human accountability, she said, "AI will not be working alone; it will be working with experts". Members also asked about younger women, BRCA-related surgery, racial disparities, commercial use of data and post-market surveillance.

The Minister said the trial would operate within the existing screening programme, that women at very high risk could receive earlier checks, and that the protocol and findings would be published. She said the trial would involve about 30 centres across four nations and assess performance by ethnicity, age, socioeconomic status and breast density. She also said the Government had accepted all 44 recommendations of the National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare.

### Ofgem energy price cap
The House considered the announced 26 per cent increase in the Ofgem energy price cap from 1 January 2027. The Government said, "Policy and network costs have not contributed to today’s price cap increase", attributing the rise to higher wholesale costs associated with global conflict. Lord Moynihan questioned the contribution of grid investment, renewable-energy curtailment and carbon-market changes, while other Members discussed the effect of dependence on gas and the need to decouple electricity prices from gas prices.

The Minister referred to the removal of £150 of policy costs from bills, the removal of value added tax from domestic electricity, household payments and the planned removal of gas standing charges. She said the Government’s longer-term priorities included renewable investment, grid development and decoupling electricity prices from gas. On targeted support, she said the kick-starter programme and wider data-matching work were continuing and offered to provide further details.

### Formal motions before the resumed Health Bill Committee
The House agreed the order-of-consideration motions for the Courts and Tribunals Bill and the Public Office (Accountability) Bill. In each case, the motion was agreed without further debate: "Motion agreed". The Health Bill Committee then resumed consideration of social care and related matters.

### Health Bill: social care reform and the care-cost cap
Lord Lansley proposed bringing Sections 15 and 16 of the Care Act 2014 into force and requiring regulations setting a cap on eligible care costs. He argued that Parliament had legislated for protection against unlimited care costs and that implementation should not be deferred indefinitely. Lord Jamieson sought clarification on the proposed national care service, its relationship with local authorities, funding, workforce arrangements and co-operation between health and social care. Supporters also argued that a cap could limit catastrophic costs and provide a basis for care insurance. Lord Lansley said the failure to implement the provisions was "a contempt of Parliament".

Baroness Hyde said "it would be premature to commence a specific model of charging reform" before the independent Casey commission reported. She said the commission was expected to report in summer 2027, a fair-pay agreement for the social-care workforce was being introduced in 2028, and additional funding was planned. She said existing legislation already required co-operation between local authorities and the NHS. Amendment 177 was withdrawn; Amendments 178 to 180B were not moved.

### Health Bill: cross-border commissioning, devolved authorities and Crown dependencies
The Committee considered arrangements for information services and commissioning across the United Kingdom, including the role of the devolved Governments and co-operation with the Crown dependencies. Baroness Coffey questioned the need for powers allowing the Secretary of State to commission services anywhere in the United Kingdom and sought greater transparency over arrangements with the Isle of Man, Guernsey, Jersey, Sark and Alderney. Other Members highlighted the practical need for cross-border specialist services and information-sharing.

Baroness Hyde said, "The intention of these amendments is to provide continuity of service", because functions currently exercised by NHS England would transfer to the Secretary of State. Amendments 181, 182 and 183 were agreed. They require Scottish Ministers’ consent for specified arrangements with Scottish health bodies and extend the relevant commissioning provision across the United Kingdom. Amendments 183A and 183B were not moved; Clauses 58 and 59, as amended, and Clauses 60 to 64 were agreed. The Minister undertook to write on the proposed pan-ICB commissioning arrangements and hub-and-spoke models.

### Health Bill: HSSIB, Healthwatch and patient-safety structures
The Committee considered the proposed transfer of the Health Services Safety Investigations Body (HSSIB) to the Care Quality Commission, together with the follow-up of HSSIB recommendations and the possible extension of its remit to adult social care. Members supported retaining HSSIB as an independent, systems-focused investigator with statutory safe-space protections. Lord Scriven argued that written responses to recommendations did not ensure implementation, saying, "There is no duty to act and no duty to finish what was promised".

Baroness Merron confirmed that the Government "will remove Clauses 65 to 67 and Schedule 9 from the Bill". She said HSSIB would strengthen its internal follow-up arrangements and that a co-ordinated approach to patient-safety recommendations was being developed, but that the Government did not intend to expand HSSIB’s resources or functions. Clauses 65 and 66, Clause 67 and Schedule 9 were disagreed to. Amendment 186A, concerning Healthwatch guidance, was agreed, while Clause 70, Schedule 10 and Clause 71 were disagreed to. Amendment 186 was not moved.

### Health Bill: maternity and neonatal care
The Government introduced a statutory Maternity and Neonatal Commissioner for England following the national maternity and neonatal investigation. The proposed commissioner would promote improvements, conduct reviews, seek the views of women and others, request information, require public-authority co-operation, make recommendations and publish reports. Baroness Amos said the maternity and neonatal system was fragmented and lacked accountability, and members also debated perinatal mental-health assessments, stillbirth investigations, inequalities and implementation of the women’s health strategy.

The Minister said the commissioner would be appointed through an open public appointment process and could conduct thematic reviews without direction from the Secretary of State: "the commissioner will be able to conduct thematic reviews without needing direction from the Secretary of State". She said national standards under a modern service framework were expected by summer 2027. The Government did not accept a statutory requirement for repeated perinatal mental-health assessments before wider work had been completed. They reported that 84 of 117 women’s health strategy actions were complete or under way. Amendments 187, 188, 192 to 194 and 201 were agreed; Amendments 189 to 191 and 195 to 200E were not moved; and Schedule 11 was disagreed to.

### Health Bill: organ donation and funeral-sector regulation
Lord Hunt proposed reviews of the Organ Donation (Deemed Consent) Act 2019, NHS transplantation services and Human Tissue Authority guidance. Members discussed falling family-consent rates, the transplant waiting list, variation between centres, paediatric transplantation, specialist staffing and public education. Lord Hunt said the Government needed to give NHS Blood and Transplant a clear priority: "What NHSBT needs is a green light to really go for it". Baroness Finlay separately raised the regulation of funeral directors and proposed placing oversight with the Human Tissue Authority, referring to gaps in oversight and the Scottish model.

The Minister said, "We are committed to reversing the decline in consent rates", and referred to NHS Blood and Transplant’s review of specialist-nurse guidance and training, the organ donation recovery plan and a heart and lung transplant transformation programme due to conclude in March. She said the Human Tissue Authority’s code had been updated and that work was under way on comprehensive funeral-sector proposals, including formal regulation. Amendment 207 was withdrawn; Amendments 208 to 211 were not moved. The wider proposals on funeral-sector regulation were not resolved in the transcript.

### Myanmar: International Court of Justice judgment and UK response
The House considered a Statement made in the House of Commons concerning the International Court of Justice judgment on Myanmar and the Rohingya. The Government formally recognised the genocide, announcing sanctions against five senior military figures, planned legislation concerning aviation fuel, a £3 million accountability package, an additional £5 million in humanitarian assistance and diplomatic action through the United Nations Security Council and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations. The Statement said, "the United Kingdom formally acknowledges the genocide committed against the Rohingya".

Questions addressed the judgment’s majority, dissenting opinions and evidence, support for refugees in Bangladesh, sanctions, universal jurisdiction, sexual violence and the positions of China and Russia. The Minister said the UK had provided more than £457 million for Rohingya refugees and host communities in Bangladesh since 2017, had sanctioned five senior military figures and was considering further sanctions. She said the UK would seek wider international support and support accountability and assistance for women and girls. The timetable for legislation restricting aviation-fuel supply had not yet been fixed; the Minister said she would return to the House when it was available.

### Health Bill: medical-training prioritisation for overseas branch-campus graduates
Lord Clement-Jones proposed extending foundation-programme prioritisation to British citizens and people with indefinite leave to remain or settled status who hold medical degrees from international branch campuses of UK universities. Supporters argued that the existing geographical test had affected students who enrolled before the Medical Training (Prioritisation) Act 2026 and that some graduates remained without posts despite reported vacancies. Baroness Gerada called for transitional arrangements for the small number affected. The Government argued that prioritisation should reflect where doctors trained, NHS workforce planning and the likelihood of remaining in the country of training.

Baroness Hyde said, "Prioritisation does not mean exclusion", explaining that non-prioritised applicants could still receive places if vacancies remained and that 72 per cent of applicants on the reserve list had been allocated a 2026 foundation-programme place. She offered a meeting with officials. Lord Clement-Jones withdrew Amendment 220 and said he hoped transitional arrangements would be reconsidered.

### Health Bill: learning disabilities, communication and wheelchair services
Members debated proposals for a national learning-disability health strategy, statutory follow-up to learning-from-death reviews, a review of communication barriers and stronger national standards for wheelchair services and community equipment. Lord Scriven argued that existing programmes did not amount to a strategy and said, "The dataset counts the dead; it will not make anyone keep the next person alive". Baroness Monckton described barriers involving appointments, communication and reasonable adjustments. Lord Hunt and Baroness Grey-Thompson highlighted waiting times, local variation and the effects of inadequate wheelchair and community-equipment provision.

Baroness Merron said existing duties, annual health checks, training, accessible-information standards and the learning from life and death programme would continue, with the Secretary of State ultimately responsible for national programmes after NHS England’s abolition. She referred to a planned patient-level dataset and continuing work on learning-disability nursing and communication needs. On wheelchairs, Baroness Hyde said, "the DHSC will be responsible for ICB performance in this area", while relying on the wheelchair quality framework, existing data and local commissioning. Amendment 221 was withdrawn; Amendment 227 was withdrawn; Amendments 222 to 226 and 228 to 236 were not moved; and Amendments 238 to 255 were later not moved.

### Health Bill: research into Schedule 1 controlled substances
Baroness Hollins proposed reducing barriers to research involving Schedule 1 controlled drugs and creating a regulated compassionate-access route for patients with serious or life-threatening conditions. Supporters referred to research involving psilocybin and MDMA, treatment-resistant psychiatric conditions and end-of-life distress, while also acknowledging the need for authorisation, evidence and safeguards. Baroness Hollins said the proposal concerned "properly regulated clinical research, not to weaken controls".

The Government said, "Existing legislation already enables research through the controlled drugs licensing system", and that it had accepted in principle recommendations from the Advisory Council on the Misuse of Drugs to reduce licensing barriers. A cross-government group was considering implementation, and the Minister undertook to obtain a timetable from the Home Office and write to Baroness Hollins. She also offered a meeting with officials before Report. Amendment 237 was withdrawn; Amendments 238 to 255 were not moved. The transcript did not provide a timetable for implementation or establish a compassionate-access route.

### Closing formal proceedings
The House resumed after the Committee’s final debate, following a disagreement over whether to continue with one remaining group before the agreed 11 pm stopping time. Lord Kennedy said, "I had agreed a hard stop at 11 pm". The House then recorded that the Armed Forces Bill had been returned from the Commons with amendments and a reason, and that the Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill had been returned with reasons. The House adjourned at 10.58 pm.
