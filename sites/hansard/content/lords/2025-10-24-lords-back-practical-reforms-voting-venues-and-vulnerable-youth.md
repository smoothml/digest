## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords considered four Bills. The Absent Voting (Elections in Scotland and Wales) Bill completed Third Reading, with legislative consent from Scotland and Wales confirmed and the Bill passed. The Licensing Hours Extensions Bill received broad support at Second Reading to allow quicker, temporary national extensions to opening hours via the negative procedure. Peers also backed the Unauthorised Entry to Football Matches Bill at Second Reading, debating enforceability of a new specific offence linked to football banning orders. Finally, the Secure 16 to 19 Academies Bill had a supportive Second Reading to refine the framework for new ‘secure schools’.

## Detailed Summary
### Absent Voting (Elections in Scotland and Wales) Bill – Third Reading
Purpose and consent: The Bill aligns absent voting processes by enabling online applications for postal and proxy voting for devolved elections in Scotland and Wales. Legislative consent from both was noted: “Welsh and Scottish legislative consent granted” [ref: a1003.1/1]. Lord Murphy said this means voters “will be able to apply online for postal and proxy votes for the Senedd, the Scottish Parliament and for Scottish and Welsh local elections” [ref: a1005.0/1].

Debate and next steps: Lord Jamieson welcomed the Bill while urging Ministers to “reconsider any proposals to dilute voter ID requirements” [ref: a1003.4/4] and to “take decisive and proactive steps…to prevent malign influence, whether domestic or foreign” [ref: a1003.4/6]. For the Government, Baroness Taylor of Stevenage said “the Government will bring forward a number of changes in the forthcoming elections Bill” [ref: a1004.0/2]. Lord Rennard highlighted the “need to consolidate properly all election laws” [ref: a1004.1/2], and the Minister replied that “we need to make sure that election law keeps up” [ref: a1004.2/2]. Lord Murphy pressed for implementation ahead of next year’s devolved elections so people can “vote by proxy or by post” [ref: a1005.0/2]. Outcome: Motion agreed; “Bill passed” [ref: a1005.0/4].

### Licensing Hours Extensions Bill – Second Reading
Purpose: The Bill amends Section 172 of the Licensing Act 2003 to let Ministers make national licensing extensions by negative resolution for events “of exceptional international, national, or local significance” [ref: a1005.3/14]. The sponsor, Lord Watson of Wyre Forest, said it would “replace the affirmative procedure with the negative resolution procedure” [ref: a1005.3/10], preserving Parliament’s right to pray against an order [ref: a1005.3/12].

Debate: Peers argued the change would reduce red tape and help communities celebrate. Lord Hayward called it a “small but necessary deregulatory measure” [ref: a1012.0/4]. Some raised wider pressures on hospitality; Baroness Monckton said “110,000 jobs have been lost in this sector” [ref: a1008.0/5], and Lord Bailey cited “111,000 jobs” lost [ref: a1010.0/3]. For the Government, Lord Lemos confirmed, “The Government fully support the Bill” [ref: a1014.0/11], noting the negative procedure “would allow orders to be made even when Parliament is not sitting” [ref: a1014.0/9] and that “a call for evidence was launched” on broader licensing reforms [ref: a1014.0/6]. Outcome: Bill read a second time and committed to a Committee of the Whole House [ref: a1016.0/4].

### Unauthorised Entry to Football Matches Bill – Second Reading
Purpose: The Bill creates a specific offence to deter tailgating, forced entry and other unauthorised access at football matches. The sponsor, Lord Brennan of Canton, noted that currently “entering, or attempting to enter, a football match without a ticket is not a specific criminal offence” [ref: a1017.2/3]. The Bill would “introduce a new offence of unauthorised or attempted unauthorised entry to football matches” [ref: a1017.2/5], with convictions enabling football banning orders of between three and 10 years [ref: a1017.2/7].

Debate: Support was broad, with emphasis on enforceability. Lord Moynihan said it was “right that we give police and organisers the tools that they need” [ref: a1019.0/4] but warned, quoting Christopher Chope MP, that “an individual will be picked on every now and again” during mass incidents [ref: a1019.0/7]. Lord Shamash said, “I am particularly concerned about the mechanics of enforcement” [ref: a1027.2/2]. For the Government, Lord Lemos called it “a proportionate, targeted and necessary measure” [ref: a1030.0/9], stressing it is “not about criminalising honest mistakes” or “punishing fans who have been misled” [ref: a1030.0/7]. He added that facilitators may be prosecuted under the Fraud Act 2006 or the Theft Act 1968 [ref: a1030.0/8]. Outcome: Bill read a second time and committed to a Committee of the Whole House [ref: a1032.2/8].

### Secure 16 to 19 Academies Bill – Second Reading
Purpose: This short Bill makes targeted amendments to the Academies Act 2010 for secure 16 to 19 academies (‘secure schools’). It would “reduce the notice period for termination … from seven to two years” [ref: a1034.2/5], disapply Section 9 (local impact consideration) for these academies [ref: a1034.2/6], and amend Section 10 so providers consult on “how the secure school should work with local partners” [ref: a1034.2/7].

Debate and context: Peers endorsed the educational focus—“not ‘prisons with education’; they are primarily schools” [ref: a1041.0/8]—and highlighted unmet needs among children in custody. The Minister noted high reoffending—“66% of children released from custody go on to reoffend within a year” [ref: a1041.0/6]—and confirmed the first secure school’s temporary closure pending repairs, aiming to “reopen in early 2026” [ref: a1041.0/12]. Outcome: Bill read a second time and committed to a Committee of the Whole House [ref: a1044.0/4].
