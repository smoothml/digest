## High-Level Summary
The House of Lords held Oral Questions on offshore methane venting and flaring, the built environment’s economic role, diet and health, and disability employment before moving to substantial legislative business. Peers undertook extensive ping‑pong on the Employment Rights Bill, insisting on several Lords changes after multiple divisions, including retaining a qualifying period for unfair dismissal and keeping the 50% strike ballot turnout threshold. Report stage began on the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Bill, with the House agreeing government amendments widening offences against people‑smuggling and creating a new offence of online promotion of unlawful immigration services. Several statutory instruments were approved, the Dogs (Protection of Livestock) Bill’s committee stage was discharged, and the retirement of Baroness Meacher was announced.

## Detailed Summary
### Retirement of a Member: Baroness Meacher
The Lord Speaker announced the retirement of Baroness Meacher with effect from 28 October, thanking her for her service: "I thank the noble Baroness for her much‑valued service to the House." [ref: a1203.1/1] No further action was required.

### Offshore Oil and Gas: Venting and Flaring – Oral Question
The Government reiterated its commitment to the World Bank initiative to "zero routine flaring by 2030" [ref: a1203.5/1]. The Minister reported progress: "absolute methane emissions... have fallen by more than 60%, and flaring reduced by 51% between 2018 and 2024" [ref: a1204.0/1; a1205.5/2], with a methane action plan to be published "very, very soon" [ref: a1205.5/1]. Peers pressed for an earlier ban, stronger leak detection, and improved international monitoring after the MethaneSAT loss; the Minister cited consents, transparency and enforcement, including fines "of more than half a million pounds" [ref: a1204.0/1; a1205.3/1]. On COP30, he said: "We are advising the Brazilian Government" on zero routine flaring and working with other countries [ref: a1206.3/1]. No decisions were taken; the Minister committed to publish the methane plan shortly.

### Built Environment Sector – Oral Question
Ministers highlighted the sector’s scale: it "contributes hundreds of billions of pounds annually... supports millions of jobs" [ref: a1206.6/1]. They pointed to a £625 million skills package to "deliver up to 60,000 additional skilled workers" [ref: a1207.3/1], forthcoming technical colleges and a skills White Paper [ref: a1207.5/1], and SME support including "streamlined planning rules for small sites" and easing biodiversity net gain for small sites [ref: a1208.1/1]. Assurances were given that deregulation would not compromise standards: "We need to have the right standards, skills and funding" [ref: a1208.3/1]. Questions on land banking and the Gardens Trust’s consultee status will be answered in writing [ref: a1209.2/1; a1209.4/1].

### Plant‑based Diet – Oral Question
Ministers reiterated the UK’s Eatwell Guide, linking predominantly plant‑based diets to improved health and environmental outcomes [ref: a1210.0/1]. They said the Department for Education is updating school food standards, focusing on foods "high in saturated fat, salt and sugar" [ref: a1210.4/1], and emphasised the Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition’s independence: members must not hold "significant interests" in relevant industries [ref: a1210.4/2]. Defra will publish a farming road map "in due course" and noted the Sustainable Farming Incentive has "more than 39,000 multi‑year live agreements" [ref: a1211.1/1‑a1211.1/2]. A neonicotinoids update will be provided by letter [ref: a1212.3/1].

### People with Disabilities: Employment – Oral Question
The Government promoted the Connect to Work supported employment programme, where specialist advisers support participants and employers to create inclusive workplaces [ref: a1213.3/1]. Ministers prioritised support over tax changes, with the independent Keep Britain Working review to be "out very soon" [ref: a1215.2/1], £167 million to expand Connect to Work with all areas "open by early next year" [ref: a1215.0/1], and new employer guidance offering step‑by‑step help on health and disability [ref: a1215.4/1].

### Victims and Courts Bill – First Reading
The Bill was brought from the Commons, read a first time and ordered to be printed [ref: a1216.2/1]. There was no debate at this stage.

### Statutory Instruments and Bill Order
The House approved the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023 (Mutual Recognition Agreement) (Switzerland) Regulations 2025 [ref: a1217.1/4] and the Private International Law (Implementation of Agreements) Act 2020 (Extension of Operative Period) Regulations 2025 [ref: a1217.3/4]. The Dogs (Protection of Livestock) (Amendment) Bill’s order of commitment was discharged: "Motion agreed" [ref: a1217.6/2]. No divisions were held.

### Employment Rights Bill – Commons Amendments and Reasons (Ping‑Pong)
Peers considered multiple areas and insisted on several Lords changes after divisions. The House agreed Lord Fox’s Motion A1 (302–159) to require employers, after each reference period, to notify eligible workers of the right to a guaranteed hours offer, with an option to decline: "the employer must send the worker a written notice" [ref: a1220.1/3; a1231.1/5]. On unfair dismissal, the House insisted (Motion E1; 301–153) on retaining a qualifying period rather than a full day‑one right, with arguments that "Making unfair dismissal a day one right will inhibit hiring" [ref: a1222.0/3; a1247.3/2]. The House also agreed Motion H1 (267–153) to define seasonal work and require the Secretary of State to have regard to its characteristics in regulations [ref: a1258.4/3; a1258.5/2]. On accompaniment at disciplinary or grievance hearings, Ministers "commit to a review of the relevant legislation" [ref: a1251.5/5] and Motion G1 was withdrawn [ref: a1257.0/2]. Other outcomes included: a statutory, time‑limited review of time‑off rights for special constables (within 12 months) [ref: a1236.1/3]; acceptance of guidance for heritage railways, to be published by 31 March 2026, with regulators to "work with the Heritage Railway Association to produce written guidance" [ref: a1236.2/6‑a1236.2/7]; insisting on keeping opt‑in arrangements for union political funds (Motion L1; 249–142), with calls for "a clear and equal choice" for members—"They should be required to choose between two options" [ref: a1269.0/3; a1264.2/8]; and insisting on retaining the 50% turnout threshold for industrial action ballots (Motion M1; 240–143), despite ministers arguing the threshold "is a high bar" [ref: a1272.5/2; a1251.5/7]. Peers rejected Motion N1 (184–195) to widen local flexibility under the School Support Staff Negotiating Body; ministers stressed the framework "sets a minimum standard—the floor" [ref: a1276.2/2; a1243.4/4]. Technical changes expanded NDA protections to include failures to make reasonable adjustments [ref: a1245.4/1], and the Government declined Lords whistleblowing reforms while offering further engagement [ref: a1245.4/2]. Ministers also noted ongoing SME consultation: they had engaged "with more than 250 stakeholders... 75 were SMEs" [ref: a1245.4/3].

### Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Bill – Report (1st Day): Border Security Commander
Amendments to change the appointment basis of the Border Security Commander were withdrawn or not moved. Ministers rejected claims the role is symbolic: "This is not a gimmick" and cited delivery—"secured £150 million of funding" and staff increased "to 227" [ref: a1287.0/1‑a1287.0/2]. The Government clarified that the commander "is acting under the authority of the Home Secretary" and that the Bill "puts the post on a statutory footing" with reporting and oversight [ref: a1290.0/1; a1289.4/1]. No division was held.

### Border Security Bill: Offences on supplying/handling articles and hygiene exemptions
The House agreed government amendments expanding offences to those "concerned in the supplying of" or handling relevant articles for immigration crime, where the person knows of their use in such offences [ref: a1297.1/2; a1304.1/2]. Ministers said this targets supply chains (e.g., storage of dinghies) and preserves a knowledge threshold. The House also approved exemptions so that items "designed for use for the purposes of personal cleanliness or personal hygiene" are not caught, while excluding blades, glass and aerosols [ref: a1304.3/2‑a1304.3/3]. An amendment to exclude immediate family from liability was withdrawn [ref: a1303.3/2].

### Border Security Bill: Online advertising of unlawful immigration services
A new offence was added to criminalise creating or publishing material online that promotes unlawful immigration services, with extraterritorial effect and corporate liability. Ministers said "the purpose" is to target material that "promote[s] an unlawful immigration service" [ref: a1304.5/2]. Protections for internet service providers were included where the provider does not "select the recipient of the transmission" [ref: a1313.3/2], and the offence applies "anywhere in the world" [ref: a1315.1/4]. Agreed without division.

### Border Security Bill: Endangering another during sea crossing
An amendment to widen the new offence so that boarding any unseaworthy vessel would suffice was not accepted. Ministers argued this would capture all small‑boat passengers and is "not the specific intention of this power" [ref: a1316.4/4‑a1316.4/5]. The amendment was withdrawn; no change was made.

### Border Security Bill: Data protection and disclosure (technical)
The House agreed technical amendments removing redundant data‑protection wording, given the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, under which relevant provisions are "automatically read as being subject to data protection law" [ref: a1318.0/2‑a1318.0/3]. A proposal to disapply data protection for illegal entrants/foreign national offenders was not moved; Ministers had cautioned it would be "not only disproportionate but unnecessary" [ref: a1318.0/5].
